\begin{abstract}
Bank Indonesia's Project Garuda publicly documents wholesale central bank
digital currency. This paper reports a retail Digital Rupiah prototype on
Hyperledger Fabric 2.5 and a compliance study of its customer controls and
endorsement boundaries. The study uses four peer MSPs: CentralBankMSP,
DistributorMSP, EndUserMSP, and the Observer (MSP ID: ValidatorMSP). Three Go
chaincodes cover monetary state, customer controls, and payments. The customer
controls follow simplified customer due diligence for individual retail
customers. The ledger enforces who may transact, up to which ceiling, and until
when, while identity verification and list screening stay manual. The Observer
has read-only audit access through an Auditor role. Server-managed X.509
identities distinguish the signing actor, customer subject, and wallet custodian.

The correctness record shows 30 of 30 functional scenarios, 296 of 296 API
route-role cells, 28 of 28 validation cells, and 45 of 45 action cells. All checks pass. The prototype conforms to its specified behavior on
these checks. These checks cover only the selected scenarios. They do not establish
identity authenticity, screening effectiveness, actual PPATK reporting, or
institutional compliance.

Performance results from a one-worker Hyperledger Caliper run on a single host
are pending. Findings are limited to synthetic functional tests and single-host performance measurements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Central banks are evaluating Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) as a digital
form of sovereign money~\cite{Kiff2020A}. Bank Indonesia pursues CBDC through
Project Garuda. Its publicly documented proof of concept targets the
\emph{wholesale} tier, specifically a cash ledger for issuance and redemption
among the central bank and large financial
institutions~\cite{bi_garuda_poc,bi_whitepaper2022}. The Garuda White Paper also
describes a retail tier in which intermediary banks distribute Digital Rupiah to
households and merchants~\cite{bi_whitepaper2022}. That description remains at the policy and
architecture level, and we are not aware of a publicly documented, testable
system that implements Bank Indonesia's retail money business processes end to
end on a blockchain ledger.

Central-bank digital money involves a sequence of business processes, not a
single ledger operation. The central bank \emph{issues} wholesale Digital Rupiah
(w-Rupiah Digital) directly into the reserve of a Distributor, a payment service
provider that Bank Indonesia admits. The Distributor \emph{converts} reserve
balances into retail Digital Rupiah (r-Rupiah Digital) held as a retail float, and
funds customer wallets from that float. The Distributor is the only Custodian and
serves individual retail customers. Holders \emph{transact}, redemption retires
w-Rupiah Digital from a Distributor reserve, and an Observer institution, acting
through a read-only Auditor role,
audits the circulation. A blockchain implementation must express these
processes as deterministic transactions that each organization can validate
independently. It must also preserve two-tier intermediation to reduce bank
disintermediation~\cite{Auer2020The} and give the Observer an audit surface.

Most technical studies isolate one part of this problem: permissioned CBDC
architecture~\cite{bhawana2021}, privacy~\cite{islam2023}, security threat
models~\cite{hans2023}, or throughput in account-based
systems~\cite{Lovejoy2022A}. Deployed retail systems, including Nigeria's
eNaira~\cite{ree2023enaira}, China's e-CNY~\cite{pboc2021ecny}, and the Bahamian
Sand Dollar~\cite{centralbankbahamas2020sanddollar}, publish design papers but
not reproducible performance benchmarks. The surveyed literature therefore
provides no integrated, measurable retail reference for the Indonesian
two-tier model~\cite{sethaput2025,aryani2023critlit}.

The compliance study is the main study of this paper. It tests whether selected
customer controls and organizational endorsement boundaries behave as designed
in synthetic scenarios. It also measures the throughput and latency of the same
three chaincodes with a Hyperledger Caliper benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

To address this gap, we implement a Digital Rupiah prototype on a permissioned
Hyperledger Fabric network~\cite{androulaki2018}. The prototype maps Bank
Indonesia's business processes to chaincode transactions. This paper contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The compliance study of the prototype on a permissioned network with
	      four peer MSPs and three domain chaincodes for monetary operations,
	      customer controls, and payments. Synthetic integration scenarios
	      exercise the simplified-CDD gates, distinct compliance officer approval, restriction evidence, reserve
	      conversion and wallet funding, read-only validation, and the combined endorsement
	      requirements.
	\item A two-tier Digital Rupiah implementation with hierarchical issuance and
	      distribution, retail transfers, and read-only auditor access to subject
	      records. This extends the wholesale-only Project Garuda proof of concept with a
	      testable retail implementation.
	\item A deterministic transfer protocol with explicit eligibility, limit, and
	      balance checks, idempotent receipts keyed by business reference, Fabric
	      multi-version concurrency control (MVCC), and atomic wallet updates. The
	      protocol lets endorsing organizations apply the same state-transition
	      rules independently.
	\item A Hyperledger Caliper~\cite{hlf_caliper} benchmark of the compliance
	      study. It documents the testbed, the workload catalog, and the functional
	      checks for authorization, validation, and action outcomes. Its throughput and latency results are pending under a single-host, one-worker scope.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Two-Tier Retail CBDC}
In the two-tier (intermediated) model the central bank issues CBDC and remains
the ultimate liability holder, while regulated intermediaries serve end users and
operate customer-facing rails~\cite{Auer2020The,Kiff2020A}.
This preserves the existing division of labor and limits the central bank's
exposure to retail operations. Bank Indonesia adopts this structure for Digital
Rupiah, with intermediary Distributors between
the central bank and the public~\cite{bi_whitepaper2022}.

\subsection{Permissioned Blockchain and Hyperledger Fabric}
Hyperledger Fabric is a modular, permissioned blockchain with pluggable
ordering, channel-based data isolation, and separate execution, ordering, and
validation stages~\cite{androulaki2018}. Membership Service Providers (MSPs)
govern identity, the crash-fault-tolerant Raft protocol orders transactions,
and CouchDB can provide a queryable world state. Fabric has consequently been
used in CBDC and regulated-finance
prototypes~\cite{bhawana2021,hanif2024meetcoin}. Hyperledger
Caliper~\cite{hlf_caliper,hlf_metrics} measures throughput and latency under
different workloads.

\subsection{Authorization and Oversight}
Retail CBDC systems require explicit authorization boundaries, safe balance
updates, and read-only audit visibility. Token-based retail designs also face
denial-of-service and double-spend risks~\cite{ahuja2024limbocoin}, so transfer
validation must be deterministic. Our chaincode combines organization-aware
authorization, subject eligibility and limit checks, and read-only Observer
access to subject records. This complements studies that address privacy~\cite{islam2023}
or security~\cite{hans2023} separately. The closest published prototype is Project Aurum,
a full-stack two-tier retail CBDC built for the Hong Kong Monetary
Authority~\cite{bis2022aurum}, which pairs a wholesale interbank system with a
retail e-wallet system and, like this work, keeps the two tiers
architecturally distinct. Aurum reports no reproducible throughput benchmark,
and its source code and technical manuals are released only to BIS member
central banks rather than publicly. What distinguishes this prototype among the
representative systems in Table~\ref{tab:compare} is that its two-tier
intermediation and its authorization and oversight controls are realized in an
implementation with a throughput and latency benchmark, rather than
described in design documentation alone.

The institutional tier contains CentralBankMSP (Bank Indonesia) and the Distributor
(DistributorMSP), a bank or non-bank payment service provider. The prototype runs one
non-bank Distributor, so holding w-Rupiah Digital in a reserve is a simplification.
Bank Indonesia registers the Distributor through \texttt{RegisterDistributor} and issues
w-Rupiah Digital with \texttt{Issue}, which credits the Distributor reserve and
the wholesale supply. Bank Indonesia retires it through \texttt{RedeemReserve}. The Distributor
converts reserve balances into its retail float through \texttt{ConvertToRetail} and
funds customer wallets from that float through \texttt{FundWallet}. Individual retail
customers (EndUserMSP) then transfer value
through \texttt{Transfer}, which checks subject eligibility, limits, and balances
before the state changes. Customer subjects move through pending creation,
review, activation on approval by the reviewer, verification, closure, restriction, and unfreeze on approval by the compliance officer
(Section~\ref{sec:cdd}).

\subsection{Network Topology}
The four-organization network runs one peer per organization on one application
channel, \texttt{bi-coin} (Fig.~\ref{fig:netarch}, Table~\ref{tab:topology}). \texttt{CentralBankMSP} represents Bank
Indonesia, the issuer. It also operates the three orderers and hosts its
certificate authority \texttt{ca.centralbank}. \texttt{DistributorMSP} represents
the Distributor that holds the Distributor reserve.
\texttt{ValidatorMSP} is the Observer for the Financial Services Authority
(OJK, \emph{Otoritas Jasa Keuangan}) and the Indonesian Financial Transaction
Reports and Analysis Center (PPATK, \emph{Pusat Pelaporan dan Analisis Transaksi
Keuangan}). It has read-only audit access through an Auditor role and is not an
ordinary payment endorser of the three chaincodes. \texttt{EndUserMSP} represents
individual retail customers. Each organization operates one peer with its
own CouchDB world state and MSP, and the current network gives each organization
its own Fabric CA.
Three Raft orderers order transactions. This configuration tolerates the crash of
one consenter, which requires at least three, but all three belong to
CentralBankMSP and run on one host. The
channel's \texttt{Endorsement} and \texttt{LifecycleEndorsement} policies require
a majority of the four organizations. ValidatorMSP approves chaincode definitions
under this lifecycle policy, but every chaincode endorsement policy in
Table~\ref{tab:study-chaincodes} excludes it.

\subsection{Simplified CDD Controls}
\label{sec:cdd}
This subsection asks what a ledger can enforce about customer due diligence (CDD)
when it cannot check the evidence. We argue that the ledger enforces the decision
gates of simplified CDD (\emph{CDD sederhana}), namely who may transact, up to which
ceiling, and until when, because these gates depend only on recorded decisions.
Evidence gathering stays manual and off-chain.

\textbf{Scope and Basis.} The prototype implements CDD sederhana only, and only for
individual retail customers. Its Distributor is a non-bank payment service provider,
so the governing text is Bank Indonesia's CDD guideline for such providers, which
allows simplified CDD for a product with a limited balance or limit and a controlled
risk~\cite{bi2023pedoman}. PBI 10/2024 Pasal~31 regulates simplified CDD, including
verification after the relationship opens or when a threshold is
reached~\cite{bi2024pbi10}. PADG 15/2025 Pasal~41 keeps the guideline in force as
long as it does not conflict with the new rules~\cite{bi2025padg15}. Standard CDD and
enhanced due diligence (EDD) are out of scope.

\textbf{Who May Transact.} A subject becomes active only if the reviewer
(\texttt{kyc\_reviewer}) approves, the recorded DTTOT result is \texttt{not\_listed},
the low-risk flag is set, limits are set, and Bank Indonesia has set the ceilings
(\texttt{ApproveSubject}). DTTOT is the Indonesian list of suspected terrorists and
terrorist organizations. Otherwise the subject ends \texttt{rejected}, a terminal state. Eligibility to pay or to receive
means active, not restricted, and low risk. \texttt{CanPay}, \texttt{GetLimits}, and
\texttt{CheckPayee} share this one rule, so every endorsing peer applies the same
predicate to sender and payee.

\textbf{Up to Which Ceiling.} Bank Indonesia sets two holdings ceilings in public
ledger state through \texttt{SetCddCeilings}, Rp2,000,000 for an unverified subject
and Rp20,000,000 for a verified one. The reviewer's holdings limit may not exceed the
verified ceiling, and the effective holdings limit is the lower of that limit and the
ceiling of the subject's verification state. An unverified or downgraded subject
therefore cannot receive beyond the unverified ceiling. The two values come from the
e-money rules in PADG 32/2025 Pasal~19(1)~\cite{bi2025padg32} and serve only as
parameters, because Digital Rupiah is not e-money.

\textbf{Until When.} Simplified CDD lets verification follow the opening of the
relationship while the subject stays capped. \texttt{VerifySubject} records the
reviewer's manual confirmation with a SHA-256 evidence reference and raises the
subject to the verified ceiling. \texttt{UpdateSubject} replaces the document
reference and the DTTOT result and returns the subject to unverified, and a
\texttt{listed} result restricts it as DTTOT. \texttt{CloseSubject} ends the
relationship for missing data, a fictitious or doubtful identity, or a customer
request, and \texttt{closed} is terminal. When the compliance officer confirms a
suspicion, \texttt{RevokeLowRisk} leaves the subject active but unable to pay or
receive, which models the end of simplified CDD under suspicion.

\textbf{Manual Evidence and Monitoring.} The reviewer compares the identity document
with the applicant's data and matches the DTTOT list by hand. The ledger records
only the result and an evidence hash. A backend consumer reads
\texttt{TransferReceived} events and runs three rules whose hits are alerts for
compliance review. R1 asks for verification when an unverified balance reaches
80 percent of the unverified ceiling, R2 flags transfer velocity above a configured
count in 24 hours, and R3 flags at least three transfers within 10 percent below the
per-transaction limit in 24 hours. Confirming an alert writes a draft suspicious
transaction report (\emph{Laporan Transaksi Keuangan Mencurigakan}, LTKM) and
revokes the low-risk flag without a customer notice, to respect the anti-tipping-off
provision of the guideline~\cite{bi2023pedoman}. A closure writes the written notice
that the guideline requires.

\section{Protocol and Determinism}
\label{sec:proto}
The payments chaincode applies explicit eligibility, limit, and balance checks
during endorsement. We formalize these checks and examine their behavior under
concurrent transactions.

\subsection{Transfer-Validity Rules}
For a transfer of amount $a$ from subject $s$ to subject $r$, the payments
chaincode admits the transfer only if every one of the following checks passes:
\begin{itemize}
\item the caller is an EndUserMSP identity that can act for $s$, as evaluated by
      the controls chaincode (\texttt{CanActFor});
\item $s$ is eligible, meaning active, unrestricted, and low risk (\texttt{CanPay}), and $r$ passes the payee check (\texttt{CheckPayee}), which applies the same eligibility rule and returns only whether the transfer is permitted;
\item $a>0$, $s\neq r$, and a non-empty reference are present;
\item $a$ does not exceed the per-transaction limit of $s$, and the daily and
      monthly payment totals of $s$ are checked against its limits; and
\item $s$ has enough balance to cover $a$, and the balance of $r$ after receipt
      does not exceed its effective holdings limit, the lower of its reviewer limit and the CDD ceiling of its verification state.
\end{itemize}
If any check fails, the transfer is rejected. A successful transfer decreases the
sender balance, increases the receiver balance, and writes a receipt keyed by the
reference. A repeated call with the same reference and parameters returns the
stored receipt. A reference reused for a different operation is rejected.
Issuance and distributor registration require CentralBankMSP. Each Distributor identity
carries its distributor identifier in a \texttt{distributor\_id} certificate
attribute, and the chaincode rejects any debit of another Distributor's reserve or float.
Only a Distributor performs conversion, so only a Distributor holds a retail float.

\subsection{Determinism and Double-Spend Safety}
Every endorsing peer evaluates these checks against the same transaction arguments
and ledger state. The chaincodes do not use a peer-local clock or random values,
and usage windows are derived from the transaction timestamp. Each successful
transfer updates the sender and receiver records and writes the receipt within one
transaction, so every endorser evaluates the same balance transition before
validation.

Double-spend safety follows from Fabric's MVCC. An endorsement reads the current
version of each wallet record, and Fabric commits the transaction only if those
versions remain unchanged through ordering and validation. Two concurrent transfers
from the same wallet may endorse against the same version, but only the first
ordered transaction commits. The second fails MVCC validation after the first
update. Because the wallet records are keys in the read set, one version check
covers the balances used by the transfer and prevents a stale balance update.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{function} \textsc{Transfer}(sender, receiver, amount, reference)
\IF{caller is not EndUserMSP \textbf{or} caller cannot act for sender}
\RETURN \textsc{reject}
\ENDIF
\IF{sender is not eligible \textbf{or} receiver fails the payee check}
\RETURN \textsc{reject}
\ENDIF
\IF{amount $\le 0$ \textbf{or} sender = receiver \textbf{or} reference is missing}
\RETURN \textsc{reject}
\ENDIF
\IF{reference has a stored receipt}
\RETURN stored receipt if it matches, otherwise \textsc{reject}
\ENDIF
\IF{amount exceeds the per-transaction, daily, or monthly limit}
\RETURN \textsc{reject}
\ENDIF
\IF{sender.balance $<$ amount \textbf{or} receiver.balance + amount exceeds its effective holdings limit}
\RETURN \textsc{reject}
\ENDIF
\STATE sender.balance $\leftarrow$ sender.balance $-$ amount
\STATE receiver.balance $\leftarrow$ receiver.balance $+$ amount
\STATE \textbf{PutState} both wallets and the receipt (validated by Fabric MVCC)
\RETURN \textsc{commit}
\end{algorithmic}

chaincode. Fabric MVCC validates the wallet versions before commit.}

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}
The evaluation uses a single host with one peer per organization and no
geographic distribution or network latency. Its Caliper results therefore apply
only to the reported configuration and do not estimate national-scale
performance. Each compliance workload is confirmed with one worker over three
30-second rounds. A distributed deployment and a graded-skew contention workload
would be needed to characterize other operating conditions.

The shared Fabric channel does not provide transaction privacy. The subject record
sits in a private data collection that only three member organizations can read, and
the prototype does not use privacy-preserving cryptography. Any channel identity can
still call the payee check, which reveals whether a subject is eligible to receive and,
by probing the balance argument, how large its effective holdings limit is. The three
Raft orderers all belong to CentralBankMSP and run on one host, so ordering tolerates
the crash of one orderer but not the loss of the host or of the organization. The
prototype also omits offline payment, the RTGS giro account that backs issuance,
and any reverse path from wallet balances to a Distributor reserve. It runs one
non-bank Distributor in DistributorMSP, so a non-bank holding w-Rupiah Digital in a
reserve is a simplification, and ownership of reserves relies on the
\texttt{distributor\_id} attribute.

The compliance controls carry six limits, and each bounds what the correctness record
can show. First, identity verification is the reviewer's manual comparison, with no
check against Indonesian population data (Dukcapil), so the ledger records that a
reviewer verified an identity and not that the identity is genuine. Second, DTTOT and
proliferation-financing list matching is manual, and the prototype stores only the
reviewer's result. Third, the prototype has no signature and does not identify
beneficial owners, which fits individual retail customers. Fourth, low risk is one
product-level reason set by the reviewer, with no risk model, and standard CDD and EDD
are not implemented. A subject who is not low risk is rejected, so a customer who
needs more than the verified ceiling has no path. Fifth, LTKM drafts are not sent to
PPATK, and the prototype does not model leftover funds after a closure, their handover
to the Balai Harta Peninggalan, or the five-year purge of records. Sixth, the holdings
ceilings borrow e-money values as parameters~\cite{bi2025padg32}, and the monthly
incoming cap of the same article is not enforced.

One may argue that a ledger that cannot check a list or a population record enforces
nothing of substance. The objection holds for the quality of evidence and does not
hold for the gates. A subject that is listed, not low risk, or closed cannot pay or be
paid, and no subject can receive beyond its ceiling, whatever an off-chain system
says, because both endorsing organizations run the same eligibility and payee check.
The limit of this argument is that low risk is the reviewer's assertion, so the
system enforces a decision but cannot show that the decision is sound. Three
implementation limits follow. The unverified ceiling prompts verification, but no
deadline forces it, and rule R1 only raises an alert. A subject can read its own
low-risk flag through \texttt{GetSubject}, so a revocation after a confirmed
suspicion is visible to the customer, which weakens the rule against tipping off.
Rule R1 also rebuilds each balance from the transfer events the backend has
observed, so a wallet funded outside the backend is missed.

The authorization, validation, and action-outcome checks cover the tested paths
only. They do not establish formal security, production readiness, or
cross-machine reproducibility. Future work should repeat the protocol on
distributed hosts, instrument endorsement and commit phases, expand the
contention workload, evaluate privacy, offline payment, and interoperability, and add
standard CDD and enhanced due diligence (EDD) for customers above the verified
ceiling, beneficial-owner identification, and a Dukcapil identity check.

The compliance study is limited to synthetic chaincode integration scenarios. It
does not use live identity verification or screening feeds, submit reports to
PPATK, or establish the effectiveness of institutional compliance.
Its backend HTTP path is exercised by functional tests but has not been
load-measured. Performance results for the three chaincodes are limited to one
Caliper worker on one host with 30-second confirmation windows. The current harness
stops a search on a transport error as inconclusive, without automatic retry.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper reports a two-tier Digital Rupiah prototype on a four-organization
Hyperledger Fabric network. Its three chaincodes implement hierarchical issuance and
distribution, retail transfers, simplified-CDD gates on who may transact, up to which ceiling,
and until when, organization-aware authorization, and read-only
Observer access to subject records. The transfer protocol combines explicit
eligibility, limit, and balance checks with Fabric MVCC.

The compliance study is the main study of this paper. Its synthetic integration
scenarios passed for distinct compliance officer approval, restriction evidence, reserve funding,
retail payment, read-only ValidatorMSP access, and expected endorsement failures. Its
throughput and latency results for the same three chaincodes on a single host are
pending. This functional evidence does not depend on the Caliper results in
Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

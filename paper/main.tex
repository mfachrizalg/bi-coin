\begin{abstract}
Bank Indonesia's Project Garuda publicly documents wholesale central bank
digital currency. This paper reports a retail Digital Rupiah prototype on
Hyperledger Fabric 2.5 and a compliance study of its customer controls and
endorsement boundaries. The study uses four peer MSPs: CentralBankMSP,
DistributorMSP, EndUserMSP, and the Observer (MSP ID: ValidatorMSP). Three Go
chaincodes cover monetary state, customer controls, and payments. The Observer
has read-only audit access through an Auditor role. Server-managed X.509
identities distinguish the signing actor, customer subject, and wallet custodian.

In run-20261014-01, reported under the earlier identifiers, the correctness record
shows 20 of 20 synthetic scenarios, 207 of 207 authorization cells, 27 of 27
validation cases, and 15 of 15 action outcomes; the post-rename correctness re-run
is pending. These checks cover only the selected scenarios; they do not establish
identity authenticity, screening effectiveness, actual PPATK reporting, or
institutional compliance.

The compliance study also reports throughput and latency from a one-worker
Hyperledger Caliper run on a single host. Findings are limited to synthetic functional tests and single-host performance measurements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Central banks are evaluating Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) as a digital
form of sovereign money~\cite{Kiff2020A}. Bank Indonesia pursues CBDC through
Project Garuda. Its publicly documented proof of concept targets the
\emph{wholesale} tier, specifically a cash ledger for issuance and redemption
among the central bank and large financial
institutions~\cite{bi_garuda_poc,bi_whitepaper2022}. The Garuda White Paper also
describes a retail tier in which intermediary banks distribute Digital Rupiah to
households and merchants~\cite{bi_whitepaper2022}. That description remains at the policy and
architecture level, and we are not aware of a publicly documented, testable
system that implements Bank Indonesia's retail money business processes end to
end on a blockchain ledger.

Central-bank digital money involves a sequence of business processes, not a
single ledger operation. The central bank \emph{issues} wholesale Digital Rupiah
(w-Rupiah Digital) directly into a Distributor reserve held by a wholesale or
retail bank. A retail bank \emph{converts} reserve balances into retail Digital
Rupiah (r-Rupiah Digital) held as a retail float, and funds customer wallets from
that float. The retail bank is the only Custodian and serves customers and
merchants in the retail tier. Holders \emph{transact}, redemption retires
w-Rupiah Digital from a Distributor reserve, and an Observer institution, acting
through a read-only Auditor role,
audits the circulation. A blockchain implementation must express these
processes as deterministic transactions that each organization can validate
independently. It must also preserve two-tier intermediation to reduce bank
disintermediation~\cite{Auer2020The} and give the Observer an audit surface.

Most technical studies isolate one part of this problem: permissioned CBDC
architecture~\cite{bhawana2021}, privacy~\cite{islam2023}, security threat
models~\cite{hans2023}, or throughput in account-based
systems~\cite{Lovejoy2022A}. Deployed retail systems, including Nigeria's
eNaira~\cite{ree2023enaira}, China's e-CNY~\cite{pboc2021ecny}, and the Bahamian
Sand Dollar~\cite{centralbankbahamas2020sanddollar}, publish design papers but
not reproducible performance benchmarks. The surveyed literature therefore
provides no integrated, measurable retail reference for the Indonesian
two-tier model~\cite{sethaput2025,aryani2023critlit}.

The compliance study is the main study of this paper. It tests whether selected
customer controls and organizational endorsement boundaries behave as designed
in synthetic scenarios. It also measures the throughput and latency of the same
three chaincodes with a Hyperledger Caliper benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

To address this gap, we implement a Digital Rupiah prototype on a permissioned
Hyperledger Fabric network~\cite{androulaki2018}. The prototype maps Bank
Indonesia's business processes to chaincode transactions. This paper contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The compliance study of the prototype on a permissioned network with
	      four peer MSPs and three domain chaincodes for monetary operations,
	      customer controls, and payments. Synthetic integration scenarios
	      exercise distinct senior approval, restriction evidence, reserve
	      conversion and wallet funding, read-only validation, and the combined endorsement
	      requirements.
	\item A two-tier Digital Rupiah implementation with hierarchical issuance and
	      distribution, retail transfers, and read-only auditor access to subject
	      records. This extends the wholesale-only Project Garuda proof of concept with a
	      testable retail implementation.
	\item A deterministic transfer protocol with explicit eligibility, limit, and
	      balance checks, idempotent receipts keyed by business reference, Fabric
	      multi-version concurrency control (MVCC), and atomic wallet updates. The
	      protocol lets endorsing organizations apply the same state-transition
	      rules independently.
	\item A Hyperledger Caliper~\cite{hlf_caliper} benchmark of the compliance
	      study. It documents the testbed, the 31 workloads, and the functional
	      checks for authorization, validation, and action outcomes. It reports
	      throughput and latency under a single-host, one-worker scope.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Two-Tier Retail CBDC}
In the two-tier (intermediated) model the central bank issues CBDC and remains
the ultimate liability holder, while regulated intermediaries serve end users and
operate customer-facing rails~\cite{Auer2020The,Kiff2020A}.
This preserves the existing division of labor and limits the central bank's
exposure to retail operations. Bank Indonesia adopts this structure for Digital
Rupiah, with Distributor Banks acting as distributors between
the central bank and the public~\cite{bi_whitepaper2022}.

\subsection{Permissioned Blockchain and Hyperledger Fabric}
Hyperledger Fabric is a modular, permissioned blockchain with pluggable
ordering, channel-based data isolation, and separate execution, ordering, and
validation stages~\cite{androulaki2018}. Membership Service Providers (MSPs)
govern identity, the crash-fault-tolerant Raft protocol orders transactions,
and CouchDB can provide a queryable world state. Fabric has consequently been
used in CBDC and regulated-finance
prototypes~\cite{bhawana2021,hanif2024meetcoin}. Hyperledger
Caliper~\cite{hlf_caliper,hlf_metrics} measures throughput and latency under
different workloads.

\subsection{Authorization and Oversight}
Retail CBDC systems require explicit authorization boundaries, safe balance
updates, and read-only audit visibility. Token-based retail designs also face
denial-of-service and double-spend risks~\cite{ahuja2024limbocoin}, so transfer
validation must be deterministic. Our chaincode combines organization-aware
authorization, subject eligibility and limit checks, and read-only Observer
access to subject records. This complements studies that address privacy~\cite{islam2023}
or security~\cite{hans2023} separately. The closest published prototype is Project Aurum,
a full-stack two-tier retail CBDC built for the Hong Kong Monetary
Authority~\cite{bis2022aurum}, which pairs a wholesale interbank system with a
retail e-wallet system and, like this work, keeps the two tiers
architecturally distinct. Aurum reports no reproducible throughput benchmark,
and its source code and technical manuals are released only to BIS member
central banks rather than publicly. What distinguishes this prototype among the
representative systems in Table~\ref{tab:compare} is that its two-tier
intermediation and its authorization and oversight controls are realized in an
implementation with a measured throughput and latency baseline, rather than
described in design documentation alone.

The institutional tier contains CentralBankMSP (Bank Indonesia) and the Distributor
Banks, which are wholesale or retail banks (DistributorMSP). Bank Indonesia registers
each Distributor Bank with its kind through \texttt{RegisterDistributor} and issues
w-Rupiah Digital with \texttt{Issue}, which credits the bank's Distributor reserve and
the wholesale supply. Banks move w-Rupiah Digital between reserves through
\texttt{TransferReserve}. Bank Indonesia retires it through \texttt{RedeemReserve}. A retail bank
converts reserve balances into its retail float through \texttt{ConvertToRetail} and
funds customer wallets from that float through \texttt{FundWallet}. Customers and
merchants (EndUserMSP) then transfer value
through \texttt{Transfer}, which checks subject eligibility, limits, and balances
before the state changes. Customer subjects move through pending creation,
review, a standard or high-risk decision, restriction, and release on approval.

\subsection{Network Topology}
The four-organization network runs one peer per organization on one application
channel, \texttt{bi-coin} (Fig.~\ref{fig:netarch}, Table~\ref{tab:topology}). The
measured run used the earlier channel name \texttt{rupiah-study}, as
Section~\ref{sec:eval} discloses. \texttt{CentralBankMSP} represents Bank
Indonesia, the issuer. It also operates the single orderer and hosts its
certificate authority \texttt{ca.centralbank}. \texttt{DistributorMSP} represents
the wholesale and retail Distributor Banks that hold Distributor reserves.
\texttt{ValidatorMSP} is the Observer for the Financial Services Authority
(OJK, \emph{Otoritas Jasa Keuangan}) and the Indonesian Financial Transaction
Reports and Analysis Center (PPATK, \emph{Pusat Pelaporan dan Analisis Transaksi
Keuangan}). It has read-only audit access through an Auditor role and is not an
ordinary payment endorser of the three chaincodes. \texttt{EndUserMSP} represents
retail, merchant, and business users. Each organization operates one peer with its
own CouchDB world state and MSP, and the current network gives each organization
its own Fabric CA (Section~\ref{sec:eval} notes the CA setup of the measured run).
A single Raft orderer orders transactions. This configuration provides ordering
but not crash fault tolerance, which requires at least three consenters. The
channel's \texttt{Endorsement} and \texttt{LifecycleEndorsement} policies require
a majority of the four organizations. ValidatorMSP approves chaincode definitions
under this lifecycle policy, but every chaincode endorsement policy in
Table~\ref{tab:study-chaincodes} excludes it.

\section{Protocol and Determinism}
\label{sec:proto}
The payments chaincode applies explicit eligibility, limit, and balance checks
during endorsement. We formalize these checks and examine their behavior under
concurrent transactions.

\subsection{Transfer-Validity Rules}
For a transfer of amount $a$ from subject $s$ to subject $r$, the payments
chaincode admits the transfer only if every one of the following checks passes:
\begin{itemize}
\item the caller is an EndUserMSP identity that can act for $s$, as evaluated by
      the controls chaincode (\texttt{CanActFor});
\item $s$ and $r$ are both active and unrestricted (\texttt{CanPay});
\item $a>0$, $s\neq r$, and a non-empty reference are present;
\item $a$ does not exceed the per-transaction limit of $s$, and the daily and
      monthly payment totals of $s$ are checked against its limits; and
\item $s$ has enough balance to cover $a$, and the balance of $r$ after receipt
      does not exceed its holdings limit.
\end{itemize}
If any check fails, the transfer is rejected. A successful transfer decreases the
sender balance, increases the receiver balance, and writes a receipt keyed by the
reference. A repeated call with the same reference and parameters returns the
stored receipt. A reference reused for a different operation is rejected.
Issuance and distributor registration require CentralBankMSP. Each bank identity
carries its distributor identifier in a \texttt{distributor\_id} certificate
attribute, and the chaincode rejects any debit of another bank's reserve or float.
Conversion is limited to retail banks, so only they hold a retail float.

\subsection{Determinism and Double-Spend Safety}
Every endorsing peer evaluates these checks against the same transaction arguments
and ledger state. The chaincodes do not use a peer-local clock or random values,
and usage windows are derived from the transaction timestamp. Each successful
transfer updates the sender and receiver records and writes the receipt within one
transaction, so every endorser evaluates the same balance transition before
validation.

Double-spend safety follows from Fabric's MVCC. An endorsement reads the current
version of each wallet record, and Fabric commits the transaction only if those
versions remain unchanged through ordering and validation. Two concurrent transfers
from the same wallet may endorse against the same version, but only the first
ordered transaction commits. The second fails MVCC validation after the first
update. Because the wallet records are keys in the read set, one version check
covers the balances used by the transfer and prevents a stale balance update.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{function} \textsc{Transfer}(sender, receiver, amount, reference)
\IF{caller is not EndUserMSP \textbf{or} caller cannot act for sender}
\RETURN \textsc{reject}
\ENDIF
\IF{sender or receiver is inactive or restricted}
\RETURN \textsc{reject}
\ENDIF
\IF{amount $\le 0$ \textbf{or} sender = receiver \textbf{or} reference is missing}
\RETURN \textsc{reject}
\ENDIF
\IF{reference has a stored receipt}
\RETURN stored receipt if it matches, otherwise \textsc{reject}
\ENDIF
\IF{amount exceeds the per-transaction, daily, or monthly limit}
\RETURN \textsc{reject}
\ENDIF
\IF{sender.balance $<$ amount \textbf{or} receiver.balance + amount exceeds holdings limit}
\RETURN \textsc{reject}
\ENDIF
\STATE sender.balance $\leftarrow$ sender.balance $-$ amount
\STATE receiver.balance $\leftarrow$ receiver.balance $+$ amount
\STATE \textbf{PutState} both wallets and the receipt (validated by Fabric MVCC)
\RETURN \textsc{commit}
\end{algorithmic}

chaincode. Fabric MVCC validates the wallet versions before commit.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Results}
In run-20261014-01 under the earlier identifiers, all 31 workloads passed their
preflight checks. The correctness record of that run shows 20 of 20 scenarios, 207 of
207 authorization cells, 27 of 27 validation cases, and 15 of 15 action outcomes. The
post-rename correctness re-run is pending.

Thirty of 31 workloads are bracketed by measured probes. The fund-reserve upper
bound is unresolved because two probes failed on fetch errors before measurement. Its
nearest measured failure, 0.21875 ops/s, lies above the confirmed 0.183594 ops/s.
Confirmed write rates range from 0.183594 to 4.75 TPS across the 21 write workloads,
and 19 of 21 confirm at 0.78125 TPS or below (Table~\ref{tab:capacity}). Read
workloads confirm at 272 to 296 ops/s. Under the earlier identifiers, every read confirmation round in run-20261014-01 passed.
The two payments reads confirm at 272 ops/s, below the 296 ops/s of the controls and
monetary reads. In failing read probes, delivered rate stayed below the
offered target, so the read rates are lower bounds on read capacity.

For every write workload except fund-reserve, the first failing probe completed
transactions after its probe window and recorded no failed or timed-out
transactions. Verified throughput over the probe duration plus drain therefore fell
below 90 percent of the delivered rate. The configured two-second batch timeout is a
plausible source of this drain latency, but the run records no block contents, so
the link is not isolated.

Read p50 latency is 6 to 7 ms, and read p95 latency is 7 to 10 ms
(Table~\ref{tab:latency}). Write p50 latency ranges from 788 to 2147 ms, and write

0.5 ops/s have p50 values between 2060 and 2147 ms, close to the two-second batch
timeout. The two highest-rate writes, create-pending and open-wallet, have p50 values
of 1159 and 1161 ms.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}
The evaluation uses a single host with one peer per organization and no
geographic distribution or network latency. Its Caliper results therefore apply
only to the reported configuration and do not estimate national-scale
performance. Each compliance workload is confirmed with one worker over three
30-second rounds. A distributed deployment and a graded-skew contention workload
would be needed to characterize other operating conditions.

The shared Fabric channel does not provide transaction privacy, and the prototype
does not use private data collections or privacy-preserving cryptography. The
single orderer provides ordering but does not tolerate its own failure. The
prototype also omits offline payment, the RTGS giro account that backs issuance,
and any reverse path from wallet balances to a Distributor reserve. Wholesale and
retail banks share one DistributorMSP, so bank ownership relies on the
\texttt{distributor\_id} attribute.

The authorization, validation, and action-outcome checks cover the tested paths
only. They do not establish formal security, production readiness, or
cross-machine reproducibility. Future work should repeat the protocol on
distributed hosts, instrument endorsement and commit phases, expand the
contention workload, and evaluate privacy, offline payment, and interoperability.

The compliance study is limited to synthetic chaincode integration scenarios. It
does not use live identity verification or screening feeds, submit reports to
PPATK, or establish the effectiveness of a risk model or institutional compliance.
Its backend HTTP path is exercised by functional tests but has not been
load-measured. Performance results for the three chaincodes are limited to one
Caliper worker on one host with 30-second confirmation windows. The run was
interrupted by a host or power interruption and resumed under the same stamp from
its last checkpoint. After the restart, the network was brought up again with
existing identity material, and chaincode definitions already committed to the
channel were skipped. The two payments reads measured after the restart (272 ops/s)
are lower than the eight controls and monetary reads measured before it (296 ops/s).
The paper does not attribute this difference to the restart, and it reports no
fresh-network comparison for the payments reads. The failure classifier used in
this run counted transport errors as transaction failures; the fix was made after
the run and is not reflected in its results. The current harness stops a search on
a transport error as inconclusive, without automatic retry.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper reports a two-tier Digital Rupiah prototype on a four-organization
Hyperledger Fabric network. Its three chaincodes implement hierarchical issuance and
distribution, retail transfers, organization-aware authorization, and read-only
Observer access to subject records. The transfer protocol combines explicit
eligibility, limit, and balance checks with Fabric MVCC.

The compliance study is the main study of this paper. As reported for
run-20261014-01 under the earlier identifiers, its synthetic integration scenarios
passed for distinct senior approval, restriction evidence, reserve funding, retail
payment, read-only ValidatorMSP access, and expected endorsement failures; the
post-rename correctness re-run is pending. It also reports throughput and latency for
the same three chaincodes on a single host. This functional evidence does not depend on the Caliper results in
Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

\begin{abstract}
Bank Indonesia pursues Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) through Project
Garuda, but the project's publicly documented experiments cover only the
wholesale tier. To our knowledge, no publicly available system provides a
testable implementation of Bank Indonesia's retail money business processes
(issuance, two-tier distribution, risk-based customer onboarding, payment,
redemption, and supervision) on a single auditable blockchain ledger. This paper
presents a retail Digital Rupiah prototype deployed on a permissioned,
five-organization Hyperledger Fabric network. Its Go chaincode anchors KYC
status, risk level, due-diligence level, senior approval, expiry, and document
hashes on the ledger, while raw identity data remains in an off-chain PostgreSQL
store. The chaincode derives the BASIC, STANDARD, or MERCHANT wallet tier from
an approved KYC profile and applies five per-tier transfer and balance limits,
using the Fabric transaction timestamp so that every endorser obtains the same
result. We formalize the tier-derivation function, transfer-validity predicate,
and double-spend-safety argument, then evaluate the prototype with a
reproducible Hyperledger Caliper protocol that varies client concurrency and
exercises the business processes directly. Across transfer rounds at three
concurrency levels and four business-process workloads, the prototype committed
814 of 816 transactions, and transfer throughput saturates at
14.50$\pm$0.32~TPS regardless of client concurrency, with bounded latency (mean
$\le$0.99~s, maximum $\le$2.63~s). Three dedicated workloads test the compliance
and safety claims rather than inferring them: all 34 negative-path submissions
across seven rule classes were rejected with no enforcement gap, every numeric
cap held on both sides of its boundary, and concurrent transfers from a single
wallet were serialized, with every losing transaction rejected at multi-version
concurrency control validation. Container-level
measurements place no component near saturation, so the ceiling is not explained
by endorsement or database capacity. The prototype provides an auditable testbed
for studying blockchain-based implementation of Bank Indonesia's business
processes, not a production-ready system.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Central banks are evaluating Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) as a digital
form of sovereign money~\cite{Kiff2020A}. Bank Indonesia pursues CBDC through
Project Garuda. Its publicly documented proof of concept targets the
\emph{wholesale} tier, specifically a cash ledger for issuance and redemption
among the central bank and large financial
institutions~\cite{bi_garuda_poc,bi_whitepaper2022}. The Garuda White Paper also
describes a retail tier in which intermediary banks and payment service
providers (PJP) distribute Digital Rupiah to households and
merchants~\cite{bi_whitepaper2022}. That description remains at the policy and
architecture level, and we are not aware of an openly available, testable system
that implements Bank Indonesia's retail money business processes end to end on a
blockchain ledger.

Central-bank digital money involves a sequence of business processes, not a
single ledger operation. The central bank \emph{issues} money against reserves,
banks \emph{distribute} liquidity to PJP, and PJP \emph{onboard} customers and
merchants through risk-based Know-Your-Customer (KYC) checks. Holders then
\emph{transact} within category-specific limits, intermediaries \emph{redeem}
money to the central bank, and the supervisor \emph{reconciles} and audits its
circulation. A blockchain implementation must express these processes as
deterministic transactions that each organization can validate independently.
It must also preserve two-tier intermediation to reduce bank
disintermediation~\cite{Auer2020The} and give the supervisor an audit surface.

Most technical studies isolate one part of this problem: permissioned CBDC
architecture~\cite{bhawana2021}, privacy~\cite{islam2023}, security threat
models~\cite{hans2023}, or throughput in account-based
systems~\cite{Lovejoy2022A}. Deployed retail systems, including Nigeria's
eNaira~\cite{ree2023enaira}, China's e-CNY~\cite{pboc2021ecny}, and the Bahamian
Sand Dollar~\cite{centralbankbahamas2020sanddollar}, publish design papers but
not reproducible performance benchmarks. The surveyed literature therefore
provides no integrated, openly measurable retail reference for the Indonesian
two-tier model~\cite{sethaput2025,aryani2023critlit}.

To address this gap, we implement a Digital Rupiah prototype on a permissioned
Hyperledger Fabric network~\cite{androulaki2018}. The prototype maps Bank
Indonesia's business processes to chaincode transactions. This paper contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item A two-tier Digital Rupiah chaincode that integrates
	      hierarchical issuance and distribution, risk-based KYC with
	      deterministic wallet-tier derivation, tiered transfer limits, and
	      supervisor-facing reconciliation. This extends the wholesale-only
	      Garuda proof of concept with a testable retail implementation.
	\item A formal, deterministic, double-spend-safe transfer protocol that
	      combines a tier-limit validity predicate, Fabric multi-version
	      concurrency control (MVCC), and a transaction-timestamp clock, so that
	      limit counters and validation are identical across endorsing
	      organizations.
	\item An open, reproducible retail-CBDC benchmark with Hyperledger
	      Caliper~\cite{hlf_caliper} that documents the testbed, workload,
	      population seeding, a client-concurrency sweep, and business-process
	      profiles. The benchmark identifies the throughput saturation point,
	      bounds the resource envelope at that point with container-level
	      metrics, and pairs the contention-free load with negative-path,
	      boundary-value, and single-key adversarial workloads that test policy
	      enforcement, cap placement, and MVCC double-spend rejection as
	      measured outcomes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Two-Tier Retail CBDC}
In the two-tier (intermediated) model the central bank issues CBDC and remains
the ultimate liability holder, while regulated intermediaries onboard end users,
perform KYC, and operate customer-facing rails~\cite{Auer2020The,Kiff2020A}.
This preserves the existing division of labor and limits the central bank's
exposure to retail operations. Bank Indonesia adopts this structure for Digital
Rupiah, with banks and PJP acting as distributors between the central bank and
the public~\cite{bi_whitepaper2022}.

\subsection{Permissioned Blockchain and Hyperledger Fabric}
Hyperledger Fabric is a modular, permissioned blockchain with pluggable
ordering, channel-based data isolation, and separate execution, ordering, and
validation stages~\cite{androulaki2018}. Membership Service Providers (MSPs)
govern identity, the crash-fault-tolerant Raft protocol orders transactions,
and CouchDB can provide a queryable world state. Fabric has consequently been
used in CBDC and regulated-finance
prototypes~\cite{bhawana2021,hanif2024meetcoin}. Hyperledger
Caliper~\cite{hlf_caliper,hlf_metrics} measures throughput and latency under
different workloads.

\subsection{Compliance, Limits, and Supervision}
Retail CBDC policy calls for AML/CFT controls and holding limits to be part of
the system design. In Indonesia, these controls are subject to
anti-money-laundering regulation~\cite{bi2024pbi10}. Token-based retail designs
also face denial-of-service and double-spend risks~\cite{ahuja2024limbocoin}, so
policy enforcement must be deterministic. Our chaincode combines tiered limits,
KYC gating, and supervision events, complementing studies that address
privacy~\cite{islam2023} or security~\cite{hans2023} separately. Deployed retail
CBDCs already document tiered, KYC-linked limits: e-CNY defines wallet
categories with graduated balance and transaction ceilings~\cite{pboc2021ecny},
and eNaira applies tiered daily transaction and balance
limits~\cite{ree2023enaira}. The closest published prototype is Project Aurum,
a full-stack two-tier retail CBDC built for the Hong Kong Monetary
Authority~\cite{bis2022aurum}, which pairs a wholesale interbank system with a
retail e-wallet system and, like this work, keeps the two tiers
architecturally distinct. Aurum reports no reproducible throughput benchmark,
and its source code and technical manuals are released only to BIS member
central banks rather than publicly. What distinguishes this prototype among the
representative systems in Table~\ref{tab:compare} is that its two-tier
intermediation and KYC-and-limit policy are realized in a publicly available
implementation with a reproducible benchmark, rather than described in design
documentation alone.

The wholesale tier contains Bank Indonesia, banks, and PJP. These institutional
participants follow a lifecycle of submission, approval, freezing, unfreezing,
rejection, and offboarding. Bank Indonesia mints Digital Rupiah to a treasury
wallet. Banks request issuance and distribute liquidity to PJP; a chaincode
guard permits this validator-to-PJP flow but prevents a PJP from requesting
issuance directly. The PJP serves customers and merchants in the retail tier.
Institutional wallets have system-level limits but no retail tier.

\subsection{Network Topology}
The network runs five peer organizations on one application channel
(Fig.~\ref{fig:netarch}, Table~\ref{tab:topology}).
\emph{BankIndonesiaOrg} is the issuer; \emph{HimbaraBankOrg} (HIMBARA,
Indonesia's association of state-owned banks) and \emph{CommercialBankOrg} are
validator and distributor banks; \emph{PJPOrg} represents the payment service
provider; and \emph{OJKObserverOrg} is the supervisor (OJK, \emph{Otoritas Jasa
Keuangan}, the financial-services authority), which initiates no business
transactions but whose peer still participates in endorsement under the majority
policy. Each organization operates one
peer with a CouchDB world state and maintains its own certificate authority and
MSP for identity management. A single Raft orderer orders transactions; this
configuration provides ordering but not crash fault tolerance, which requires at
least three consenters. The
MAJORITY
endorsement policy requires at least three of the five organizations to validate
a transaction before it can commit.

The two layers enforce different things, and the split matters for how the
prototype's guarantees should be read. The chaincode enforces the monetary and
compliance invariants of Section~\ref{sec:proto}: tier derivation, transfer
limits, KYC validity, wallet freezing, system-level caps, and the
validator-to-PJP issuance guard. Every endorsing peer re-evaluates them
independently, so no single organization can commit a state change that violates
them. Caller authorization is different: the mapping from roles to permitted
functions in Table~\ref{tab:roles} is enforced only in the backend, and the
chaincode does not additionally check the submitting client's MSP identity
against the requested function. A five-organization endorsement policy therefore
protects the integrity of the rules but not the attribution of the request, and
the single backend and PostgreSQL instance are a trust and availability
concentration point. A production design would bind each organization to its own
backend and re-check the submitter's certificate attributes inside the
chaincode; Section~\ref{sec:limits} returns to this.

\section{Protocol and Determinism}
\label{sec:proto}
The chaincode applies the following compliance rules during endorsement. We
formalize these rules and examine their behavior under concurrent transactions.

\subsection{Wallet-Tier Derivation}
Let $s$ be the subject type, $d$ the due-diligence level, and $r$ the risk
level of an approved KYC profile. The wallet tier $\tau$ is a deterministic
function
\begin{equation}
	\tau(s,d,r)=
	\begin{cases}
		\textsc{basic}    & d{=}0,\ s{=}\mathrm{C},\ r{=}0 \\
		\textsc{standard} & d{\ge}1,\ s{=}\mathrm{C},\ r{\le}2 \\
		\textsc{merchant} & d{\ge}1,\ s{=}\mathrm{Mr},\ r{\le}2 \\
		\bot              & \text{otherwise,}
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:tier}
\end{equation}
where $s\in\{\mathrm{C}\text{ (customer)},\mathrm{Mr}\text{ (merchant)}\}$ is
the subject type. The due-diligence level $d\in\{0,1,2\}$ denotes
simplified, standard, or enhanced due diligence, and the risk level
$r\in\{0,1,2\}$ denotes low, medium, or high risk. Under standard or enhanced
due diligence ($d{\ge}1$), a customer maps to \textsc{standard} and a merchant
to \textsc{merchant} at any of these three risk levels; simplified due diligence
($d{=}0$) is confined to a low-risk customer, who maps to \textsc{basic}. A
high-risk subject ($r{=}2$) therefore remains tier-eligible, but only with
enhanced due diligence and senior approval, both enforced when the KYC profile
is approved. A fourth, prohibited risk classification is rejected at approval and
never reaches $\tau$; the value $\bot$ identifies any other profile that cannot
support a retail wallet. Because $\tau$ uses only the approved profile, users
cannot select their own tier.

\subsection{Transfer-Validity Rules}
Each wallet tier has five limits, listed in Table~\ref{tab:limits}: a
per-transaction cap, a daily outgoing cap, a monthly outgoing cap, a monthly
incoming cap, and a maximum balance. For a transfer of amount $a$ from a sender
to a receiver, the chaincode admits the transfer only if every one of the
following checks passes:
\begin{itemize}
	\item neither the sender's nor the receiver's wallet is frozen;
	\item the sender's KYC profile is approved and not expired;
	\item the amount does not exceed the sender's per-transaction cap;
	\item the sender's running daily and monthly outgoing totals, plus this
	      transfer, stay within the sender's daily and monthly outgoing caps;
	\item the receiver's running monthly incoming total, plus this transfer,
	      stays within the receiver's monthly incoming cap;
	\item the sender has enough balance to cover the transfer; and
	\item the resulting receiver balance does not exceed the receiver's
	      maximum-balance cap.
\end{itemize}
If any check fails, the transfer is rejected. Institutional wallets (Bank
Indonesia, banks, and PJP) skip these tier limits but are still bounded by the
system-level caps in Table~\ref{tab:syslimits}: a total supply cap on the sum of
all balances, a per-participant balance cap, and a per-transaction cap.

initialization and revisable through \texttt{SetTierLimit}, so limit policy is
itself an auditable on-chain transaction rather than a compiled-in constant. The
BASIC tier is anchored to existing Indonesian electronic-money regulation, which
caps unregistered electronic money at Rp2{,}000{,}000 and caps monthly incoming
transaction value at Rp20{,}000{,}000~\cite{bi2018pbi206}; both figures are
reproduced exactly in the BASIC row, which lets the lowest tier stand in for the
regulated no-KYC instrument. The STANDARD and MERCHANT tiers are not regulatory
values. They are scaled above the electronic-money ceilings to represent
bank-intermediated retail accounts, for which the same regulation sets no
directly comparable limit, and they are test parameters rather than a
calibration proposal: the merchant ceilings in particular sit well below the
turnover of a mid-size Indonesian retailer. Setting defensible national values
is a policy exercise outside the scope of this paper.

\subsection{Determinism and Double-Spend Safety}
Every endorsing peer evaluates the transfer rules independently. The
daily/monthly counters and KYC-expiry check therefore cannot use a peer's local
clock, because clock differences could produce different decisions. Instead,
the chaincode uses Fabric's transaction timestamp, which is identical for all
endorsers of a transaction, to select the applicable day and month windows. The
running totals are not recomputed from transaction history. Each wallet record
carries its own spent-today, spent-this-month, and received-this-month counters
together with the day and month stamps at which they were last reset; a transfer
derives the current day and month from the transaction timestamp, zeroes any
counter whose window has rolled over, and then tests the limits against the
updated values. Because every input to this evaluation is either the transaction
timestamp or the two wallet records read during endorsement, each endorser
computes the same totals and reaches the same decision.

Double-spend safety combines this deterministic check with Fabric's built-in
multi-version concurrency control (MVCC). An endorsement reads the current
version of a wallet record, and Fabric commits the transaction only if that
version remains unchanged through ordering and validation. Two concurrent
transfers from the same wallet may endorse against the same record and version.
Only the first ordered transaction commits. The second fails MVCC validation
even if it passed the balance check during endorsement. Keeping the limit
counters on the wallet record rather than in a separate index is what extends
this protection to them: balance and counters share one key, so a single version
check covers both, and no range or rich query enters the read set where a
phantom read could arise. Fig.~\ref{alg:transfer} summarizes the transfer
routine.

\begin{figure}[!t]
	\centering
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\STATE \textbf{function} \textsc{Transfer}(sender, receiver, amount)
		\STATE timestamp $\leftarrow$ \textsc{GetTxTimestamp}() \COMMENT{same value on every endorser}
		\IF{sender frozen \textbf{or} receiver frozen \textbf{or} sender's KYC not valid at timestamp}
		\RETURN \textsc{reject}
		\ENDIF
		\STATE derive day and month from timestamp; zero any sender or receiver
		counter whose window has rolled over
		\STATE dailyOut, monthlyOut $\leftarrow$ sender's stored outgoing counters
		\STATE monthlyIn $\leftarrow$ receiver's stored incoming counter
		\IF{any transfer-validity rule fails (Sec.~\ref{sec:proto})}
		\RETURN \textsc{reject}
		\ENDIF
		\STATE sender.balance $\leftarrow$ sender.balance $-$ amount; increment
		sender's daily and monthly outgoing counters
		\STATE receiver.balance $\leftarrow$ receiver.balance $+$ amount;
		increment receiver's monthly incoming counter
		\STATE append record to sender's and receiver's history; emit supervision
		event if high-risk
		\STATE \textbf{PutState} (validated by Fabric MVCC)
		\RETURN \textsc{commit}
	\end{algorithmic}
	\caption{Deterministic, double-spend-safe transfer executed on every
		endorsing peer. ``Sender's KYC not valid'' means not approved or expired;
		``transfer-validity rule'' refers to the checklist in
		Sec.~\ref{sec:proto}.}
	\label{alg:transfer}
\end{figure}

The experiment uses four workload families. The transfer workload comprises
70\% customer-to-customer transfers, 25\% customer-to-merchant transfers, and
5\% reads (Table~\ref{tab:workload}). It runs at three client-concurrency levels
(1, 2, and 4 Caliper workers), with warmup, sustained, and peak rounds at each
level, and is then repeated for five identical peak rounds so that throughput
can be reported with a confidence interval. Every round submits 40 transactions,
which at the achieved rates lasts roughly three to six seconds; rounds this short
capture the saturated steady state only briefly, which is why the peak round is
the one repeated. Intervals reported as $\pm$ are two-sided 95\% Student's
$t$ intervals over those five rounds ($n{=}5$, four degrees of freedom).
Four functionality profiles exercise
the business processes in Section~\ref{sec:bp}: KYC onboarding, monetary
operations (issuance, distribution, redemption), supervision reads, and
admin/policy.

Three further workloads test the compliance and safety claims directly. The
conformance workload submits only transactions that each violate one policy
rule, so enforcement is observed as an outcome rather than inferred from an
absence of rejections. The boundary workload then probes each numeric cap from
both sides, pairing a submission exactly at the limit with one a single rupiah
past it, which distinguishes a correctly placed cap from an off-by-one
comparison. The adversarial workload directs four concurrent workers at a single
sender wallet, forcing repeated MVCC version conflicts on one balance key. The
transfer and adversarial designs are deliberately separate: the contention-free
transfer workload measures the throughput ceiling without abort noise, while the
single-key workload measures conflict handling without confounding it with
saturation.

The conformance workload needs a metric of its own, because for it a rejection
is the desired result and Caliper scores every rejected submission as a failed
transaction. The workload therefore keeps its own counters: a submission that the
chaincode rejects is recorded as \emph{enforced}, and one that commits is
recorded as an enforcement \emph{gap} and re-raised so that it cannot pass
silently. The conformance verdict is the gap count, not Caliper's success ratio.
The round is configured for 42 transactions, of which 34 are policy-violating
submissions and the remainder are fixture-seeding calls that are expected to
succeed; we report the 34 assertions and their verdict.

During every round, Caliper's Docker resource monitor samples processor, memory,
network, and disk use once per second for all eleven containers, comprising five
peers, five CouchDB instances, and the orderer. Processor figures are reported
with Caliper's CPU normalization enabled, which divides each container's share of
system-wide CPU time by the number of logical cores. A reported value is
therefore a percentage of the whole host's eight-thread capacity, not of a single
core: 100\% would mean the container saturated the entire machine.

For each round, we report throughput and efficiency $\eta$. Throughput is the
number of successful transactions divided by wall-clock duration, while
efficiency divides throughput by the offered (requested) transaction rate. A
value near 1 indicates that the system keeps pace with the offered load. A value
well below 1 indicates saturation. All reported figures come from the Caliper
report generated against the deployed chaincode on a clean ledger.

\subsection{Results}
The performance workloads committed 814 of 816 transactions. The two failures
occurred in the four-worker peak round, where a seeded sender exhausted its
balance; this is an artifact of workload seeding rather than a system fault. The
predicate in Section~\ref{sec:proto} produced consistent endorsement decisions
throughout the run.

Table~\ref{tab:results} reports transfer scaling. Peak throughput saturates near
14.5~TPS and does not increase when client concurrency rises from one to four
workers, which excludes a client-side limit. Efficiency falls from
$\eta\!\approx\!0.75$ during warmup to $\eta\!\approx\!0.21$ at peak, placing
the saturation point below the sustained offered load. Latency remains bounded:
the mean is at most 0.99~s and the maximum is at most 2.63~s. Across five
identical peak rounds, throughput is 14.50~TPS with a 95\% confidence interval
of $\pm$0.32~TPS (standard deviation 0.26~TPS), mean latency 0.68$\pm$0.04~s,
and maximum latency 2.31$\pm$0.09~s, so the saturation point is stable across
repetitions. Efficiency is already below one in the warmup rounds, at an offered
rate near 10~TPS: these rounds start against a cold chaincode container and
empty peer caches, and at 40 transactions they are too short for that startup
cost to amortize. Fig.~\ref{fig:saturation}(a) shows the resulting curve: the
three concurrency levels trace the same trajectory, which departs from the ideal
$\eta{=}1$ line as offered load grows and then flattens.

\subsection{Policy Enforcement and Contention}
Throughput results alone cannot show that the compliance rules reject anything,
because a workload built from valid transactions can never trigger them. The
conformance workload therefore submits only violations. Across 34 assertions
spanning the seven rule classes in Table~\ref{tab:negpath}, split evenly between
two workers, every submission was rejected and the run recorded no enforcement
gap.

Rejecting an input that sits well inside the violating region does not show that
a cap is placed correctly, so a second workload probes each numeric limit from
both sides: one transfer whose value lands exactly on the limit, which must
commit, and one a single rupiah past it, which must be rejected. A rule with an
off-by-one comparison passes the first workload and fails this one. Caliper
issues submissions concurrently, so each of the eight assertions uses its own
sender and receiver, and every accumulated state, whether a partial daily spend,
a receiver pre-filled to its ceiling, or a drained sender, is established during
seeding; no assertion can therefore be rejected by an MVCC conflict rather than
by the rule under test. All eight outcomes matched
(Table~\ref{tab:boundary}). The per-transaction, daily-outgoing, and
receiver-balance caps and the sender-balance check are inclusive and placed
exactly where Section~\ref{sec:proto} specifies.

The adversarial workload then tests double-spend safety directly. Four workers
issued 200 concurrent transfers from one sender wallet, so every transaction read
the same balance version. Twelve committed and 188 were rejected at validation,
and the twelve that committed were serialized rather than applied against a
stale balance. This is the behavior argued in Section~\ref{sec:proto}:
transactions that pass the balance check during endorsement still fail MVCC
validation once an earlier transaction has advanced the version of that key. The
argument predicts the serialization, not the split between the two outcomes; how
many of the 200 commit depends on the offered rate and on endorsement latency,
so the 94\% rejection share characterizes this run rather than the protocol. The
zero conflicts recorded by the contention-free transfer workload and the 188
recorded here are consistent, because the two workloads differ only in whether
they share a sender key.

\subsection{Discussion}
These measurements characterize a single-host functional prototype, not
national capacity. Transfer throughput plateaus near 14.5~TPS and monetary
operations near 20~TPS. Both are modest for permissioned DLT and, like other
private-DLT retail prototypes, remain far below the throughput of centralized
card networks.

Throughput remains almost unchanged from one to four client workers, which
already excludes a client-side limit, and the resource measurements narrow the
alternatives further. At the highest offered load no monitored container comes
close to saturation. Expressed as a share of the whole host, mean processor use
stays at or below 0.08\% on the peers, 0.13\% on the CouchDB instances, and
0.01\% on the orderer, with per-sample peaks of 0.20\%, 0.34\%, and 0.03\%
respectively; peer memory remains flat between 123 and 127~MB, and peer disk
writes stay near one megabyte per round. No component is close to exhausting
processor, memory, or write bandwidth, so the ceiling is not a capacity limit in
any single container.

Locating it more precisely is harder than the aggregate figures suggest, and two
candidate mechanisms remain. Sustained throughput of 14.5~TPS against a
ten-message block maximum corresponds to a block cycle of roughly 0.7~s. That
cycle is consistent both with the ordering service cutting and delivering blocks
at that interval and with the serialized per-channel validation and commit path
taking that long to apply a ten-transaction block to five peers and their CouchDB
instances. Low aggregate processor use does not discriminate between them,
because a commit path bounded by write latency and by sequential validation can
throttle throughput while leaving every container mostly idle, which is the
regime other Fabric performance studies also
report~\cite{fabric_perf2023,dinh2017blockbench}.

The latency data does not settle the question either, and one apparent clue
should be discounted. Maximum latency sits between 2.12 and 2.63~s in every
round, close enough to the two-second batch timeout to invite the reading that
block cutting is timeout-driven. That reading does not survive the arithmetic:
at 14.5~TPS a ten-message block fills in about 0.7~s, so the timeout is not the
binding condition at peak load. The latency tail is better explained by queueing
in the fixed-rate round. A peak round offers its 40 transactions in roughly
0.6~s and the system drains them at 14.5~TPS over about 2.8~s, which places the
last transaction's latency near 2.2~s, as observed. Distinguishing block
formation from commit serialization therefore requires an experiment this study
did not run: a sweep of \texttt{MaxMessageCount} and \texttt{BatchTimeout}, with
block-interval timestamps read back from the ledger. We report the plateau and
its resource envelope as measured, and leave the attribution open.

Enforcement and safety rest on direct observation rather than on the absence of
counterexamples. All 34 conformance submissions were rejected with no
enforcement gap, every numeric cap held on both sides of its boundary, and the
concurrent single-key transfers were serialized, every losing transaction
failing at MVCC validation. These outcomes are what the determinism and
double-spend arguments in Section~\ref{sec:proto} require. The boundary result
is the sharper of the two enforcement checks, since an off-by-one cap would
leave the negative-path run looking identical.

Absolute TPS values should not be compared directly across platforms because
the platform, measurement tool, network size, and workload differ among
studies. Instead, the documented protocol fixes the workload mix, seeded
population, concurrency sweep, and business-process profiles so that the
saturation point can be measured again under another configuration.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}
The evaluation uses a single host with one peer per organization and no
geographic distribution or network latency. Its Caliper results therefore apply
only to the reported configuration and do not estimate national-scale
performance. The workload pair also brackets rather than samples realistic
traffic: the transfer workload holds every sender key disjoint, while the
adversarial workload drives all writers onto one key. Retail payment traffic
lies between these extremes, since popular merchants attract skewed but not
exclusive concurrency. A graded-skew workload would be needed to measure
throughput under partial contention, and the throughput figures reported here
should be read as the contention-free ceiling.

Two caveats bound what the measurements support. The conformance and boundary
workloads cover the numeric caps and the seven rule classes, but not the
temporal edges: expiry exactly at the transaction timestamp and the rollover of
a daily or monthly window are untested, and both depend on timestamp handling
rather than on an integer comparison. And while the resource envelope rules out
capacity exhaustion as the cause of the throughput plateau, it does not separate
block formation from validation and commit serialization; that attribution
awaits the parameter sweep described below.

Authorization is the prototype's clearest architectural limitation. As
Section~\ref{sec:proto} describes, the chaincode enforces the monetary and
compliance invariants at every endorser, but the mapping from caller roles to
permitted functions lives only in the backend, which does not re-present the
submitter's identity for chaincode-side checking. The five-organization
endorsement policy consequently secures what may be written, not who asked for
it, and a single backend and database concentrate trust that the network
topology otherwise distributes. Binding each organization to its own backend and
enforcing role checks from client certificate attributes inside the chaincode is
the natural next step, and it would let a supervisor verify request provenance
from the ledger alone. Related is the position of the OJK peer, which endorses
every transaction it will later audit; excluding the supervisor from the
endorsement policy while retaining full ledger visibility is the alternative
worth evaluating. The single orderer is likewise a functional convenience rather
than a design: it provides ordering without crash fault tolerance, so its loss
halts settlement until it is restarted.

The KYC process is programmatic and has no connection to a real eKYC provider.
Authorized peers can see transaction data because the ledger uses neither
private data collections nor privacy-preserving cryptography, which leaves the
tension between supervisory visibility and holder privacy unaddressed rather
than resolved. The prototype also
omits payment-queue gridlock resolution and offline, network-disconnected
payment. Offline payment would require secure devices and post-reconnection
settlement of the kind studied in Project Polaris~\cite{polaris_handbook}.
Connections to domestic infrastructure, including BI-RTGS and BI-FAST, remain
stubs. The prototype thus demonstrates internal integration but not the full
interoperability and interconnection required for Digital Rupiah.

Future evaluation should use distributed multi-host deployments, a graded-skew
contention workload, and a sweep of the block-cutting parameters combined with
block-interval and commit-phase instrumentation, which is what would separate
the two candidate explanations for the current ceiling. Further implementation
work should integrate real eKYC and identity controls, move caller authorization
into the chaincode, test privacy-preserving supervision, and add gridlock
resolution and offline payment.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper implements Bank Indonesia's digital money business processes in a
two-tier Digital Rupiah prototype on a five-organization Hyperledger Fabric
network. One chaincode integrates hierarchical issuance and distribution,
risk-based KYC with deterministic wallet-tier derivation, tiered transfer limits
based on the transaction timestamp, and supervisor-facing reconciliation.

The formal model specifies the tier-derivation function, transfer-validity
predicate, and double-spend-safety argument. Under the reproducible Hyperledger
Caliper protocol, the prototype committed 814 of 816 transactions across a
client-concurrency sweep and four business-process workloads, and transfer
throughput saturated at 14.50$\pm$0.32~TPS with bounded latency. Three further
workloads test the claims directly: all 34 negative-path submissions across
seven rule classes were rejected with no enforcement gap, every numeric cap held
on both sides of its boundary, and concurrent transfers from a single wallet
were serialized, every losing transaction failing at MVCC validation, as the
double-spend argument requires. Resource measurements
show no container near saturation at the plateau, so the ceiling is not a
capacity limit; separating block formation from commit serialization as its
cause requires a block-parameter sweep that we leave to future work.

The prototype extends the public, wholesale-only Project Garuda experiments
with an auditable and measurable retail reference. It provides a testbed for
studying blockchain-based central-bank business processes but does not establish
production readiness.

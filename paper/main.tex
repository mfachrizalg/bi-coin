\begin{abstract}
Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) is a strategic Bank Indonesia initiative
under Project Garuda, but the publicly documented Garuda experiments focus on
the wholesale tier, leaving a technical gap for a testable system that digitizes
Bank Indonesia's retail money business processes (issuance, two-tier
distribution, risk-based customer onboarding, payment, redemption, and
supervision) under one auditable blockchain ledger. This paper designs and
implements such a system: a retail Digital Rupiah prototype on a permissioned
five-organization Hyperledger Fabric network. Go chaincode stores only KYC
status, risk level, due-diligence level, senior-approval status, expiry, and
document hashes, while raw identity data remains in an off-chain PostgreSQL
store. Retail wallet tiers (BASIC, STANDARD, MERCHANT) are derived
deterministically from the approved KYC profile rather than selected by the
user, and each tier enforces per-transaction, daily-outgoing, monthly-outgoing,
monthly-incoming, and maximum-balance limits computed from the Fabric
transaction timestamp for endorsement determinism. Institutional Bank Indonesia,
bank, and payment-service-provider wallets remain outside retail tiering and use
system-level limits. We formalize the transfer-validation predicate, the
tier-derivation function, and the double-spend-safety argument, and evaluate
performance with a reproducible Hyperledger Caliper protocol that sweeps client
concurrency and exercises the business processes directly. Across transfer rounds
at three concurrency levels and four business-process workloads the prototype
processed 616 transactions with zero failures, zero MVCC read conflicts, and zero
policy or limit rejections. Transfer throughput saturates near 14.5~TPS
independent of client concurrency while latency stays bounded (mean $\le$0.81~s,
maximum $\le$2.32~s), and monetary operations reach 19.9~TPS at 0.32~s mean
latency. The result is an auditable technical basis for studying how Bank
Indonesia's business processes can be carried on blockchain-based digital money
without claiming production readiness.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Central banks worldwide are evaluating Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) as a
digital form of sovereign money~\cite{Kiff2020A}. In Indonesia, Bank Indonesia
pursues this agenda through Project Garuda, whose publicly documented proof of
concept targets the \emph{wholesale} tier: a cash ledger for issuance and
redemption among the central bank and large financial
institutions~\cite{bi_garuda_poc,bi_whitepaper2022}. The retail tier, the
distribution of Digital Rupiah to households and merchants through intermediary
banks and payment service providers (PJP), is described at the level of policy
and architecture in the Garuda White Paper~\cite{bi_whitepaper2022}, but no
openly available, testable system carries Bank Indonesia's retail money business
processes end to end on a blockchain ledger.

Digital money for a central bank is not a single ledger operation but a chain of
business processes: the central bank \emph{issues} money against reserves, banks
\emph{distribute} liquidity downstream to PJP, PJP \emph{onboard} customers and
merchants under risk-based Know-Your-Customer (KYC) checks, holders
\emph{transact} within category-specific limits, intermediaries \emph{redeem}
money back to the central bank, and the supervisor \emph{reconciles} and audits
the whole circulation. A blockchain-based realization must encode each of these
processes as deterministic, independently validated transactions while
preserving two-tier intermediation that protects banks from
disintermediation~\cite{Auer2020The} and exposing an audit surface to the
supervisor. Prior technical work tends to isolate single concerns: permissioned
CBDC architecture~\cite{bhawana2021}, privacy designs~\cite{islam2023}, security
threat models~\cite{hans2023}, or raw throughput on account-based
systems~\cite{Lovejoy2022A}. Deployed retail systems such as Nigeria's
eNaira~\cite{ree2023enaira}, China's e-CNY~\cite{pboc2021ecny}, and the Bahamian
Sand Dollar~\cite{centralbankbahamas2020sanddollar} publish design papers but
not reproducible performance benchmarks, and the surveyed literature lacks an
integrated, openly measurable retail reference for the Indonesian two-tier
model~\cite{sethaput2025,aryani2023critlit}.

This paper addresses that gap with a Digital Rupiah prototype on a permissioned
Hyperledger Fabric~\cite{androulaki2018} network that maps Bank Indonesia's
business processes onto chaincode transactions. The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item An integrated two-tier Digital Rupiah chaincode that unifies
	      hierarchical issuance and distribution, risk-based KYC with
	      deterministic wallet-tier derivation, tiered transfer limits, and
	      supervisor-facing reconciliation in a single artifact, completing the
	      retail design beyond the wholesale-only Garuda proof of concept.
	\item A formal, deterministic, double-spend-safe transfer protocol that
	      combines a tier-limit validity predicate, Fabric multi-version
	      concurrency control (MVCC), and a transaction-timestamp clock, so that
	      limit counters and validation are identical across endorsing
	      organizations.
	\item A reproducible open retail-CBDC benchmark with Hyperledger
	      Caliper~\cite{hlf_caliper} that documents workload, population
	      seeding, a client-concurrency sweep, and business-process profiles,
	      and that locates the throughput saturation point and verifies the
	      business processes under load.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Two-Tier Retail CBDC}
In the two-tier (intermediated) model the central bank issues CBDC and remains
the ultimate liability holder, while regulated intermediaries onboard end users,
perform KYC, and operate customer-facing rails~\cite{Auer2020The,Kiff2020A}.
This preserves the existing division of labor and limits the central bank's
exposure to retail operations. Bank Indonesia adopts this structure for Digital
Rupiah, with banks and PJP acting as distributors between the central bank and
the public~\cite{bi_whitepaper2022}.

\subsection{Permissioned Blockchain and Hyperledger Fabric}
Hyperledger Fabric is a permissioned, modular blockchain with pluggable
ordering, channel-based data isolation, and an execute--order--validate
transaction flow~\cite{androulaki2018}. Identity is governed by Membership
Service Providers (MSPs); ordering uses the crash-fault-tolerant Raft protocol;
and the world state can be backed by CouchDB to support rich queries. These
properties make Fabric a common substrate for CBDC and regulated-finance
prototypes~\cite{bhawana2021,hanif2024meetcoin}. Standard frameworks such as
Hyperledger Caliper~\cite{hlf_caliper,hlf_metrics} provide measurement protocols
for throughput and latency under varying load.

\subsection{Compliance, Limits, and Supervision}
Retail CBDC policy work stresses embedding AML/CFT controls and holding limits
into the design rather than bolting them on, which in Indonesia is governed by
anti-money-laundering regulation~\cite{bi2024pbi10}. Token-based retail designs
are also exposed to denial-of-service and double-spend
risks~\cite{ahuja2024limbocoin}, motivating deterministic on-chain enforcement.
This work integrates these concerns (tiered limits, KYC gating, and supervision
events) into one chaincode, complementing single-aspect studies of
privacy~\cite{islam2023} and security~\cite{hans2023}.
Table~\ref{tab:compare} positions the prototype against representative deployed
and prototype retail CBDC systems: it is the only entry that combines two-tier
intermediation, an integrated KYC-and-limit policy, and an openly reproducible
benchmark.

The wholesale tier comprises institutional participants (Bank Indonesia, banks,
and PJP) registered through a participant lifecycle (submit, approve, freeze,
unfreeze, reject, offboard). Bank Indonesia mints Digital Rupiah to a treasury
wallet; banks request issuance and distribute liquidity downstream, with a
chaincode guard that permits validator-to-PJP distribution but blocks a PJP from
requesting direct issuance. The retail tier comprises customers and merchants
served by the PJP. Institutional wallets are not retail-tiered; they are bounded
by system-level limits.

\section{System Architecture}
The architecture follows a four-layer stack (client, backend API, off-chain
store, and Fabric network) shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:sysarch}. A web client calls
a Go REST backend (Gorilla Mux) that authenticates requests with JWT and
role-based access control, persists off-chain KYC and authentication data in
PostgreSQL, and submits transactions to the ledger through the Fabric Gateway
SDK. The permissioned network itself is detailed in Fig.~\ref{fig:netarch}.

\subsection{Network Topology}
The network (Fig.~\ref{fig:netarch}, Table~\ref{tab:topology}) runs five peer
organizations on one application channel: \emph{BankIndonesiaOrg} (issuer),
\emph{HimbaraBankOrg} and \emph{CommercialBankOrg} (validator/distributor
banks), \emph{PJPOrg} (payment service provider), and \emph{OJKObserverOrg} (a
read-only supervisor). Each organization runs one peer with a CouchDB world
state and its own certificate authority; identity is governed by
per-organization MSPs; and ordering uses a single Raft orderer. The chaincode
endorsement policy requires a MAJORITY of organizations to endorse before a
transaction commits. With five organizations in the network, that means at
least three peers (a simple majority) must independently validate a
transaction before it is accepted.

\section{Protocol and Determinism}
\label{sec:proto}
This section formalizes the compliance logic carried by the chaincode and argues
its safety under concurrent endorsement.

\subsection{Wallet-Tier Derivation}
Let $s$ be the subject type, $d$ the due-diligence level, and $r$ the risk
level of an approved KYC profile. The wallet tier $\tau$ is a deterministic
function
\begin{equation}
	\tau(s,d,r)=
	\begin{cases}
		\textsc{basic}    & d{=}0,\ s{=}\mathrm{C},\ r{=}0 \\
		\textsc{standard} & d{\ge}1,\ s{=}\mathrm{C},\ r{\le}1 \\
		\textsc{merchant} & d{\ge}1,\ s{=}\mathrm{Mr},\ r{\le}1 \\
		\bot              & \text{otherwise,}
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:tier}
\end{equation}
where the subject $s\in\{\mathrm{C}\text{ (customer)},\mathrm{Mr}\text{ (merchant)}\}$,
the due-diligence level $d\in\{0,1,2\}$ encodes simplified/standard/enhanced, the
risk level $r\in\{0,1,2\}$ encodes low/medium/high, and $\bot$ denotes a profile
that cannot back a retail wallet. A high-risk
subject additionally requires enhanced due diligence and senior approval, and a
prohibited risk level is rejected. Because $\tau$ depends only on the approved
profile, the tier is derived rather than user-selected.

\subsection{Transfer-Validity Rules}
Each wallet tier has five limits, listed in Table~\ref{tab:limits}: a
per-transaction cap, a daily outgoing cap, a monthly outgoing cap, a monthly
incoming cap, and a maximum balance. For a transfer of amount $a$ from a sender
to a receiver, the chaincode admits the transfer only if every one of the
following checks passes:
\begin{itemize}
	\item neither the sender's nor the receiver's wallet is frozen;
	\item the sender's KYC profile is approved and not expired;
	\item the amount does not exceed the sender's per-transaction cap;
	\item the sender's running daily and monthly outgoing totals, plus this
	      transfer, stay within the sender's daily and monthly outgoing caps;
	\item the receiver's running monthly incoming total, plus this transfer,
	      stays within the receiver's monthly incoming cap;
	\item the sender has enough balance to cover the transfer; and
	\item the resulting receiver balance does not exceed the receiver's
	      maximum-balance cap.
\end{itemize}
If any check fails, the transfer is rejected. Institutional wallets (Bank
Indonesia, banks, and PJP) skip these tier limits but are still bounded by the
system-level caps in Table~\ref{tab:syslimits}: a total supply cap on the sum of
all balances, a per-participant balance cap, and a per-transaction cap.

\subsection{Determinism and Double-Spend Safety}
Because every endorsing peer evaluates the rules above independently, the
daily/monthly counters and the KYC-expiry check must not depend on each peer's
own wall clock, or different peers could reach different answers. To avoid
this, the chaincode reads Fabric's transaction timestamp, which is identical
for every endorser of a given transaction, and uses it to decide which day or
month window a transfer falls into. Each wallet's daily and monthly outgoing
and incoming totals are simply the sum of its prior transfers that fall inside
that window, so every endorser computes exactly the same totals and reaches the
same accept-or-reject decision.

Double spending is prevented by combining this deterministic check with
Fabric's built-in multi-version concurrency control (MVCC): every endorsement
reads the current version number of a wallet's balance, and Fabric only commits
a transaction if that version number has not changed by the time the
transaction reaches the ordering and validation stage. If two transfers try to
spend from the same wallet at the same time, both endorse against the same
starting balance and version, but only the first one to be ordered commits; the
second fails MVCC validation and is rejected, even though it independently
passed the balance check. Algorithm~1 states the transfer routine.

\begin{figure}[!t]
	\centering
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\STATE \textbf{function} \textsc{Transfer}($u,v,a$)
		\STATE $\zeta \leftarrow \textsc{GetTxTimestamp}()$
		\IF{$\text{frozen}(u) \vee \text{frozen}(v) \vee \neg\,\text{kycOK}(u,\zeta)$}
		\RETURN \textsc{reject}
		\ENDIF
		\STATE compute $o_d(u),o_m(u),i_m(v)$ from history using $\zeta$ \COMMENT{daily/monthly totals, see Sec.~\ref{sec:proto}}
		\IF{$\neg\,\textsc{Valid}(u,v,a)$}
		\RETURN \textsc{reject}
		\ENDIF
		\STATE $b(u)\leftarrow b(u)-a$;\; $b(v)\leftarrow b(v)+a$
		\STATE append record to $H(u),H(v)$;\; emit supervision event if high-risk
		\STATE \textbf{PutState} (validated by Fabric MVCC)
		\RETURN \textsc{commit}
	\end{algorithmic}
	\caption{Deterministic, double-spend-safe transfer executed on every
		endorsing peer.}
	\label{alg:transfer}
\end{figure}

\section{Performance Evaluation}

\subsection{Methodology}
Performance is measured with Hyperledger Caliper on Fabric~2.5.16, consistent
with blockchain performance-metric guidance~\cite{hlf_metrics}. The ledger is
seeded with the population of Table~\ref{tab:population}: 200 retail customers
(20\% BASIC, 80\% STANDARD) and 50 merchants, all funded within their tier caps
and holding approved KYC anchors before measurement. Two workload families are
run. The first is a transfer workload (70\% customer-to-customer, 25\%
customer-to-merchant, 5\% reads; Table~\ref{tab:workload}) executed at three
client-concurrency levels (1, 2, and 4 Caliper workers), each with warmup,
sustained, and peak rounds, to probe scaling. The second is a set of four
functionality profiles that exercise the business processes of
Section~\ref{sec:bp}: KYC onboarding, monetary operations (issuance,
distribution, redemption), supervision reads, and admin/policy. For each round
we report two numbers: throughput, the number of successful transactions
divided by the round's wall-clock duration, and efficiency $\eta$, the
throughput divided by the offered (requested) transaction rate. An efficiency
near 1 means the system keeps up with the offered load; an efficiency well
below 1 means the system is saturated and falling behind. Reported figures
derive from the Caliper report generated against the deployed chaincode on a
clean ledger.

than national capacity. Transfer throughput plateaus near 14.5~TPS and monetary
operations near 20~TPS; both sit below the order-of-magnitude wholesale traffic
threshold (around 30~TPS) reported for permissioned DLT, consistent with
private-DLT retail prototypes that operate far below centralized card networks.
That throughput does not rise from one to four client workers isolates the
bottleneck to the server side, so future scaling work should target endorsement
parallelism, block-cutting parameters, and CouchDB indexing rather than client
concurrency. The zero MVCC conflicts and zero limit rejections across 616
transactions provide empirical evidence that the determinism and double-spend
arguments of Section~\ref{sec:proto} hold under load. Cross-platform throughput
comparisons are not directly meaningful: differences in platform, measurement
tool, network size, and workload scope make absolute TPS values incomparable
across studies. The value here is a documented, reproducible protocol (fixed
workload mix, seeded population, concurrency sweep, and business-process
profiles) under which the saturation point can be re-measured.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
The evaluation runs on a single host with one peer per organization and no real
geographic distribution or network latency, so the Caliper figures apply to the
reported configuration and do not generalize to national scale. The KYC process
is simulated programmatically rather than integrated with a real eKYC provider.
The ledger offers no transaction privacy beyond authorized-peer visibility; no
private data collections or privacy-preserving cryptography are used. The design
implements neither a payment-queue gridlock-resolution mechanism nor offline,
network-disconnected payment, the latter requiring secure devices and
post-reconnection settlement as studied in Project
Polaris~\cite{polaris_handbook}. External integration with domestic financial
infrastructure (for example BI-RTGS and BI-FAST) is stubbed rather than
implemented, so the prototype satisfies internal integration but not the full
interoperability and interconnection mandated for Digital Rupiah. Future work
should re-run the benchmark across distributed multi-host topologies, integrate
real eKYC and identity controls, evaluate privacy-preserving supervision, and
extend the rails toward gridlock resolution and offline payment.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented a blockchain-based realization of Bank Indonesia's digital
money business processes: a two-tier Digital Rupiah prototype on a
five-organization Hyperledger Fabric network that integrates hierarchical
issuance and distribution, risk-based KYC with deterministic wallet-tier
derivation, tiered transfer limits enforced with a transaction-timestamp clock,
and supervisor-facing reconciliation in a single chaincode. We formalized the
tier-derivation function, the transfer-validity predicate, and the
double-spend-safety argument, and a reproducible Hyperledger Caliper protocol
processed 616 transactions with zero failures, zero MVCC conflicts, and zero
policy or limit rejections across a client-concurrency sweep and four
business-process workloads, with transfer throughput saturating near 14.5~TPS at
bounded latency. By closing the wholesale-only gap left by the public Project Garuda
experiments with an auditable, openly measurable retail reference, the prototype
offers a technical basis for studying how central-bank business processes can be
carried on blockchain-based digital money without claiming production readiness.

ABSTRACT
Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) mark a significant evolution in digital finance. This
study examines the factors influencing CBDC adoption, highlighting the roles of regulatory
quality and digital financial access. Results indicate that regulatory frameworks, rather than
the level of digital infrastructure, predominantly drive CBDC initiatives. The findings caution
against the rapid deployment of CBDCs in less prepared environments, which could pose risks
to financial stability. The research underscores the importance of comprehensive regulatory
strategies over technological readiness for successful CBDC implementation. Developed markets
are advised to consider policies that mitigate risks associated with the transition towards digital
currencies.

1. Introduction
FinTech has emerged as arguably the most important change and potential regulatory challenge for the global economy. FinTech
can be used to further many societal goals, including sustainable development, improving access to finance while reducing the cost
of access, and simultaneously increasing access speed primarily through disintermediation. FinTech further mitigates information
asymmetries between different parties of financial transactions. In the following research, we set out to establish whether sovereign
characteristics, such as internet access,1 access to online financial services and sovereign regulatory quality provide explanatory
power when considering an international financial arms race that has taken place to develop and implement Central Bank Digital
Currencies (CBDCs), a form of cryptocurrency that has been and is being researched by at least 138 countries.2 Recent studies provide
a foundation for understanding how country-specific factors influence the adoption and implementation of Central Bank Digital
Currencies (CBDCs). For instance, Náñez Alonso et al. (2021) identify geographic and economic traits that predispose certain regions

Similarly, Alonso et al. (2020) emphasises the significance of regulatory quality and financial inclusion as determinants for adopting
CBDCs, illustrating that countries with robust regulatory frameworks and high levels of digital financial access are better positioned
to implement and manage CBDCs effectively. These insights underline the critical influence of a country’s financial infrastructure
and regulatory environment on its capacity to adopt new financial technologies, suggesting that for CBDCs to be successfully
implemented, a harmonisation of technological readiness and regulatory frameworks is essential. This literature collectively informs
the hypothesis that nations with advanced digital infrastructure and stringent regulatory standards are more likely to develop and
sustain CBDC initiatives, providing a nuanced understanding of the global financial landscape’s evolution towards digital currencies.
There are two primary reasons for the widespread global interest in CBDCs. First, CBDCs potentially enable countries to
implement more seamless monetary policy, a particularly useful characteristic when responding to financial crises. Secondly,
with greater traceability, CBDCs could more effectively deter criminal activity and broad financial malpractice. However, such
implementation is not without substantial risk. Substantial volatility and regulatory challenges persist through opportunistic fraud
inherent within private digital currencies (Foley et al., 2019; Allen et al., 2021; Conlon et al., 2023). These issues have led many
central banks to take further time to contemplate the future use of CBDCs. While this global interest is apparent, the conditions
under which CBDC could be successfully implemented in different countries worldwide are not. We must consider whether a
country’s regulatory quality is influential in fostering CBDC research and development or whether a lack of online financial access
and technological infrastructure explains a significant movement by emerging economies to rapidly proceed with respective CBDC
projects into later stages, such as proof of concept phases and the initiation of pilot programs.3
The introduction of CBDCs presents both opportunities and challenges for modern financial systems. This study hypothesises
that while CBDCs can potentially streamline monetary policy implementation, thereby enhancing the responsiveness and precision of
financial regulation, they may also introduce significant risks. Specifically, the digital nature of CBDCs could compromise privacy due
to the traceability of transactions, eroding the anonymity that cash transactions provide. Moreover, integrating CBDCs into existing
financial systems could make monetary policy and financial stability more susceptible to rapid shifts in public sentiment, amplified
by social media and other digital platforms. This sensitivity arises from the immediate and visible nature of digital transactions
and the rapid dissemination of information online, which could lead to quicker and potentially more volatile market reactions than
traditional currencies. More specifically, this study separates itself from existing literature by focusing on the interaction between a
nation’s digital infrastructure and regulatory frameworks in the context of CBDC development, contrasting with previous research,
which predominantly examines economic or technological factors in isolation.4 Ultimately, our findings present three core outcomes:
First, regulatory quality emerges as a crucial determinant in the initiation and progression of CBDC projects, validating its pivotal
role across all stages of development. Second, contrary to initial expectations, enhanced online financial access does not necessarily
accelerate CBDC adoption, indicating that mere digital readiness may not suffice without robust regulatory frameworks. Lastly, the
study exposes the risks associated with the precipitate deployment of CBDCs in environments with inadequate infrastructural and
regulatory preparedness, suggesting that such actions could destabilise rather than strengthen financial systems.
While CBDC technology appears promising, it is not without concerns and potential drawbacks. Specifically, CBDCs could erode
the ability to conduct private transactions while threatening domestic disintermediation by cross-border transactions completed with
foreign CBDCs (Corbet and Larkin, 2023). And in turn, there could be uncertain and unforeseen international regulatory, legal and
system-based repercussions. Geopolitical shocks make international coordination with a CBDC increasingly opaque, while national
adoption may crowd out the private sector competition, potentially hindering the development of a more robust system. Moreover,
how this technology will integrate with the traditional banking system remains unclear. Attempts to short-cut years of financial
development should not be traded with financial instability, particularly in already economically exposed regions. A specific set
of concerns includes cybersecurity-related vulnerabilities, where such breaches are particularly concerning when considering the
target would be a sovereign nation’s monetary system. Another channel through which CBDC can be negatively exposed is market
sentiment, exacerbated by the prevalence of social media technology. Examples of such coordinated attacks have been provided in
research based on /wallstreetbets and the decline of TerraLuna, where coordinated action was found to have possessed significant
market influence (Conlon et al., 2022; Corbet et al., 2022). Further, during severe geopolitical instability, CBDC could become a
significant weak point through which a coordinated digital attack could compromise economic stability.

We utilise a panel regression methodology to analyse the evolution of CBDCs across eighty-five countries from January 2015 to
June 2023, generating a dataset that includes 680 annual observations. The dependent variable, representing the development stage
of CBDC projects, is coded within four possible categories used to create a dummy variable, taking a value of unity if falling into one
of the following phases of CBDC development: research, proof of concept, pilot, and cancellation. If not categorised into one of these
groups, each respective value is represented as zero. This approach allows us to track each project’s annual progress or cessation
within individual countries, capturing the dynamic progression of CBDC initiatives worldwide. We examine the influence of annually
measured explanatory variables such as internet access, digital financial inclusion, and regulatory quality on these stages of CBDC
development. Our regression model is detailed to reflect the nuances of these variables over time, providing a robust analysis of the
factors that drive or hinder the advancement of CBDCs across different national contexts.
3. Results
The cumulative number of CBDC projects, as separated by time-varying status, is presented in Fig. 1. Multiple periods of sharp
growth in research relating to CBDC can be observed, coinciding with European and Caribbean announcements incorporating
multiple countries. Many have pointed to the need for a holistic approach for successful development and implementation, noting
that international collaboration rather than a race would be a more prudent approach (Barontini and Holden, 2019). Interestingly,
some cancellations and delays resulted from changing regulatory stances or hesitation surrounding the legal status of such digital
currency. In contrast, CBDC would be deemed illegal under governance structures in countries such as Morocco. In contrast, other
central banks, such as the Reserve Bank of India, signalled strong opposition due to the potential threat to monetary autonomy.
In Fig. 2, we observe the proportion of bank account access available digitally by country. Countries with less developed banking
sectors, specifically digital access to financial institution accounts, appear to be predominantly linked with advanced stages of
CBDC project development. Should CBDC be successfully implemented, it is possible that countries lagging in terms of financial
development could short-cut years of financial development and attempt to catch up with developed financial systems rapidly using
CBDC. However, such a decision would simultaneously expose such emerging economies to all of the unknown risks and implicit
threats associated with CBDCs, which could potentially be far more potent to developing nations than more developed nations.
Countries with lower online financial access expect lower marginal costs and higher potential marginal benefits when adopting
CBDC.

In our analysis, the role of regulatory quality is underscored as a critical driver in fostering the adoption and development of
CBDCs, supporting findings from Auer et al. (2020), which highlight the importance of robust regulatory frameworks for enhancing
monetary policy effectiveness through CBDCs. While Elsayed and Nasir (2022) explores the nuanced implications of CBDCs on
monetary policy and financial stability, our results similarly emphasise the risks of premature CBDC deployment in environments
with underdeveloped digital infrastructure. However, contrary to the expected strong role of digital financial access, our findings
suggest that its influence is less direct in accelerating CBDC projects than previously assumed. Furthermore, the study by Sandhu
et al. (2023) on India’s CBDC initiative underscores the importance of trust and regulatory transparency, reinforcing our findings
that the effectiveness of CBDC implementation is largely shaped by the strength and clarity of regulatory frameworks rather than
solely by technological readiness. This collective body of work highlights that successful CBDC implementation is intricately linked
to comprehensive regulatory strategies aligned with the nation’s broader economic policies and conditions.
4. Concluding comments
While CBDCs offer the potential to enhance the efficiency, stability, and safety of financial systems and bolster central bank
control over monetary policy, our empirical findings suggest a more complex interplay of factors influencing CBDC adoption.
Contrary to preliminary assumptions, our analysis reveals that higher online financial access does not directly correlate with
accelerated CBDC development. Instead, regulatory quality emerges as a critical driver, facilitating the progression from research to
more advanced stages of development. This insight underscores the importance of robust regulatory frameworks over mere digital
access in determining the pace and success of CBDC initiatives. Nations with limited online financial access do not necessarily
rush their CBDC projects; rather, the pace of development appears to be more closely aligned with the strength of regulatory
environments. Thus, while the urgency to ’catch up’ in digital finance is observable, it is not synonymous with hasty or illconsidered deployments. Policymakers and regulators should note that careful planning and comprehensive regulatory frameworks
are paramount to mitigate potential vulnerabilities emerging from rapid advancements in CBDC technologies.

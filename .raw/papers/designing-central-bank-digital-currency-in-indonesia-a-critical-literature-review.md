ABSTRACT
Central bank digital currency (CBDC) has emerged as a potential solution to some of the
challenges faced by traditional payment systems. This paper aims to assess whether the
proposed design of r-Digital Rupiah is suitable for Indonesia by conducting a comparative
critical literature review of the Project Garuda: Navigating the Architecture of Digital Rupiah
(2022) white paper by Bank Indonesia. In doing so, it will examine, analyze, and synthesize
the existing literature on CBDCs to extract key information pertaining to the design
frameworks of Indonesia's CBDC. A comparative analysis is also conducted to explore
similarities, differences, and potential implications by comparing the designs of the retail
CBDC of Indonesia with those implemented in other countries. The findings from this study
contributes to the understanding of the CBDC's design, identify potential strengths and
weaknesses, and provide recommendations for optimizing its design to ensure its
effectiveness, compliance, and compatibility with international standards and practices. This
critique argues that further research and analysis are necessary to fully understand the
potential implications of Digital Rupiah. Overall, the critique highlights the importance of
designing CBDCs that are not only suitable for a specific country's context but also take into
account the potential impact on the broader financial system.
Keywords: Central Bank Digital Currency, Project Garuda, Digital Rupiah, r-CBDC

INTRODUCTION
In recent years, central bank digital currency (CBDC) has emerged as a potential solution to
some of the challenges facing traditional payment systems. The current trajectory of
decreasing cash usage, driven by the increasing popularity of digital payments, presents a
significant challenge for central banks in fulfilling their crucial roles of preserving financial
and monetary stability and ensuring a secure, efficient, and inclusive payment system.
Undeniably, digital payments, run by tech and digital companies, are reshaping the banking
sector. The evolving landscape makes it increasingly complex for central banks to carry out
their responsibilities effectively.
As the payment system becomes more decentralized, central banks face difficulties in
implementing monetary policies, which raises concerns about the potential loss of control
over the overall financial system. Complicating matters further is the rapid advancement of
technology, which often outpaces regulatory efforts. Central banks must exert substantial
action to keep pace with the rapid expansion of digital payments and bridge the gap between
technological developments and regulatory frameworks. CBDC, a new form of digital money
issued and backed by central banks, is seen as a potential solution because it offers numerous
benefits over existing payment systems, such as faster and cheaper payments, better privacy
and security, reduced operational costs, and improved access to financial services for the
unbanked.
At its core, CBDC is a digital currency that utilizes blockchain technology. The primary
objective of CBDC is to provide a secure and efficient medium of exchange, allowing central
banks to influence the financial system directly. CBDC aims to enhance financial inclusion
by addressing the needs of underserved populations, including those in rural areas who may
have limited access to traditional banking services. Additionally, CBDC offers the potential
to improve payment systems, reducing transaction costs and increasing the speed of
transactions.
By implementing CBDC, central banks can regain control over the financial system, ensuring
monetary stability and effective policy implementation. This digital currency also allows
central banks to leverage technological advancements to modernize their operations and
foster innovation in the financial sector. The concept of CBDC represents a transformative

1.1.1. Background
The evolution of payment throughout human history has resulted in many different payment
methods. In ancient times, dating back to 6000 BC, humans used a barter system to exchange
goods and services to fulfill their needs, as a medium of exchange is yet to be invented
(Svizzero and Tisdell, 2019). Bartering involves swapping one good or service for another,
based on mutually agreed-upon values. However, as societies grew more complex and trade
expanded, the barter system emerged as a more organized method of exchange.
The next significant milestone in payment system evolution occurred with the invention of
money. Around the 7th century BCE in Lydia (modern-day Turkey), the first coins made of
precious metals such as gold and silver were introduced (Geva, 1987). These standardized
coins carried inherent value and facilitated trade by providing a universally recognized
medium of exchange. Over time, coins became the dominant form of currency in various
civilizations and played a crucial role in promoting commerce and economic development.
The concept of paper money, also known as banknotes, emerged in China during the Tang
Dynasty (618-907 CE), according to DK (2022). These early banknotes were issued by
merchants or government authorities and represented a promise to pay the bearer a specified
amount of precious metal, typically silver or gold. Paper money gradually gained acceptance
as a convenient alternative to carrying heavy coins, and its usage spread worldwide.

The next significant leap in payment systems occurred with the rise of banking institutions in
medieval Europe. Merchants and individuals began depositing their coins with goldsmiths,
who provided receipts acknowledging the deposited amounts. These receipts could then be
used for transactions, effectively serving as an early form of paper money. In our modern
world, paper money has played a pivotal role as our primary mode of payment, serving as the
backbone of our monetary system. As the widely accepted and official medium of exchange,
it has facilitated countless transactions and economic activities. In fact, paper money stood as
the sole mode of payment for a considerable period before the advent of digital and electronic
alternatives.
One of the significant advantages of paper money is its convenience. Its physical form allows
for easy portability, making it accessible for everyday transactions, whether small or large.
Furthermore, paper money provides anonymity, allowing individuals to conduct transactions
without revealing personal details or financial information. This aspect has been particularly
valued by those prioritizing privacy and discretion in their financial dealings. Additionally,
paper money has been widely accepted by merchants and businesses, offering a universally
recognized and trusted means of payment. Its tangibility and familiarity have contributed to a
sense of reliability and acceptance within society.
However, the usage of cash has seen a decline in recent years due to various factors. One
significant reason is the high cost associated with issuing and storing physical currency. The
expenses incurred in printing, distributing, and managing cash have become a burden for
many countries. An estimation of the private costs associated with cash usage in Uruguay
amounted to approximately 0.6 percent of the country’s GDP, according to Alvez et al.
(2019). A comprehensive examination of relevant literature revealed a range of private costs,
varying from 0.2 percent (Norway) to 0.6 percent (Belgium).
Similarly, Kosse et al. (2017) arrived at comparable figures for cash usage in Canada, with
costs equating to 0.5 percent of GDP. However, Banka (2018) reported significantly higher
costs for Albania (1.0 percent) and Guyana (2.5 percent). It is worth noting that these costs
primarily affect banks, firms, and households​. This financial strain has prompted
governments and financial institutions to explore alternative payment methods that are more
cost-effective and efficient.

3.1. Critical review of Central Bank Digital Currency: Overview of CBDC
The history of CBDC can be traced back to 1987 when Nobel laureate James Tobin proposed
the idea of a retail central bank digital currency that could manage the heavy reliance on
payment system deposit insurance. Tobin argued that a CBDC would provide a more stable
and secure form of money and would help to reduce systemic risk in the financial system. In
the years since Tobin's proposal, there has been increasing interest in CBDCs from central
banks worldwide. This interest has been driven by a number of factors, including the rise of
digital payments, the increasing use of cryptocurrencies, and concerns about financial
stability.
There are various definitions associated with the concept of Central Bank Digital Currency
(CBDC). The International Monetary Fund (IMF) defines CBDCs as a central bank issued
and regulated digital representations of physical cash. On the other hand, the Bank for
International Settlements (BIS) provides a broader definition, describing CBDC as a new
form of central bank money that serves as both a medium of exchange and a store of value.
According to the BIS, CBDC is denominated in an existing unit of account and represents a
central bank liability. The Federal Reserve, in its definition, considers CBDC as a digital
liability of the central bank, widely accessible to the general public.
In terms of the consensus among researchers, Ozili (2022) conducted a systematic literature
review and found a general consensus that CBDCs are liabilities of the central bank and have
cash-like attributes. It is important to note that there is no universally agreed-upon definition
of CBDC. However, all of the definitions mentioned above highlight key characteristics of
CBDC, such as being issued by a central bank, existing in a digital or electronic form, and
being available to the general public.
Despite the term "digital currency" being included in the definition, CBDC differs
fundamentally from digital currency. CBDC, as a form of Central Bank Money (CeBM),
represents a liability of the central bank and is denominated in the local unit of account. It
functions as both a medium of exchange and a store of value and holds the status of legal
tender (CPMI, 2018).

3.1.1. CBDC Design Features
CBDC is generally divided into two models, which are wholesale and general-purpose.
Wholesale CBDC (w-CBDC) is designed for limited use in wholesale payments, such as
interbank transactions and cross-border settlements, and is accessible only to specific firms.
On the other hand, general-purpose CBDC is universally accessible to all individuals and
enables retail payments, hence referred to as retail CBDC (r-CBDC).
Various conceptual design choices, such as distributed ledger technology (DLT) or non-DLT,
account-based or token-based systems, and interest-bearing or non-interest bearing, further
contribute to the diversity within the general-purpose CBDC category (BIS and CEMLA,
2020). This vast range of design options and the extent of parties participating in r-CBDCs
make its implementation more complex compared to w-CBDCs, as Ahiabenu (2022) noted
that w-CBDC has a more controlled environment for experimentation thanks to the limited
numbers of stakeholders involved. Thus, due to its complex design choices, more research
and debates are encircling the implementation of r-CBDC.
The first design consideration is the choice between using DLT or non-DLT platform. This
choice determines whether the CBDC uses a centralized or decentralized settlement method
and the level of control and accessibility associated with the CBDC (Mahardika et al., 2023).
DLT (distributed ledger technology), such as blockchain, are characterized by their
decentralized nature and immutability.
A CBDC based on DLT will employ a duplicated and distributed ledger among multiple
participants. The verification process for this ledger can be assigned to either a specific group
of users with permission (referred to as "permissioned") or to all participants in the network
("permissionless"). Additionally, access to this ledger can be either open to anyone ("public")
or limited to a specific group of chosen participants ("private").
In contrast, a CBDC, which utilizes non-DLT technology, operates through a centralized
system. This implies that a single institution, typically the central bank, is responsible for
conducting clearing and settlement functions. Unlike decentralized CBDCs that leverage DLT
for distributed verification and transaction validation, centralized CBDCs rely on a
centralized authority to manage these crucial aspects. Consequently, the central bank
exercises direct control over the issuance, distribution, and monitoring of the CBDC,
ensuring efficient and regulated operations within the system.

It is important to note that DLT-based and centralized CBDCs have strengths and weaknesses.
DLT-based CBDCs offer solutions to ledger tampering and transaction reversibility issues but
may not adequately address personal information security concerns (Wenker, 2022). In
decentralized networks, the risk of hackers targeting centralized trusted networks or eWallets
remains, potentially leading to the theft of users' private keys and subsequent asset loss.
Additionally, decentralized systems may be susceptible to fraudulent transactions such as
double-spending attacks (Lee et al., 2021). Moreover, scalability and energy consumption
pose challenges for DLT-based systems, as they require substantial energy resources and may
not be environmentally sustainable (Office of Science and Technology Policy, 2022).
On the other hand, centralized platforms typically offer faster transaction processing. For
instance, the VISA network has the capacity to handle up to 65,000 transactions per second
(TPS) (SARB, 2018). Meanwhile, private DLT platforms are comparatively slower,
averaging around 20 TPS, although rapid technological advancements are expected to
improve transaction speeds, with new entrants achieving up to 10,000 TPS (Mearian, 2019).
Despite the advantage of faster transactions, implementing a centralized CBDC requires
significant efforts from the central bank to oversee and control the entire system. This could
strain the central bank's resources and hinder its ability to fulfill other responsibilities.
Balancing these factors is crucial for developing an efficient and secure CBDC system that
meets users' requirements and the central bank's objectives.
The design choice between token-based and account-based CBDCs relates to the privacy and
anonymity considerations of the digital currency. According to World Bank (2021),
account-based CBDCs are subject to existing customer due diligence requirements, similar to
other account-based payment services like mobile money and payment cards.
Initially, account-based CBDCs may lack anonymity. However, since CBDC is a liability of
the central bank, regardless of the distribution model, the central bank needs access to all
account-holder information and transaction data. This implies that unlike in the current
context, where each PSP sees only a subset of transactions, in the CBDC context, the central
bank can see all user transactions. Even for token-based designs, end-users may need to rely
on PSPs to safeguard and utilize tokens, requiring the same customer due diligence processes
as account-based solutions.

of CBDC should minimize the risk of individuals losing ownership for any reasons.

anonymity, particularly for smaller transactions, can address privacy concerns while

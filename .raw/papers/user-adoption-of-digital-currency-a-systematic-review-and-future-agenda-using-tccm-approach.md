Scholarly investigations into blockchain-based currencies are progressively acknowledging the transformative
capacity of Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) in addressing the issues related to digital payments. However,
this emerging field of study is presently dispersed and fragmented from the user’s behavioral perspective. This
paper extends the existing literature by establishing an in-depth conceptual boundary using a standard review
protocol. It comprehensively analyses the 78 papers published till December 2023 to identify key research gaps.
It identifies the primary contextual dimension, bibliometric information, and key clusters of concepts exhibited
in the existing literature using inductive analysis techniques, Excel, R-Studio, and Vosviewer software.
Furthermore, this paper also aims to suggest a future research agenda based on the TCCM approach, which will
serve as a valuable resource for making informed decisions regarding CBDC development and adoption.

1. Introduction
The rise of e-commerce has accelerated the demand for online pay­
ment mechanisms in the form of m-Payment (Agur et al., 2022).
M-Payment refers to mobile payment involving various financial trans­
actions using smartphones or other related devices. These transactions
involve the payment of bills, procurement of items, transfer of money,
and so on. It includes mobile wallets and technologies such as UPI, NFC,
and QR codes, along with payment apps like PhonePe, Paytm, Apple
Pay, Google Pay, etc. (Franque et al., 2021; Park et al., 2019; Phon­
thanukitithaworn et al., 2016).
Moving further, the emergence of innovative financial tools like
cryptocurrency, digital money, and virtual currency has critically
transformed the payment environment (Filimonau and Naumova, 2020;
Utkina et al., 2023). These constant technological improvements have
significantly enhanced the shopping mechanism and experiences (Ghosh
and Chowdhury, 2023). The rise of m-Payment has not only provided
consumers with more options for conducting online transactions, but it
has also facilitated blockchain-based payment systems. Now, business
houses, investors, consumers, and market players have all engaged in
the m-Payment world with cryptocurrencies since Bitcoin’s creation
(Barkhordari et al., 2017; Elsayed and Nasir, 2022). The rise of cryp­
tocurrency assets has presented difficulties for central banks in man­
aging currency circulation and implementing monetary policies,

facilitating the development of a parallel currency ecosystem (Akin
et al., 2023). In reaction, especially in conjunction with previous digital
currency endeavors like ‘Finland’s Avant project’ in the 1990s (Grym,
2020), central banks worldwide have expedited their investigation of
“Central Bank Digital Currency” (hereafter CBDC) (Boar Alkhowaiter,
2020). It is a digital form of paper money that possesses similar char­
acteristics and operationality as traditional currency (Bijlsma et al.,
2021; Kumhof and Noone, 2018; Ozili, 2022a). It can also be described
as the digital form of cash recognized as valid by law and easily
exchanged for paper currency (Cheng, 2023). There are two kinds of
CBDC: retail and wholesale. A digital banknote, known as a retail CBDC,
can be utilized by individuals to make payments to merchants, retailers,
or other individuals. Alternatively, banks and financial institutions can
employ a wholesale CBDC to facilitate the settlement of monetary market
swaps (Auer et al., 2021a; Çağlayan et al., 2023).
The deployment of centralized digital currency is now being tested to
improve its technical and practical capabilities and establish a frame­
work for global central banks. In late 2022, the BIS conducted a poll of
86 central banks to assess their participation in CBDC implementation
and their reasons and plans for possibly introducing one. The 2023
publication of the study revealed that four central banks, namely “The
Bahamas,” “the Eastern Caribbean,” “Jamaica,” and “Nigeria”, have
successfully introduced a functional retail CBDC. This poll revealed that
18% of central banks expressed their intention to create a retail CBDC

2. Conceptual boundaries
The introduction of cryptocurrencies and CBDC marks a turning
point in the monetary system. This shows how cash is moving from
physical to digital, and the transition toward CBDC is critical. So,
establishing theoretical boundaries is crucial for understanding and
exploring CBDC acceptance. It significantly influences the financial
ecosystem; hence, studying their potential to revolutionize online as
well as offline payment systems and integrate monetary systems with
digital technology is vital. To fully understand CBDC adoption, specific
conceptual limits have been established.
2.1. Currency and its evolution
The fascinating history of money dates back thousands of years
(Adjepong-Boateng, 2023). The evolution of money and finance has
resembled the changes in monetary history. It evolved from barter to
metal coinage to paper currency. In the modern monetary system, the
currency is a legal tender form of money issued entirely by the sovereign
(or its representative, the central bank) (Bank, 2022). In every economy,
money serves three fundamental roles: medium of exchange, store of
value, and unit of account (Amamiya, 2018). The form and functions of
money have evolved in response to changes in the economy and the
payment system, and these factors will continue to shape the future of

2.2. Cryptocurrencies and digital currency
These two concepts share the term “currency” but have distinct
meanings (Solberg Söilen and Benhayoun, 2022). Blockchain-based
cryptocurrencies are not governed by governments or their banking
institutions (Tredinnick, 2019). According to Sankar (2021), the rise of
several programmable cryptocurrencies in unspecified systems has
raised apprehensions among nations regarding probable risk of financial
stability. A CBDC is fiat money in an electronic form issued by a central
bank. It is a central bank liability and can be used for making payments
or storing value (Soderberg et al., 2022). It offers broader accessibility
than reserves, making it a versatile option. It also provides excellent
utility for transactions between retailers compared to cash. Additionally,
it has a different operational framework from other available currencies
(sovereign currencies and crypto assets), aiding it to serve a funda­
mentally different objective (Bonnet and Teuteberg, 2023; Kumhof and
Noone, 2018). The key objectives are enhancing financial inclusion,
improving the efficiency of payment systems, reducing reliance on
physical cash, strengthening the central bank’s ability to implement
monetary policy, etc. (Ahnert et al., 2022; Auer and Böhme, 2020;
Barrdear and Kumhof, 2016). Cryptocurrencies are tradable and possess
risk and return (Tredinnick, 2019). However, CBDCs are convertible to
existing money and do not bear any interest (Agur et al., 2019).
Although most designs of CBDCs already lack interest-bearing charac­
teristics, this matter continues to be a topic of continuous discussion.
Some research suggests interest-bearing CBDCs might significantly
affect their acceptance (Bibi and Canelli, 2023; Rahman, 2024). Auer
et al. (2021a), in their paper for “Bank for International Settlement,” have
suggested six factors that influence CBDC usage and design.
Cross-border payments, global accessibility, privacy assurance, stable
and robust operations, and powers like cash and real-time payments are
these six qualities.

3. Methodology
3.1. Review approach
For making an addition to the part of the growing field of conducting
reviews of literature in digital currency research (Bhaskar et al., 2022;
Elsayed and Nasir, 2022), we make an effort to understand the findings
of prior work on CBDC by using a hybrid systematic approach (Bem,
1995; Kraus et al., 2022; Tranfield et al., 2003). A systematic literature
review (SLR) is the most valuable scientific method (Paul and Criado,
2020). SLR addresses questions for research about a discipline’s status
quo. It also clarifies issues that a single study may overlook (Cook et al.,
1997; Paul and Bhukya, 2021; Petticrew and Roberts, 2008). A
well-written systematic review paper gives researchers a complete
overview, finds study gaps, and proposes future research (Lim et al.,
2021; Paul et al., 2021; Rosado-Serrano et al., 2018). So, systematic
methods clearly show that data gathering and synthesis processes in­
crease reproducibility (Kapoor et al., 2014; Williams et al., 2015). In
addition, Bibliometric analysis is also conducted to present the review
more thoroughly (Paul and Bhukya, 2021). These methods comprise
citation analysis, bibliography coupling, and co-citation analysis (Gao
et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2020). This method relies on statistical and
quantitative assessments, which helps to decrease subjective bias and
lead the researchers to the most relevant studies (Di Vaio et al., 2023;
Dohale et al., 2020; Rodríguez-López et al., 2020). The explanation of
the review process is presented in Fig. 1 and discussed thoroughly in the
following section.

3.5. Article coding
In this paper, the review process encompasses the analysis and syn­
thesis of data inductively, which is conducted through a systematic
coding methodology involving the development of primary codes, sec­
ondary themes, and key dimensions (Bouquet, 2005; Gioia et al., 2013;
Yessoufou et al., 2018). We analyzed the sample articles and coded them
in a manner that prioritized their content. Both researchers thoroughly
analyzed the papers and generated primary codes that closely resembled
the original phrasing. Subsequently, the initial step involved the
compilation and revision of the first-order codes, which were subse­
quently transformed into second-order themes. Finally, these themes
were condensed into aggregate dimensions (see Fig. 2). Moreover, it was
anticipated that the method would reveal findings regarding the evo­
lution of research throughout the years.
The initial analysis, known as 1st-order analysis, focuses on tracking
the terms used in research articles. However, it does not extract classi­
fications, causing an increase in the number of categories at the begin­
ning of a study. As the investigation progresses, we identify matches and
variations across numerous groups, which lowers the appropriate clas­
sifications to a controllable number. We name those groups and examine
the array. After finding a good set of themes and ideas, we try to extract
the new 2nd-order themes into “aggregate dimensions."
When we have the complete set of 1st-order words, 2nd-order
themes, and aggregate dimensions, we can build a data structure (see
Fig. 2). This stage is the most crucial in our study methodology. The data
structure facilitates the organization of our data into a helpful visual
representation. However, it also demonstrates how we proceeded from
the collection of articles to terms and subjects while conducting the
analysis, which is a crucial step for evaluating the rigor of the research.

4. Findings
4.1. Results from the systematic coding process
The data collected for this study has been categorized into three
distinct categories through a systematic coding process. This coding
process involved identifying and assigning primary codes, secondary
themes, and overall dimensions to the collected data. The resulting
categories provide a structured framework for comprehensively under­
standing CBDCs and present a systematic flow of information that sheds
light on various aspects of CBDCs, including their origins, essential
characteristics, factors influencing their adoption, and potential areas
for future research.
4.1.1. Regulatory/government perspective
Governments are increasingly acknowledging the need to prioritize
the development of CBDC. The government’s endorsement would
significantly influence the facilitation of CBDC adoption among the

theoretical models to present findings regarding CBDC adoption. An
investigation conducted by Liu et al. (2022) developed a theoretical
framework by combining the “Technological Acceptance Model (TAM)"

(Davoodalhosseini, 2022). In relation to CBDCs, individuals are more
likely to adopt this currency mechanism if they perceive it as
user-friendly, easily understandable, and beneficial for carrying out
transactions. Concurrently, TIM enhances the TAM by redirecting its
attention from the simple adoption and utilization of technology to its
wider impact on users. While TAM emphasizes the significance of
perceived utility and convenience of use in influencing the adoption of
technology, TIM considers the vital aspect of impact. It acknowledges

intricate digital financial instruments that require widespread adoption
and comprehensive analysis of their effects on economic stability,
financial inclusiveness, and user pleasure. Utilizing TIM, scholars, pol­
icymakers, and stakeholders may evaluate the influence of CBDC on
users’ financial behavior and address any possible conflicting
expectations.

literature on CBDC adoption. In their study, León et al. (2024), the re­

learning, and adaptation of distinct customers and merchants to various
currencies and payment modalities. The model precisely reproduces the
adoption of rCBDC. Their hypothetical scenarios correspond to debates
on the public digital euro. The findings indicate that consideration of
design options, stimulation, and constraints on rCBDC holding may
contribute to finding an optimal level of adoption. Additionally,
implementing a reverse waterfall mechanism, a favorable compensation
spread, and government subsidies through rCBDC can encourage the
adoption. In their study, Ramadiah et al. (2021) introduce a
multi-period agent-based model to analyze the macro-financial impacts
of implementing a retail CBDC. By calibrating it using aggregate sta­
tistics from the German retail payment market, they demonstrate how
the simulation model can systematically measure the influence of a
CBDC on adopting alternative payment methods, reducing in­
termediaries in the banking sector, and distributing consumer wealth.
The findings indicate that CBDC may be designed without significantly
affecting the financial sector’s balance sheet. Nevertheless, card service
providers may experience a significant decrease in their transaction
income. So, by integrating these models into the study, academicians
can enhance the comprehension of the complex aspects that influence
the adoption of CBDC and identify the obstacles that must be resolved.

5.1.3. Characteristics
The third pillar of the TCCM approach refers to the characteristics
that define the antecedents (refer to Table 4 and Figure no. 5). Several
studies have identified key characteristics like trust, risk, benefit, atti­
tude, relative advantage, and so on. However, there is a strong call to
understand these characteristics from different angles. For example, in
the case of trust, it is imperative to distinguish between trust in the
currency per se, trust in the fundamental technology, and trust associ­
ated with stakeholders such as central banks or financial institutions (Ma
et al., 2022; Solberg Söilen and Benhayoun, 2022). Further research can
also investigate the impact of trust on the adoption of CBDC. Moreover,
the determinants like user confidence can be studied as antecedents of
CBDC. Additionally, it is imperative for scholars to delve into more at­
tributes such as perceived benefits, perceived risk, and user engagement
in order to attain a holistic comprehension regarding the adoption dy­
namics of CBDC. Two potential research inquiries could be integrated
into future investigations, which upcoming researchers may explore
(refer to Table 4). By identifying pivotal elements, scholars can obtain
valuable perspectives on the attributes that facilitate the integration of
CBDCs as a reliable mode of electronic transaction. The objective of
these two inquiries is to investigate traits associated with CBDCs. By
examining the perceived advantages and risks of CBDC, including
transactional security, ease of use, and financial inclusivity, future
scholars can gain insight into the motivations behind factors that drive
individuals to use digital currencies (Roussou et al., 2019).
Furthermore, investigating the user experience while using CBDC,
such as user interface, usability, and customer support, can offer valu­
able information for enhancing user design and promoting widespread
acceptance.

quantitative and qualitative methods of data collecting and analysis to
provide a thorough understanding of user viewpoints, emotions, and
actions about digital currency payment. It is essential to triangulate the
data by employing surveys, experiments, interviews, and content anal­

This paper provides a valuable contribution to the existing body of
literature by identifying gaps in research pertaining to the adoption and
utilization of CBDC. Identifying these gaps is a foundation for subse­
quent theoretical inquiries in this nascent domain. The study delves into
the impact of user attributes and design elements on adopting CBDC.
This will broaden the knowledge of the determinants that mold users’
inclination toward digital currencies. The identified future research gap
in the study offers significant potential for the development of theoret­
ical constructs and frameworks. The analysis of CBDC’s consequences on
monetary policy and associated risks improves our understanding of its
potential effects on financial stability and the efficacy of monetary
policy transmission. Furthermore, China’s progress in the development
of digital currency and payment systems offers important insights for the
academic community.

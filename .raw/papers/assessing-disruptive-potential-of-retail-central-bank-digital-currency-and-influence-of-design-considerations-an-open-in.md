Monetary authorities globally have studied central bank digital currency (CBDC) for wholesale and retail ap­
plications since the 1990s. Besides countering private cryptocurrencies’ influence, central banks have also
explored other benefits of CBDC, such as improving their financial oversight, enhancing digital payment systems,
and reducing transaction fees and friction. For several years, the Bank of Thailand has also invested in research
and development of CBDC, including retail use cases. However, an optimized design for an official launch of a
retail CBDC in Thailand has yet to be determined, resembling fragmented progress observed among other central
banks. This research has leveraged disruptive innovation theory to provide a fresh perspective on retail CBDC as
potentially disruptive financial innovations. Additionally, it has proposed an open innovation-based methodol­
ogy to determine industry preferences concerning twelve design considerations for retail CBDC and their impact
on nine indicators of disruptive innovation. Data has been collected and analyzed from 98 respondents outside
the Bank of Thailand, all possessing relevant expertise or experience in electronic payments. Crosstab and logistic
regression analyses have indicated that retail CBDC in Thailand could embark on a disruptive innovation tra­
jectory by adhering to defined design choices. Significantly, entering a new detached market has been identified
as the most promising entry point, accompanied by explicit recommendations on nine out of twelve design
considerations.

Introduction
Central banks worldwide are exploring Central Bank Digital Cur­
rencies (CBDC) for various reasons, e.g., to improve financial oversight,
enhance security, and increase digital payment systems’ efficiency
(Cirasino, 2021; Ozili, 2023; Schueffel, 2023; Xu, 2022). CBDC is a
digital version of money issued by a central bank or federal agency
(Berentsen and Schär, 2018). It can be transacted on a centralized,
decentralized, or hybrid system (Berentsen and Schär, 2018; Panwar and
Agarwal, 2023). As of October 2024, 113 jurisdictions have initiated
projects to research and develop retail CBDC (r-CBDC) for local use,
wholesale CBDC (w-CBDC) for institutional or cross-border settlements,
or both (Mikhalev et al., 2024). This research focuses on r-CBDC.

r-CBDC possesses several benefits, such as information security,
faster settlement times, and reduced transaction fees for financial in­
stitutions (Bhawana and Kumar, 2021; Fung and Halaburda, 2016).
They also have points of sale security enhancement potential (Schueffel,
2023) and promote financial inclusion by formalizing a country’s
informal economy (Oh and Zhang, 2022; Ozili, 2023; Sarmiento, 2022;
Zams et al., 2020). By shifting towards a cashless society, r-CBDC can
enhance transparency and efficiency while reducing expenses associated
with physical cash (Fung and Halaburda, 2016). Furthermore, r-CBDC
could counter alternative cryptocurrencies by maintaining monetary
sovereignty and balancing government-issued currency with
private-issued digital currencies (Fung and Halaburda, 2016; Mukher­
jee, 2023; Suwanik et al., 2021).

Critics of r-CBDC have also raised concerns about privacy, central
banks’ reputational risk, decreased profitability and stability of com­
mercial banks, cybersecurity vulnerabilities, concentration of financial
power, and infringement on people’s freedoms (Schueffel, 2023).
Despite these criticisms, r-CBDC projects continue globally due to ex­
pected benefits and favorable regulatory conditions. However, most
projects are still in research and pilot stages, with only a few official
launches (Mikhalev et al., 2024). Fragmented progress with diverse
challenges remains.
r-CBDC projects worldwide vary in target users, scope, architecture,
and technology. An optimal approach for successful adoption is yet to be
determined (Didenko et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2021).
Jurisdiction-specific attributes, such as preferences for cash substitutes
and digital payment maturity, affect r-CBDC’s progress and acceptance
rate. Factors like internet coverage, smart device penetration, and dig­
ital literacy in each country also impact support for CBDC initiatives
(Khiaonarong and Humphrey, 2019; Lee et al., 2021; Monetary Au­
thority of Singapore, 2022; Oh and Zhang, 2022). Consequently, central
banks worldwide, including the Bank of Thailand (BoT), are continually
exploring suitable models of r-CBDC for their jurisdictions, leaving
scholars with vast research opportunities.
Primary research themes for r-CBDC involve central bank projects,
comparisons with other digital currencies, monetary policy implica­
tions, design and technology, payment systems, and regulations. Studies
also focus on factors affecting user adoption and public acceptance,
including societal contexts, r-CBDC characteristics, user perceptions,
privacy concerns, and technical aspects (Bhaskar et al., 2022; Hoang
et al., 2023). These studies shared a view that r-CBDC is a new form of
digital money that can coexist with cash, bank deposits, and crypto­
currency (Bolt et al., 2022; Jiang, 2020; Kunaratskul and Koonprasert,
2021; Schwarcz, 2022). Despite the progress made, significant research
gaps still exist. Few studies embrace notions of open innovation
(Chesbrough, 2003a, b) in their r-CBDC explorations, and none of them
examine its potential to disrupt money usage norms and become a new
form of cash for ordinary people.
Disruptive innovation offers inferior product features but improves
over time to surpass dominant incumbents (Bower and Christensen,
1995). For r-CBDC, its incumbents are the central bank’s cash and
electronic money (Bolt et al., 2022; Jiang, 2020; Kunaratskul and
Koonprasert, 2021; Schwarcz, 2022). Compared to the incumbent,
r-CBDC possesses some inferior attributes, such as technological per­
formance (e.g., transaction processing speed, scalability) and lack of
clear regulatory grounds for public trust (Kunaratskul and Koonprasert,
2021; Lay, 2023; Nocoń, 2023; Soderberg et al., 2023). Therefore, from
this perspective, the development of r-CBDC could be geared toward
disruptive innovation pathways.
Determining an innovation’s disruptiveness is crucial for under­
standing its potential market encroachment strategy (Christensen et al.,
2015). Scholars have proposed various frameworks to assess an in­
novation’s disruptiveness and identify its prospective market entrance
trajectory. These frameworks offer qualitative or quantitative methods,
ex-ante or ex-post analysis, and consider various kinds of measurements
(Guo et al., 2019; Pettersson and Lilliesköld, 2022). Knowing its
disruptive characteristics, r-CBDC can target underserved low-end
market segments, where current money options fall short in perfor­
mance and cost, or explore overlooked new fringe or detached markets,
where people’s demands differ. By establishing its presence in these
three emerging segments, r-CBDC can avoid head-to-head competition
with the incumbent monies and gain a strong foothold (Christensen,
1997; Christensen et al., 2015). This research distilled and proposed
nine disruptive potential indicators as measuring components of a
disruptiveness assessment framework. Its findings also suggest a prom­
ising pathway for r-CBDC.
However, as monetary authorities worldwide are still determining
optimal designs for r-CBDC, examining available options is essential
before assessing its disruptive innovation potential. This research

consolidates six studies on CBDC design and proposes twelve key con­
siderations for r-CBDC in Thailand. Individuals outside the Thai Central
Bank shared their r-CBDC design preferences and evaluated potential
disruptiveness. Their responses were analyzed to identify design pref­
erences, perceptions of the digital Thai baht’s disruptiveness, and how
twelve design choices influence nine indicators of disruptive potential
for r-CBDC.
This research aims to assess r-CBDC’s potential to follow a disruptive
innovation market encroachment pathway in Thailand and examine
how r-CBDC design alternatives could influence such potential. Our
research questions are: How do current design options for r-CBDC in­
fluence its disruptiveness in the Thai context? And if r-CBDC is posi­
tioned to pursue a disruptive innovation strategy in Thailand, which
market entrance appears most promising?
The next chapter details a literature review that forms our theoretical
background and presents a new perspective on r-CBDC as a disruptive
innovation. Chapter three synthesizes twelve key design considerations
and proposes an open innovation-based methodology to identify r-CBDC
design preferences. This chapter also examines relationships between
twelve design options and nine indicators of disruptive potential
through a framework for an innovation exploring disruptive pathways.
The subsequent chapter analyzes survey responses and their statistical
interpretation. The discussion chapter assists r-CBDC issuers in devel­
oping and implementing r-CBDC in Thailand, aligning with a disruptive
innovation trajectory and offering an alternative path for an official
launch. This paper concludes with academic contributions, research
limitations, and suggestions for future research.
Literature review
CBDC: A new digital form of money
As depicted in Figure 1, money can be classified by representation
(virtual or physical form), transaction (centralized or decentralized
systems), and creation (issued by a central bank or produced by a
network like cryptocurrency’s blockchain) (Berentsen and Schär, 2018).
Accordingly, this research defined CBDC as virtual money issued
monopolistically and transacted over decentralized systems. However, it
is worth noting that there is currently no consensus on handling CBDC
transactions. Unlike decentralized cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin,
most CBDC projects are still centralized (Panwar and Agarwal, 2023),
though decentralization would be more technologically sensible
(Berentsen and Schär, 2018). A dominant design for CBDCs has yet to be
established, with ongoing research exploring various ledger options,
such as account-based versus token-based systems (Abass, 2022; Chan,
2021), and distinctions among public, permissioned, and private
blockchains (Guo et al., 2022).

CBDC is divided into two primary use cases: w-CBDC and r-CBDC
(Abass, 2022). w-CBDC is designed to facilitate inter-bank transactions
and international settlements. It is exclusively issued and managed by
central banks or a designated institution, functioning like commercial
banks’ reserves. Access to w-CBDC is limited to select financial in­
stitutions, resulting in non-anonymous transactions (Bhawana and
Kumar, 2021; Lee et al., 2021; Monetary Authority of Singapore, 2022).
In contrast, r-CBDC is akin to traditional cash and is mainly used for
domestic transactions. Issued by central banks to the public, r-CBDC
serves as a medium of exchange for goods and services (Bhawana and
Kumar, 2021). While a hybrid design combining both use cases is
possible (Lee et al., 2021), this research focuses on r-CBDC.

The Bank of Thailand’s r-CBDC endeavor
In its 2020–2022 strategic plan, the Bank of Thailand (BoT) clearly
states an aim to “develop the central bank digital currency (CBDC) and
formulate policies and the regulatory framework for the private-sector digital
assets to preserve the effectiveness of monetary policy transmission and sta­
bility of the financial and payment systems.” (The Bank of Thailand, 2020).
The BoT was motivated to explore r-CBDC because privately issued
digital currencies could threaten financial and monetary stability. Thai
Central Bank expects increasing demand for r-CBDC, which could
partially replace cash and bank deposits. Issuing its own r-CBDC will
benefit Thailand by providing a safe digital currency and creating an
open digital payment infrastructure that fosters financial inclusion and
innovation. (Kunaratskul and Koonprasert, 2021).
In 2020, the BoT conducted surveys, workshops, forums, and dis­
cussions with various stakeholders, including regulators, financial in­
stitutions, and fintech startups, to explore the development of r-CBDC
(Kunaratskul and Koonprasert, 2021), resonating the open innovation
principle to embrace external ideas (Chesbrough, 2003a). Thai central
bank’s studies concluded that a promising r-CBDC for Thailand should
be (1) cash-like and interest-free, (2) distributed by traditional financial
intermediaries, and (3) have conversion limits. Participants agree with
the BoT that these features will prevent competition with deposits, avoid
bank runs, and support monetary policy while preserving financial in­
termediaries’ roles and fostering innovation. Subsequently, the BoT
launched two r-CBDC pilot tracks in August 2022: a foundation track
with two banks and a payment provider and an innovative CBDC
Hackathon competition (The Bank of Thailand, 2022). Moreover, from
late 2022 to October 2023, the BoT partnered with two commercial
banks and a payment service provider to conduct a pilot project.
Approximately 4000 pilot users transacted using retail r-CBDC at around
140 merchants. However, the Thai Central Bank concluded that further
studies and design explorations are still needed (Tansanguan et al.,
2024), leaving room for future research.

Rationales for nations to embrace r-CBDC
Central banks around the globe have been exploring CBDC projects,
the earliest of which originated from the Bank of Finland’s Avant card
project in 1993 for offline payments (Grym, 2020). As of October 2024,
authorities in 113 jurisdictions have launched 149 digital currencies and
initiated 170 projects for domestic r-CBDC, w-CBDC, or both (Mikhalev
et al., 2024). These authorities aim to improve financial oversight by
combating corruption, tax evasion, and money laundering. They also
support monetary policy implementation, enhance digital payment
systems, and promote innovative tax collection methods (Cirasino,
2021; Ozili, 2023; Schueffel, 2023; Xu, 2022). r-CBDC also enhances
security at points of sale or online and peer-to-peer transactions,
reducing fees and friction. As a central bank-issued legal tender, it
eliminates default and counterparty risks, leading to greater financial
system robustness, security, and stability (Schueffel, 2023).
There is a growing belief that private cryptocurrencies such as Bit­
coin, Litecoin, and Ethereum cannot become currencies for future
monetary systems (Xu, 2022), making them less hostile to financial
stability and monetary policy implementation. However, central banks
still consider r-CBDC as an appropriate response to these privately issued
digital currencies (Fung and Halaburda, 2016; Mukherjee, 2023).
r-CDBC remains a method to balance government-issued public money
with alternative digital currencies, preserve the country’s monetary
sovereignty, and make the government’s public money fit for trans­
forming the digital age’s financial landscape (Suwanik et al., 2021).

Extant studies on CBDC

Criticism and development progress across the world

Over the past decade, research on CBDC has expanded significantly.
Hoang et al. (2023) analyzed CBDC publications from 1997 to 2022,
identified seven primary research themes, and noted that motivations
for adoption differ by country, suggesting further investigation into
specific influencing factors. Bhaskar et al. (2022) examined 174
CBDC-related publications since 2018, identifying three key themes and
highlighting an ongoing potential for theoretical and methodological
advancements. In addition, scholars have explored factors influencing
central banks’ adoption of CBDC and public acceptance. Their studies
focus on how national and societal contexts impact CBDC development
and public acceptance. (Alfar et al., 2023; Khiaonarong and Humphrey,
2019; Luu et al., 2022; Maryaningsih et al., 2022; Ngo et al., 2023; Oh
and Zhang, 2022). Another focus area is CBDC’s features and functions,
trustworthiness, user characteristics and perceptions, privacy concerns,
implementation options, and how these factors may impact people’s
privacy concerns and acceptance of CBDC (Abass, 2022; Bai, 2020;
Bijlsma et al., 2021; Khiaonarong and Humphrey, 2022; Kunaratskul
and Koonprasert, 2021; Lee et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2022;
Söilen and Benhayoun, 2021; Tronnier et al., 2022; Wenker, 2022; Xia
et al., 2023). Finally, some studies discuss technical and architectural
aspects or propose viable implementation solutions (Braine and Shukla,
2022; Goodell et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2022; Jung and Jeong, 2021;
Kocsis et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2021).

Introducing r-CBDC has attracted criticism alongside its potential
benefits. Some researchers argue that central banks should avoid issuing
anonymous cryptocurrencies due to reputational risks (Berentsen and
Schär, 2018). Commercial banks may also question fairness in KYC and
AML compliance when r-CBDC holders can transact anonymously.
Scholars have highlighted several disadvantages, including risks of bank
runs, decreased profitability and stability for commercial banks, reduced
competition, cybersecurity vulnerabilities, and privacy concerns
Schueffel (2023). Forbes (2022) expressed his worries about personal
freedoms linked to r-CBDC, urging the US Congress to halt developments
of a digital US dollar, which he labeled “a formula for tyranny.” President
Donald Trump’s recent executive order to halt CBDC progress resonates
with this concern (Trump, 2025).
Despite discontentment, r-CBDC’s global progress is driven by
anticipated benefits. As of October 2024, only four r-CBDC projects have
been launched: the Sand Dollar of the Bahamas in 2020, the e-Naira of
Nigeria in 2022, the JAM-DEX of Jamaica in 2023, and the Zig of
Zimbabwe in 2023 (Mikhalev et al., 2024). Most projects remain in
either pilot, proof of concept, or research stages. Fragmentation of
r-CBDC projects varies by progress and design parameters, such as target
segments and technology. In countries with large informal economies,
r-CBDC may face acceptance issues if holding costs outweigh benefits

Disruption is a process, not a one-off event, and it commences when
an innovation enters either low-end footholds that seek similar tech­
nologies, probably with lower performance but at a lower cost, or newmarket footholds with unprecedented demand for novel technologies
(Christensen et al., 2015). In an interview with Forbes magazine,
Christensen also stated that starting at the bottom of a market or nascent
segment is not always the case for disruption. Still, it is correlated with
an unattractive business model to the incumbents (Adams, 2016).
Schmidt and Druehl (2008) further explained that disruptive innovation
for a new market can either enter a new fringe market or a new detached
market. The new fringe market is characterized by buyers’ preferences
that are only incrementally different from those of the current low-end
market. In contrast, the new detached market consists of buyers whose
needs are so divergent or dramatically different from the current
low-end market that a simple price reduction may not suffice for a
purchase.
Instead of launching r-CBDC on a high-end trajectory as incumbent
monies and risk failure, the central bank can consider three alternative
encroachments for disruptive innovation: the low-end, the new fringe,
and the new detached markets. By embracing disruptive innovation
theory, we can increase the odds of a newcomer like r-CBDC seizing its
market foothold and surviving (Christensen, 1997) while incumbents,
such as cash or electronic money, can maintain their positions with
mainstream users until both trajectories converge (Christensen et al.,
2015).

Despite a wide range of CBDC research themes, two commonalities
are observed. First, most of this research focuses mainly on r-CBDC. A
possible explanation is that w-CBDC is relatively straightforward
regarding its use case: settlements among central banks, commercial
banks, and selective financial institutes. As a result, room for academic
contribution may not justify further study of w-CBDC. In contrast, rCBDC remains in a large research avenue.
Second, extant studies generally view r-CBDC as a new digital form of
money and propose compromising ways for its coexistence with cash,
bank deposits, cash equivalent, and privately issued cryptocurrency
(Bolt et al., 2022; Jiang, 2020; Kunaratskul and Koonprasert, 2021;
Schwarcz, 2022). However, in countries with wide adoption of cash
substitutes such as mobile or digital payments, i.e., Thailand (Toomgum,
2022), this proposition may not be viable (Khiaonarong and Humphrey,
2019).
As a result, two significant research gaps exist. First, few studies
emphasize how a central bank should embrace the open innovation
concept and solicit opinions from broader industry experts, which could
lead to new markets and new business models (Chesbrough, 2003b).
Embracing open innovation in fintech research is not uncommon, as it
can enable intensive collaboration between regulators and private sec­
tors (Goo and Heo, 2020) or help financial industry members accom­
plish win-win partnerships (Al Nawayseh, 2020). Moreover, in the
fourth industrial revolution and post-COVID-19 era, subtle finance and
banking niche markets are more demanding, and open innovation dy­
namics are an approach to serve their needs (Niankara and Traoret,
2023).
Second, none of these studies adopt disruptive innovation theories to
discuss how innovation could disrupt existing norms and drive change
without competing head-on with incumbents (Christensen et al., 2015).
By adopting these notions as a theoretical background, research can
explore a new avenue for r-CBDC’s alternative market entrance that
might create a strong foothold and traction.

disruptiveness, categorize its disruptive trajectory, or evaluate its
disruptive potential degree. These frameworks offer qualitative or
quantitative analyses, either before (ex-ante) or after (ex-post) a market
disruption, and may or may not consider policy and macroeconomic
factors or internal connectivity of selected indicators (Guo et al., 2019;

widely adopted, it is logical to postpone a quantitative evaluation of its
disruptive potential until more empirical data becomes available.
However, a qualitative assessment of its disruptive nature and a
pathway it should pursue remains relevant and feasible. This investi­

r-CBDC without comparing it to other innovations.

Applying disruptive innovation theory in r-CBDC’s context
Disruptive innovation theory (Christensen, 1997; Christensen et al.,
2015) explains a process by which an innovation is introduced with
affordability and accessibility, generally targeting a low-end or new
market. Then, it progresses to mainstream users, ultimately displacing
market incumbents. According to this theory, a new entrant innovation
will likely fail if it pursues a sustaining strategy and competes
head-to-head with established competitors in mainstream or high-end
markets. An innovation entrant is more likely to survive and disrupt a
market if it starts in “Low-end footholds” or “New-market footholds”
that incumbents typically overlook (Christensen et al., 2015). Disruptive
innovation initially offers a new set of product features that are inferior
to attributes most valued by mainstream users (Bower and Christensen,
1995; Christensen, 1997; Christensen et al., 2015). However, technology
improves over time, surpasses dominant technologies, and eventually
displaces incumbents in mainstream or high-end markets. Since a
disruptive innovation possesses unique attributes and requires a
different market diffusion (Schmidt and Druehl, 2008), mislabeling it
may reduce its chances of success (Christensen et al., 2015).
r-CBDC is likely a disruptive innovation compared to cash or elec­
tronic money due to its current inferior attributes. Such drawbacks
hindering its wide adoption and acceptance include technological per­
formance and regulatory and public trust challenges. Average trans­
action response time and scalability while preserving user’s privacy are
concerning performance indicators for r-CBDC (Kunaratskul and Koon­
prasert, 2021; Soderberg et al., 2023), and solutions applied for private
cryptocurrency still have significant limitations (Nocoń, 2023). As a
result, some researchers, e.g., Zhang et al. (2021) or Lovejoy et al.
(2022), have studied alternatives to increase transaction processing

Methodology

Design considerations of r-CBDC

Research method

Unlike other financial innovations, a universally accepted standard
for r-CBDC design and implementation has yet to come. Several studies
have been conducted to establish different CBDC design considerations
(Table A.2 in the Appendix). Each design proposition uses different
taxonomies even though they refer to the same thing. Therefore, they
must be consolidated and de-duplicated first.
Since this research focuses on the Thai context, design considerations
proposed by the BoT studies (Kunaratskul and Koonprasert, 2021;
Tansanguan et al., 2024) became our baseline. Relevant design consid­
erations from other studies were embedded, while irrelevant consider­
ations, such as choosing between retail and wholesale, were omitted.
Other design considerations excluded are the degree of implementation,
such as money supply level, security controls, system resiliency, super­
visory level, or risk appetite. In summary, twelve design considerations

To operate our research framework, an online self-administered
survey was created. An Item-Objective Congruence (IOC) assessment
form was also prepared and distributed online to four domain experts for
validity check. Two of them have regulatory and business expertise from
banking, fintech start-ups, digital assets, and cryptocurrency businesses.
Another is a university lecturer with a solid academic background in
technology innovation. The last one is an entrepreneur specializing in
innovation management and product development. Comments from
these experts were embraced to improve the questionnaire, which was
subsequently re-assessed. This process was repeated until the IOC index
indicated that all questionnaires were valid.
To test our hypotheses, survey respondents were asked to contem­
plate twelve design aspects to develop a model of r-CBDC in their minds.
Next, they were prompted to assess nine disruptive innovation in­
dicators related to their anticipated model of r-CBDC. Finally, we
analyzed all 108 hypothesized correlations between independent and
dependent variables using crosstab analysis and the Chi-square test.
Additionally, these relationships were examined with binary or multi­
nomial logistic regression to confirm associations and gauge the extent
of an influence each design consideration has on each disruptive
indicator.
To reasonably determine r-CBDC’s disruptive potential and potential
market encroachment, we also employed the Constructivist Grounded
Theory (CGT) research method. Developed by Kathy Charmaz in 1995,
CGT posits that researchers construct data and theories through their
interactions with interviewees or focus groups (Mohajan and Mohajan,
2022). CGT encourages creativity and helps participants make sense of
their experiences, which is particularly useful when exploring a complex
topic (Dovetail Editorial Team, 2023), such as identifying r-CBDC design
preferences and assessing its disruptive potential. Consequently, we
adopted CGT by conducting in-depth interviews with subject matter
experts based on hypothesis testing results to ensure that information
gathered on r-CBDC design considerations, its disruptive characteristics,
and their associations were theoretically saturated.
Since this work involves human subjects, formal approval from our
designated institutional committee was requested and obtained. All
procedures were also performed in compliance with relevant laws and
institutional guidelines.

Instrument design

Respondents’ choices for twelve design considerations were
analyzed with the Chi-square test for homogeneity to identify designs
with homogeneous preferences. Supposed that any three-option design
consideration demonstrates significant Chi-square results (p-value <
0.05), its top two selected options would be tested again, resulting in
column “2nd Round Test” of Table A.6. Consequently, of the 98 responses,
six design considerations, including onboarding (DC02), interestbearing (DC05), architecture (DC08), privacy (DC09), programma­
bility (DC10), and transaction cost (DC11), statistically demonstrate a
homogenous preference. Table A.7 compares design preferences from
our survey with the BoT pilot test (Tansanguan et al., 2024) and reveals
that three design preferences from our survey differ from those of the
Thai central bank.

A three-section online questionnaire for data collection was devel­
oped. The first section introduces our research’s background and ob­
jectives. It also includes screening questions regarding participants’
experience with digital payments. Suppose respondents have no expe­
rience with mobile banking, internet banking, electronic money, digital
wallets, or ATMs/CDMs. In that case, they will be excluded from the
survey and presented with a thank-you message. Qualified respondents
will then answer profiling questions covering their industry sector, work
experience, job level, and role. After completing these questions, re­
spondents will proceed to an informative page about r-CBDC in Thailand
to establish a common understanding.
The second section collects responses for variables representing rCBDC design considerations through twelve questions (Table A.3).
These variables are coded with a prefix “DC” followed by a two-digit
sequence. This section aims to collect respondents’ design preferences
and establish their version of r-CBDC.
The third questionnaire section assesses r-CBDC’s disruptive poten­
tial and classification of encroachment pathways. This section is devel­
oped using constructs proposed by dos Santos Paulino and Le Hir (2016),
Schmidt and Druehl (2008), and Christensen et al. (2002), as shown in
Table A.4. Respondents would assess their version of r-CBDC for nine
disruptiveness indicators, coded with a “DI” prefix followed by a 2-digit
sequence. Answers to this section can determine how the respondents
assess their version of r-CBDC for each disruptive innovation charac­
teristic, which can lead to corresponding market encroachment path­
ways as summarized in Table A.5.

Discussion
Early findings from this research identify six homogeneous design
preferences that align with works by other scholars. Bai (2020), for
instance, concluded that a user’s intention to adopt r-CBDC is positively
influenced by effort expectancy and facilitating conditions, aligning
with our respondents’ preference toward less complex unconditional
onboarding (DC02). Bijlsma et al. (2021) also supported interest-bearing
features (DC05). Hybrid architecture (DC08) is similar to a conclusion by
Goodell et al. (2021) and Braine and Shukla (2022). The former author
also proposed a model implying limited privacy (DC09), with which we
concur. In addition, Kocsis et al. (2021) offered a smart contract pro­
totype for r-CBDC that supports industrial cooperation, aligning with
limited programmability (DC10) preference. Finally, Khiaonarong and
Humphrey (2022) also conclude that transaction cost (DC11) is an
adoption determinant. Based on these alignments, an r-CBDC issuer
should consider our six preferences as a baseline for design
considerations.
Although only twelve out of 108 hypotheses are partially accepted
based on the findings from crosstab and logistic regression analyses,
these results still address the research questions. By integrating these
statistically accepted hypotheses with corresponding indicators for each
disruptive innovation market encroachment in Table A.5, we can outline
how r-CBDC should be designed to accommodate low-end, new fringe,
or newly detached disruptive pathways and determine which pathway is
more promising.

Suppose the BoT decides to pursue a new detached market pathway.
In that case, this research has identified five key design considerations to
support this direction (Table A.26). These include launching r-CBDC for
a specific user group (DC01), implementing conditional registration
(DC02), distributing directly from the BoT to users (DC04), allowing
software wallets only (DC06), and granting open programmability
(DC10). Issuance mode (DC03), connectivity (DC07), and transaction
limitations (DC12) are areas that may require further A/B testing.
However, an option for no transaction limitation (DC12) tends to have
more supporting disruptive indicators. In summary, considering four
homogeneous design preferences, including interest-bearing capabilities
(DC05), architecture (DC08), privacy (DC09), and transaction costs
(DC11), the r-CBDC issuer would have already determined nine out of
twelve design choices that favor new detached market entrance.
In addition, despite diverse design combinations, survey re­
spondents’ views on r-CBDC’s disruptive potential are more likely to
favor a new detached market pathway. This interpretation is supported
by three out of seven favorable indicators, while three additional in­
dicators show ambivalence. Only one performance-related indicator
(DI01) displays a negative outlook. Nevertheless, industry expert in­
terviewees argue that performance is a vital factor that cannot be
compromised. Therefore, respondents may assess their r-CBDC’s per­
formance optimistically and consider it comparable to existing incum­
bent monies.
Based on design preferences, assessments of disruptive potential,
their associations, and industry experts’ comments, an outlook for rCBDC entering the new detached market appears more promising than
other options. With nine out of twelve design choices determined,
implementation uncertainty is also minimized. Additionally, exper­
imenting with target users from this niche market could create a more
specific testing environment, making this option a more sensible path for
further exploration. Despite a novelty, our suggestion for r-CBDC to
explore a new detached market, where users’ needs are dramatically
different, aligns with Isabelle et al. (2023) who proposed that CBDC
could address a trust issue in emerging economies, such as a virtual
world or metaverse.
Hence, a new critical question arises: which economic sectors in
Thailand can be classified as a new detached market where user re­
quirements are significantly different? And whether this market suits rCBDC? While the answers depend on future research objectives and
context, the first potential candidate could be the Web3 industry, which
focuses on decentralizing Internet services and transforming various
sector infrastructures (Guidi et al., 2022; Potts and Rennie, 2019). Since
participants in this virtual economy require virtual currency to trade
virtual assets, a trustworthy government-issued r-CBDC could provide a
compelling value proposition (Isabelle et al., 2023; Vidal-Tomás, 2023).
The entertainment complex industry is another fresh candidate for a
new detached market of r-CBDC, particularly in Thailand. As of
November 2024, the Thai government is working to pass a bill to legalize
gaming, which is predominantly illegal in the country. This new eco­
nomic sector would be confined to specific areas where only licensed

businesses are permitted to operate (Sattaburuth, 2024), making it a
promising new detached market where r-CBDC could establish a strong
presence.
Researchers can employ various product development tools to test rCBDC in a prospective new detached market, such as product pro­
totypes, use case simulations, and scenario testing. They can also apply
widely recognized frameworks, like the extended Unified Theory of
Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT2) by Venkatesh et al.
(2012), to measure r-CBDC’s user acceptance and usage determinants.
By following this approach, Thailand could develop an optimal version
of disruptive r-CBDC innovation and select a promising encroachment
strategy that leads to sustainable traction.
Conclusion
Our research employs disruptive innovation theory and proposes a
framework for identifying alternative market encroachments of r-CBDC
based on its disruptive potential and relevant design considerations. It
also incorporates open innovation principles by crowdsourcing insights
from industry stakeholders outside the Thai central bank. Our findings
establish connections between r-CBDC design choices and their align­
ment with three disruptive innovation trajectories. Our proposed
framework may also be applied to innovations seeking to evade direct
competition with incumbents by exploring disruptive pathways.
While this research primarily focuses on the Thai context and ex­
plores alternative pathways for launching r-CBDC in Thailand, research
frameworks and tools that we have developed can be adapted for other
innovations and jurisdictions. Similar benefits can be expected, i.e.,
suggestions of how design choices can influence disruptive innovation
pathways. However, we acknowledge potential limitations from sam­
pling error or dominant responses by regulated financial or fintech
groups. Therefore, future research could target a larger sample size with
greater diversity and might consider respondents’ literacy levels and
experiences in electronic payments or cryptocurrency as well.
Empirical results indicate that r-CBDC can achieve user adoption
through disruptive innovation pathways in Thailand. Statistical analysis
suggests specific design combinations for r-CBDC that align with each
disruptive innovation encroachment strategy. Nine design choices
associated with a new detached market significantly enhance r-CBDC’s
feasibility. Therefore, researchers should further identify economic
sectors in Thailand that fit this new detached market definition and
tailor their r-CBDC version based on resulting design considerations. The
Web3 industry and the entertainment complex business are potential
new detached market candidates, offering significant opportunities for
future study.

Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) could contribute to modernizing the financial sector by
making wholesale and retail payments more efficient, deepening financial inclusion and reducing
informality in emerging economies. Despite these potential benefits, the question remains on
whether a CBDC system could bring material benefits when a country already has a wellestablished and advanced fast payment system (FPS). This paper assesses the value proposition
of a CBDC system in comparison to current payments schemes such as Pix in Brazil, based on
quantitative and qualitative data collected through a survey of a sample of one thousand adults
and merchants and focus groups in urban, peri‑urban and rural areas in Brazil. Findings suggest
that connectivity and securities issues are the most significant challenges for further adoption of
Pix. A well-designed CBDC system could address the challenges of failed transactions due to
connectivity and security issues, if it is designed specifically considering these shortcomings as
priorities. The question remains whether this is the suggested approach, or the focus should be on
addressing these shortcomings by improving and developing the current Pix payments
infrastructure.

1. Introduction
By January 2026, there were three live retail CBDCs implemented globally in Jamaica, Bahamas, and Nigeria while 11 central
banks were conducting retail CBDC pilots (CBDC Tracker). A CBDC is a form of digssital money, denominated in the national unit of
account, which is a direct liability of the central bank (BIS, 2021). CBDCs are often described as digital cash, can be used digitally for
low-value transactions in their retail form, and its conversion rate to cash is at par.
In the central banking community, there is a certain level of acknowledgement and understanding of the potential advantages of
having a CBDC system. However, there is no consensus on the benefits to pursue the development of a retail CBDC system which mostly
depends on the local context (Fig. 1).
Considering this background, this paper has the goal of assessing whether a CBDC system could significantly complement the
financial system and bring further material benefits in comparison to FPS such as Pix in Brazil. The paper examines quantitative and
qualitative data on the access and usage of payment solutions, specifically Pix in Brazil. It synthesizes selected data and insights
gathered from October to November 2024 through a survey with a sample of one thousand individuals and merchants and focus groups
conducted in Itatiba and Mococa in the State of Saõ Paolo. Further details on the methodology and the sample for the collection of the
quantitative and qualitative data is in Annex A.

2. Diagnosis of payment systems in Brazil
This section provides a diagnosis of Pix and the CBDC (Digital Drex) in Brazil, based on the framework outlined in Section 2. It starts
with a brief overview of the fast payment solutions before Pix (the card schemes: debit and credit cards), current landscape of Pix and
the CBDC, and then the framework to assess the systems is applied.
2.1. Fast payment solutions before Pix: debit and credit cards
The BCB launched Pix in November 2020 with the policy goal of reducing the usage of cash, promoting financial inclusion, fostering
competition in the financial sector and achieving an efficient and resilience payments system in terms of cost and settlement of
transactions (IMF, 2023).
Before Pix, despite the Brazilian legal and regulatory framework provided for the development of a payment scheme that facilitates
real-time retail payment transfers, there was a lack of existing payment solutions designed by the private sector that allow for fast and
instant settlement of payments transactions (Lobo et al., 2021). The cards scheme allowed for digital payments; however, generally,
with a deferred and not real-time settlement and at high cost for merchants accepting payments, specifically, micro and small retailers.
In addition, the cards scheme facilitated mainly Person-to-Business (P2B) and not Person-to-Person (P2P) payments.
The implication of this was a high rate of usage of cash. In 2019, 77 percent of retail transactions were conducted in cash,12 percent
with debit cards and 7 with credit cards (BCB, 2021) (Fig. 2). From a demographic perspective, the usage of cash was also predominant
in the low-income segment (87 percent), individuals who completed up to primary education (88 percent), and individuals in the
North and Northeast of the country. In addition, by 2019, before the launch of Pix, 70 percent of adults have not used the mobile phone
to make a payment, increasing to 82 percent in the North of Brazil and 77 percent for adults from 46 to 60 years old.
The main motivation for consumers to use cash was the lack of acceptance of digital payments by merchants for low-value
transactions and security issues for high-value transactions. In addition, cost is a relevant aspect reported by individuals and mer­
chants using cash. Fig. 4 reflects that credit cards had a low rate of preference among merchants (BCB, 2021) (Figs. 3 and 4). Prior to
the development of Pix, interchange fees were implemented with the policy goal of reducing merchants’ acceptance fees. Also, before
Pix, there was concentration in the acquiring market and closed-loop systems. An example that preceded Pix was the launch of a big
tech payment services which merchants’ fees amounted to 3.99 percent (BIS, Bulletin No 52, 2022). Two relevant policy goals of the
introduction of the Pix system were fostering competition and reducing the merchants’ acceptance cost of digital payments. The
average cost of Pix transactions is 0.33 percent in comparison to 1.13 percent for debit cards and 2.34 percent for credit cards (Saiyid,
2023).

2.2. Pix

Pix was designed to support any use cases for retail payments (Lobo et al., 2021). This includes P2P, merchants’ acceptance, utility
bills payments, Government-to-Person (G2P), P2B, Business-to-Business (B2B), recurring payments, cross-border payments, among
others. The use case that is used the most is P2P with transactions amounting to 2.3 billion in September 2024. However, the adoption
of the use case P2B is increasing significantly and reaching the same level of usage than P2P (Fig. 11).
Since the early stage of design and implementation of the Pix system, the BCB have worked and engaged closely with the private
sector with the purpose of ensuring the adoption of the system. The BCB created the Pix Forum which is the vehicle to engage with the
financial sector. A key activity under the forum is the definition of use cases and product features among the BCB and the private sector.
Product features include Pix automatic and agendado which allow consumers to schedule recurrent and program payments, and troco
and saque which are cash out capabilities, among others. In addition, minimum user experience requirements are set out by the BCB in
collaboration with the private sector to ensure a user-centric approach. This could be sometimes a challenge since this function goes
beyond the usual scope of monetary authorities (Lobo and Brandt, 2021).
Pix is owned by the BCB that manages as well as the real-time payment infrastructure (SPI), which is a RTGS platform operational
since 2002, and the user address database (DITC) which includes identifiers such as phone numbers, email addresses and randomly
generated identification (which are payment alias with zero personal data) to access and use the Pix system and minimize fraud. Pix
requires access to the digital infrastructure in the form of a smartphone with internet and mobile connectivity which can be unreliable
in some regions of the country, specifically in the northern states. Regulated financial entities are required to participate in the Pix
system. Financial entities that are not regulated by the BCB are able to participate in the Pix system indirectly through regulated
entities but are required to comply with certain regulations and supervision applicable to the offering of Pix services, including
technical and operational capabilities such as liquidity risk management, cybersecurity, AML/CFT and capital requirements.
2.3. The digital real: drex
The BCB is exploring the issuance of their CBDC, the Digital Brazilian Real or Drex. The Drex would be a form of digital money,
denominated in the national unit of account, which is a direct liability of the central bank. The main difference between the Drex and
commercial bank and e-money transferred through Pix is that the Drex would be issued by the BCB (a central bank) in comparison to
commercial bank or e-money which is privatively-issued by a commercial bank, digital wallet or PSP. The same applies to crypto­
currencies in the form of stablecoins, tokens or others. At a retail level, in practice, this implies that holders of a CBDC would be able to
redeem its value directly with the central bank, in the same manner as cash. By contrast, holders of commercial bank money or emoney would hold a direct claim on the private institution that issued the instrument, rather than on the central bank. This is relevant
in the event of bankruptcy or liquidation of a private institution. Holders of commercial or e-money are generally protected with
deposit insurance up to a certain amount. Beyond such amount, holders would require to claim and redeem funds in a judicial
bankruptcy proceeding. This would not be the case of a CBDC, as currently occurs with cash (Fig. 12). As in the case of Pix, access to the
digital infrastructure is required in order to access the Drex. However, a Drex could be designed with an offline capability to address
the lack of reliable internet and mobile connectivity in some regions of the country.
The BCB acknowledges that Pix is addressing the financial inclusion and payments gaps; however, a Drex could complement and
deepen the access to non-payment and payment solutions such as lending and access to the capital markets. Tokenization could reduce
the number of financial intermediaries and foster automation of processes that could lead to a more efficient, resilient and affordable

The Drex journey started in August 2020 with the creation of a working group with the aim of analyzing the potential benefits,
opportunities and challenges of a CBDC. In May 2021, the BCB then established the guidelines for issuance of the digital Brazilian
currency (BCB, 2021). According to the guidelines, the priorities for the Drex are enhancing the efficiency of the retail payment system,
fostering new business models and innovation based on technology advances such as smart contracts and programmable money,
increasing efficiencies in cross-border payments. In 2021, the BCB held a series of workshops with the purpose of engagement with the
society and the private sector on the potential opportunities of the Drex. In 2022, nine projects were selected under the LIFT Challenge
Real Digital in collaboration with Fenasbac to assess specific potential use cases, including delivery versus payment (DvP), payment
versus payment (PvP), decentralized finance (DeFi), offline transactions (Banco Central do Brasil 2022).
In 2023, the BCB updated the guidelines and moved forward to the next phase of exploration of the Drex. The BCB also announced
the Drex pilot (Banco Central do Brasil 2023). The use cases defined for the pilot include collaterized credit, financing of international
trade transactions, foreign exchange, liquidity pools, tokenization of agribusiness assets and carbon credits, among others. Participants
of the pilot include national commercial banks, banking associations, digital banks, payment services providers and digital asset
services providers (BCB, Digital Real, 2024). As for Pix, the BCB created the Digital Real Forum with the purpose of engaging with the
private sector in the development of the pilot.
From a design model perspective, the BCB considers the Drex as a wholesale platform where regulated financial entities would have
access to the Drex and then tokenize it for retail use cases. The underlying technology for the Drex is Hyperledger, a private and
permissioned blockchain.
Only 34 percent of the participants from the survey have heard about the Drex, and 59 percent would be interested in using it. 48
percent of the survey reported that they do not know if the Drex will be the future. This is reasonable due to the early stage of
development of the Drex. However, if the BCB decides to implement a Drex, awareness by the both the BCB, the Comissāo de Valores
Mobiliairios (CVM) and the private sector would be required for the Drex to be adopted. 53 percent of the sample highlighted that
understanding how the Drex works would encourage them to use it, and 21 percent of the sample report that they would be encouraged
to use the Drex if it facilitates access to credit (Figs. 13-16).
3. Framework
A framework for the development of FPS is presented based on a general framework (Dong He et al., 2017) that assessed pre­
liminary considerations of fintech and digital financial services.
The enhanced framework has a two-fold approach:
1. Assess the demand for unmet payment services and limited supply by Pix.
2. Identify shortcomings which can be potentially addressed with a well-designed retail CBDC system.

Stablecoins, tokenized deposits and other digital assets
Stablecoins and tokenized deposits are privately issued digital currencies which are backed by high-liquid assets such as fiat
money and short-term government bonds in custody in regulated entities. Contrary to stablecoins, the so-called utility tokens are
digital assets which are not supported with reserves or high-liquid assets, relying on other type of reserves such as
cryptocurrencies.
Stablecoins are regulated in several jurisdictions globally. The European Markets in Crypto Assets Regulation (MiCAR) was one
of the first regulatory framework implemented in Europe in 2023 which includes stablecoin under the scope of the regulation
under the taxonomy of e-money tokens. Further jurisdictions enacted digital assets regulations as well, including the global
financial hubs: Singapore, Hong Kong, UAE, among others. In July 2025, the United States Congress enacted the GENIUS Act
which aims to provide regulatory clarity for stablecoins in the United States. In Brazil, the BCB announced that regulatory
proposals for digital assets will be finalized shortly.
A well-designed stablecoin or tokenized deposits system which considers reserves, custody, disclosure and transparency re­
quirements and clear redemption rights could provide similar opportunities and complement a wholesale or retail CBDC system
in terms of real-time settlement, programmability, traceability and affordability of domestic and cross-border transactions and
fostering competition in the financial sector. Potential risks such as consumer and investor protection, privacy, financial stability
implications and currency sovereignty, among others should be considered and addressed in the design phase of such a system.

The public demand side focuses on analyzing shortcoming of features in financial products which translates into unmet consumer
needs. This includes the following elements:
a) Trust, security and privacy
i. Lack of trust is one of the main challenges for vulnerable groups when assessing whether to access or use a digital financial service
instead of cash.
ii. Fraud and security issues are still common in many markets.
iii. Privacy and fraud are intrinsically related among each other. If personal data is not being managed appropriately by the

a) Convenience, added value and access
i. A solution is convenient when the consumer has a benefit that can be translated into time efficiency, cost savings, or acting as a
bridge or platform to ancillary services.
ii. Generally, a product is adopted when it provides an added value to the users by addressing a specific need. The value added of a
product diminishes if the user already has a similar product with the same features which is easier-to-use with another third party
provider.
a) Affordability
i. One of the main variables that usually consumers and merchants consider when using a financial product is its cost, specifically in
emerging markets with challenging economies such as high inflation rates.
a) Programmability
i. Programmability means the ability to automate payments if certain conditions are met.
ii. Use cases include automated payments at delivery or receipt of goods, tax collection automation when retailers accept a digital
payment, utility bills payments, insurance claims payments, disbursement of a credit line for MSME lending, among others.

4.1.1. Custody and segregation of customer funds requirements
Communiqué No 25.097 of 2014 issued by the BCB provides for the adoption of the Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures
(PFMI), published by the Committee on Payment and Settlement Systems of the BIS and IOSCO. The PFMI are standards that aim to
promote efficiency, transparency and safety in financial market infrastructure and to mitigate financial integrity and stability risks (BIS
CPSS/IOSCO, 2012), including custody and segregation of funds requirements under principle 16. The intention and rationale behind
such policy and regulatory requirements apply to both commercial banks and PSPs providing Pix services but based on different
structures.
Although the PFMI are applicable directly to regulated financial entities and were designed mainly for commercial banks but not for
e-money issuers such as many of the indirect participants of the Pix system, Brazil is one of the countries that already implemented
specific provisions in legislation to protect users’ funds of e-money issuers with the policy goal of minimizing insolvency and liquidity
risks. According to the Pix regulatory framework, direct participants of the Pix system are required to create an account at the BCB
where customers funds sit. Direct participants not only include commercial banks but also PSPs and digital wallets which settle
payment transactions at the SPI such as digital wallet providers like Mercado Pago.
4.1.2. Licensing and supervision
In 2013, Law No 12,865 (Brazilian Payments System) granted authority to the BCB to regulate retail payments and to develop
policies to promote competition, financial inclusion and transparency by implementing a fast payments system. Resolution 4952/2021
from the BCB defines direct and indirect participation for settlement of Pix transactions.
The BCB issued specific regulations for financial entities offering Pix services, including minimum requirement for the use expe­
rience, security requirements, fees, complaints handling mechanisms, QR standards, payments initiation, among others. The frame­
work is applicable not only to regulated entities but also other non-regulated institutions that are allowed to participate in the Pix
system. Financial entities that hold more than five hundred thousand accounts are required to participate in the system as mandatory
participants. This includes not only commercial banks but digital banks and wallets providers. Institutions with less than five hundred
thousand accounts can participate voluntarily but need to comply with certain minimum regulatory requirements including having an
arrangement with a direct participant and holding equity that amounts to Brazilian Real 1 million (BCB, Resolution 1/2020).

4.1.3. Privacy regulations
In 2018 the General Data Protection Act (GDPA) was enacted with the policy purpose of protecting users’ data from misuse and
unauthorized sharing to third parties. The GDPA also creates the national authority for data protection (ANPD) in order to monitor and
supervise entities.
The GPDA is broadly applicable to all sectors of the economy. Specifically for the financial sector, the BCB enacted provisions
applicable to regulated financial entities. These also apply to Pix participants. The BCB enacted specific rules for storing personal data
in relation to the Pix identifiers (email addresses, phone numbers, a Pix randomly generated IDs) which is linked to a deposit account.
The practical question is to what extent the transactional data linked to personal data from the Pix system is available to the BCB,
and what are the controls in place to reach a balance for the BCB to perform its supervisory functions and to protect data protection
from the public.
4.1.4. Consumer protection and financial literacy
In order to foster awareness and adoption of the Pix system, the BCB ran an extensive communication strategy with the private
sector, the society and media. Pix rollout required complementary policies to increase financial and digital literacy, particularly for
vulnerable groups such as the elderly who are less comfortable making digital payments.
Financial literacy efforts by the BCB started several years ago. The National Financial Inclusion Strategy launched in 2012
incorporated promoting financial education as a strategic goal (BCB, 2012). In 2019, the BCB issued Communiqué 34.201 which
provides for principles to promote financial literacy by regulated financial entities. This was the starting point to conduct a mapping
and assessment on how the financial sector was contributing with concrete actions with financial education. The results of the mapping
conducted in 2020 triggered the prioritization by the BCB of two strategic goals:
1. Embed financial education in the product design and business models of financial entities.
2. Conduct an impact assessment to measure financial education initiatives.
In 2021, the Relatorio de Ciudadania Financiera 2021 was issued by the BCB. Its purpose is to identify the progress and challenges of
the development of financial inclusion, education and consumer protection. The assessment identifies the main type of financial
consumer protection claims reported to the BCB and consumer authorities. Claims related to credit cards usage is one of the most
frequent categories reported by consumers, followed by fraud (BCB, 2021).
Concerning fraud, in 2023, the BCB developed a special complaint handling resolution mechanism exclusively for Pix. It is called
Mecanismo Especial de Devolução (MED) and its goal is to facilitate complaints redress in the event of fraud (BCB, 2023). The MED
requires the claimant to submit the complaint to the financial entity which shall block the funds from the recipient Pix account
immediately and assess the case within the timeframe of seven days. If assessment concludes that there was a fraudulent transaction,
the claimant should receive the funds back in 96 h. If not, the funds from the receiver Pix account will be unlocked. MED is not
exclusive for fraud, but it is also applicable to failed transactions due to the failure of the Pix system. The BCB is currently working with
the private sector in a revised version of the MED in order to trace fraudulent transactions and incorporating the possibility for
financial entities to block further Pix transfers of fraudulent funds to third party accounts (Stakeholders’ interviews, 2024).
In July 2024, the BCB also issued Resolution No 491/2024 that requires the registration of the device used to access Pix before the
financial entity and sets out the maximum amount allowed for the initiation of the Pix transactions. The policy goal of the resolution is
to mitigate security and fraud risks when initiating a payment using the Pix system.

4.2.1. Appropriate third-party custody structures
Globally, a common practice is to require financial entities to hold liquid assets in demand deposits at domestic commercial banks
or at a central bank account. This type of structures mitigate liquidity and credit risks since funds would be available at request for cash
withdrawals.
Requirements differ depending on the regulatory status of the Pix participant. Commercial banks are required to maintain a pool of
liquid funds at least equivalent to the aggregate balance of their clients’ deposits which a portion of funds is allowed to be invested in
high-quality and liquid assets such as short-term government debt. PSPs are required to hold customer funds directly at a reserve
account at the BCB. This eliminates the credit and liquidity risks, but it is less attractive because the return may be lower than a
commercial deposit.
4.2.2. Licensing and supervision of financial entities by regulators
Financial entities participate in the system directly and indirectly based on a rulebook developed by the BCB.
By September 2024, there was a total of 851 participants: 165 direct participants and 684 indirect participants in the system. Credit
cooperative is the type of financial entity that participate the most in the system (BCB, Pix Statistics, 2024). Direct participants include
commercial banks, multiple banks with commercial portfolio and the national public bank (Caixa Econômica). Indirect participants are
not regulated payment institutions. The National Treasury Secretariat is also a participant of Pix exclusively for fiscal-related financial
transactions.
Participants can act as transaction account providers. There are two forms under this category: mandatory participation for
regulated entities with more than five hundred thousand active customer accounts and payment entities authorized by the BCB subject
to certain specific requirements. They can also act as special settlement agent focusing only on providing settlement services to other
participants but not to end users. Payment initiators also participate in the Pix system to facilitate initiation of payment transactions
services, but they do not provide transaction accounts to end users. In order to act as payment initiator, entities are required to be
authorized by the BCB and are subject to proportional prudential regulation. An example of a payment initiator is GooglePay.
In November 2024, the BCB issued Resolution No 429 that adjusts the requirements for participating in the Pix system. From
January 1, 2025 only regulated entities would be able to offer Pix services. However, non-regulated entities would be entitled to
continue participating in the Pix system if they request for authorization within the term set out in the resolution. The rationale of the
issuance of the resolution is to mitigate security issues of some of the non-regulated PSPs (stakeholders’ interviews, 2024).
4.2.3. Privacy by design products
The private sector should design products that embed privacy and personal data protection based on local legislation and regu­
lations, and international standards.
The GDPA regulates the management, sharing and storage of personal data in Brazil. The BCB has enacted specific regulations on
how Pix identifiers should be managed by financial entities. Each institution develops its internal data protection programs and
policies to comply both with the GDPA and the Pix regulations (Stakeholders interviews, 2024).
A concern raised by the private sector in terms of the design of the Drex is that the current architecture allows for financial entities
to see all transactions from other financial entity on the blockchain. However, the participants of the Drex pilot are collaborating with
the BCB and the CVM to mitigate the privacy risk (BCB, 2025 and stakeholders interviews, 2024).

Circular No 3978/2020 from the BCB provides for the design and implementation of an AML/CFT program and internal control

The circular includes obligations to perform the appropriate due diligence based on the client profile, assess their risks, record

risk assessment mechanisms. This would depend on the role of each financial entity, its direct or indirect participation and the function

The Pix system was designed to ensure that users were able to transfer funds from accounts of their institutions to other,
notwithstanding their account is in a commercial, credit cooperative or digital wallet.

participate directly in the Pix system. This requirement ensured from the start of the design and then launch of Pix that users would be
able to transact with accounts in different institutions (BCB, Pix rulebook, Resolution 1/2020).

4.7.1. Rulebook and governance of the payment infrastructure
The BCB owns and manages the Pix system and has developed the rules that govern the provision of Pix services with a user-centric
approach, including on costs, use of data and other technical standards (BCB, Resolution No 1/2020). The BCB is sole responsible of the
Pix rulebook. This allows for the flexibility to adjust rules based on the evolvement and needs of the public and the market and ensures
that the Pix system achieves the policy goals of competition, innovation and inclusion set by the BCB (Lobo and Brandt, 2021).
The rulebook not only provides for the functions for participating directly or indirectly in the system, but also key rules issued in
agreement with the private sector through the Pix Forum. This includes minimum user experience requirements which aim to address
common flaws in the consumer experience through standards, including how the services should be made available to consumers by
financial entities. There is also a focus on APIs for sharing information among financial institutions and payment initiation services
providers to execute payments and provide for interoperability in the system. Network security is another relevant aspect. Participants
are required to flag suspicious activity and to assign transactions limits according to clients’ risk profile. In addition, the Pix rulebook

4.7.2. Access to central bank money
Direct participants in the Pix system only have access to central bank money, despite the interest of indirect participants as well

Direct participants have access to the SPI, the infrastructure that settles transactions through specific-purpose accounts held at the
BCB. The SPI settles transactions in real-time using a RTGS system which is available for settlement 24 h per day every day of the year,
although the BCB has the mandate to limit the settlement period due to any extraordinary situation (BCB, Circular No 4027/2020).
Liquidity is provided by funds held at the specific-purposes accounts at the BCB, Pix discount window for all non-business days and
hours and complementary, and clearing houses and clearing and settlement services may provide liquidity as well.
4.7.3. Interchange fees regulations
There are no interchange fees regulation for the Pix system.
Due to the policy goal of financial inclusion, P2P transactions are free of charge, according to the Pix rulebook. However, P2B, B2B
or B2P transactions have a low fee. PSPs also pay a low fee (BRL 0.01 per 10 transactions) to the BCB to sustain the maintenance of the
system. Pix participants are not allowed to charge fees or other type of remuneration among themselves for the provision of services.

4.8.1. Low-cost payments solutions with reasonable fee structures and long-term sustainable business models
Prior to the development of Pix, interchange fees were implemented with the policy goal of reducing merchants’ acceptance fees.
Also, before Pix, there was concentration in the acquiring market and closed-loop systems – an example that preceded Pix was the
launch of a big tech payment services which merchants’ fees amounted to 3.99 percent (BIS, Bulletin No 52, 2022).
Two relevant policy goals of the introduction of the Pix system were fostering competition and reducing the merchants’ acceptance
cost of digital payments. The average cost of Pix transactions is 0.33 percent in comparison to 1.13 percent for debit cards and 2.34
percent for credit cards (Saiyid, 2023).

5.1. Trust, security and privacy
5.1.1. What does the public demand?
Despite of the wide adoption of the Pix system by individuals and merchants, cash is the most trusted form of money, followed by
commercial bank money and e-money.
Security issues related to fraud, social engineering and unauthorized access to accounts is one of the main shortcomings of the Pix
system. 25 percent of the sample reported that they are not using Pix because of failed transactions which include security, fraud and
social engineering. Data breach is a further security issues that data shows as a challenge of using Pix. The main implication of bad
experiences using Pix is users transitioning back to cash. Examples of social engineering or fraud reported by users include Pix errado
which implies sending money to a mistaken recipient and not receiving the money back, receiving calls from scammers asking for a
money transfer pretending to be a relative or friend, consumers programming a merchant payment and then cancelling it. Data also
shows that individuals would be encouraged to use Pix when they feel it is safe to send or receive money, or pay bills using my phone.
This was also reflected in the focus groups stage where participants highlight that they sometimes do not feel safe paying with the
mobile phone in crowed places such as a bus terminal since they are afraid that their Pix balance is seen, and their mobile phones
stolen. Awareness is also important since one of the main reasons why individuals would use Pix is when they understand how it works.
Privacy is a further consideration that needs to be addressed to build trust on using Pix. 35 percent of the sample report that they
distrust giving merchant access to their personal data when making a purchase using Pix. This is also a user concern when making
physical payments, for example to street vendors since the user information is available and public to the micro merchant. There is also
a perception that the Drex could address this shortcoming. 60 percent of the sample believes that their personal data would be more
secured and protected with the Drex.
5.1.2. What elements does a regulatory framework need to have as an enabler of a FPS?
The BCB implemented the MED in order to mitigate security and fraud issues. Although complaints handling mechanism are re­
ported as robust by users and 79 percent of the sample highlighted that the time resolution for complaints is 24 h, the average time for a
scammer or golpistas to send customer funds obtained through a fraud to a third-party account is nine minutes. In addition, the money
flow of the fraud involves transferring the funds between 20 or 25 times between accounts. A common practice is the renting of ac­
counts (contas laranjas) for the only goal of transferring funds for fraud purposes (stakeholders’ interviews, 2024).
Due to this context, the BCB is working on revised version of the MED which will allow financial entities not only to block the first
account used for receiving the fraudulent funds but also trace and block all the accounts involved in the fraud. This functionality will be
available in the Pix system in 2026 (Interview with the BCB, 2024). Currently, the BCB also shares a registry of blocked accounts to
financial entities for transparency purposes. In addition, in July 2024, the BCB issued Resolution No 491 which requires the regis­
tration of devices used to access and use Pix. This regulatory measure would allow to identify the device used for the fraudulent
transaction and assist tracing the funds.
In terms of data protection, the BCB currently has access to personal data of Pix users, including complete name, domicile, if the Pix
user is using his email as a key, the email as well, among others. Feedback from the focus groups and survey data shows that privacy is
not one of the most relevant aspect or a priority when using Pix. Participants from the focus groups emphasized that the BCB having
access to their personal data is positive since they think that ‘they are doing things right’. However, a question that remains unan­
swered is whether the BCB should have access to personal data of Pix users since transactional data should be sufficient for supervision
and monetary policy purposes.
Licensing, supervision and custody requirements are important in building trust on using financial products. Awareness that users
are transacting with a regulated entity should be strengthen since only 63 percent of the sample knows that the financial entity that
offers their product is licensed and supervised by the BCB. Similarly, awareness on custody requirements should be strengthen. Only 38
percent of the sample knows that the money that they have in their account is in custody with a third party or at the central bank.
Consumer protection, specifically, disclosure and transparency requirements are a further aspect that the public is demanding to be
strengthen, specifically those related to fees. 54 percent of the sample reported that they have surprised about fees after accessing a
financial product. Resolution No 429 that adjusts the requirements for participating in the Pix system could also contribute to build
trust on the usage of Pix.

5.1.4. Would a well-designed Drex build trust on the usage of digital financial services and enhance security and privacy and mitigate fraud?
Cash is still the most trusted form of money in Brazil, however, Pix is the most accepted and preferred means of payment by
merchants. Considering the trust on cash, could a Drex deemed as digital cash address the trust issues that Pix is facing?
Evidence suggests that data breaches are still a shortcoming of Pix. Personal data is accessed and used by fraudsters for tricking

practices; however, this requires policy formulation rather than relying on technology advances. Similarly, it is not clear how the Drex
could contribute with building more effective and robust complaints handling processes since this is driven by regulations rather than
by technology developments.

5.2.1. What does the public demand?
i) Lack of connectivity
Together with trust and security challenges, lack of connectivity is one of the main the shortcomings of the Pix system.
37 percent of the sample reported poor mobile network and internet connectivity as the most relevant reason why they are not

need to pay with cash. In addition, there are cultural aspects attached to the lack of connectivity. In small municipalities individuals
tend to rely on Wi-Fi connectivity of a merchant and sometimes not having a data package affects their reputation. Adults would then
prefer to pay by cash so they do not disclose that they do not have connectivity (stakeholders interviews, 2024). 72 percent of the

6. Conclusion
This research assesses the benefits and challenges of Pix and the value proposition of a CBDC system in Brazil. Findings suggest that
security issues and lack of mobile and internet connectivity are the most significant shortcomings identified for further adoption of Pix.
Data shows that supervision and custody requirements are relevant for users to build trust on the usage of Pix by mitigating fraud
through social engineering, data breaches and unauthorized access to accounts. Awareness on how the Pix system and the Drex
platform work can assist as well. Privacy and consumer protection, specifically by strengthening the disclosure and transparency
requirements and the access by the BCB of personal data is also reported as a significant aspect to build trust.
In terms of lack of connectivity and an offline product, the question remains on the most recommended approach to minimize failed
transactions and expand the access to formal financial services. This could be mobilizing further investments to expand the mobile and
internet infrastructure. Alternatively, the private sector could design reliable offline products that can work independently of the
implementation of a CBDC system. Additional aspects to consider in the development of a FPS are real-time settlement, cost of
merchants’ payments, interoperability of systems and programmability.
The BCB should be also mindful of their role as issuer of the currency and supervisor of the financial sector. Their role should not
change due to the development of a FPS. A well-designed CBDC system could address the challenges of failed transactions due to
connectivity and security issues, particularly in rural areas, for micro and small merchants and farmers, if it is designed specifically
considering these shortcomings as priorities. The question remains whether this is the suggested approach, or the BCB should focus on
addressing these shortcomings by improving and developing the current Pix payments infrastructure.

Annex: Empirical methodology, demographic and economic outlook
Empirical methodology
The paper examines quantitative and qualitative data on the access and usage of Pix and the potential benefits Drex in Brazil.
It synthesizes selected data and insights gathered from a nationwide survey with a sample of one thousand individuals and mer­
chants and three focus groups in urban, peri‑urban and rural areas in Itatiba and Mococa in the State of Saõ Paulo in Brazil. The data
was collected from October to November 2024, with the support of the survey provider Provokers. The targeted group for the study is
unbanked, Pix users, individuals who owned a smartphone and women, farmers and merchants. The research is focused on retail and
not wholesale payments, an area that several central banks are also exploring.
The sample of the survey is representative nationwide. Self-administered quantitative surveys were conducted through an online
panel. The sample included men and women between 18 and 60 years old from different socioeconomic levels and educational levels
reflecting the diverse demographics of the country. The one thousand cases for the sample ensure that a robust sample size has been
achieved that is statistically significant and allows for generalization of the findings. Data collection was carried out throughout the
country, including cases in different regions. This geographic diversity assisted to capture a wide range of perspectives and behaviours
related to access and usage of financial services including people with and without a bank account, with and without a digital wallet,
and people using and not using Pix. Micro and small merchants from different sectors, accepting different types of payment methods,
were also included. The survey questionnaire was prepared in English and translated into and administered in Portuguese. The
questionnaire was designed to be used in self-administered interviews.
The quantitative and qualitative data obtained through the surveys and focus groups was complemented with interviews at the
central banks, national authorities, commercial banks, payment services providers, fintechs, money transfer services providers, and
CBDC technology providers. In addition, international organizations were interviewed including the World Bank (WB), International
Monetary Fund (IMF), Bank for International Settlements (BIS), Alliance for Financial Inclusion (AFI), United Nations Development
Programme (UNDP), the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) and the Latin American Development
Bank (CAF). The questionnaire for the survey consisted of 78 questions which are included in Annex B. The questionnaire of the focus
groups consisted of 32 questions which are included in Annex C, and lastly the questionnaire for central banks and the Pix services
providers consisted of 20 questions which are included in Annex D.
The quantitative and qualitative data should serve as representative insights to understand better how vulnerable groups interact
with money and digital financial services for payments.
Demographic and economic outlook
Brazil is the largest economy in Latin America and the tenth largest economy globally with a GDP that amounts to 2.2 trillion
(2023) and a population of 216 million people (2023) (World Bank, Open Data).
Brazilian population is digital savvy (99 percent of mobile cellular subscriptions and 84 percent of individuals using the internet),
experiencing a significant increase in urban residency (88 percent), and largely entrepreneurial (World Bank, Open Data). In terms of

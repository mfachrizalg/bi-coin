This study examines how the issuance of a central bank digital currency (CBDC) affects consumers' payment
instrument choices, asset allocation decisions, and the profitability of financial intermediaries. Specifically, we
advance a theoretical model in which introducing an interest-bearing CBDC expands market participants' pay­
ment and investment options but also poses a potential threat to financial intermediaries if it raises their
borrowing costs significantly. The existence of a network externality further complicates the analysis by affecting
consumers' choice of payment mechanism. Our results suggest that the issuance of a CBDC is a double-edged
sword for an economy. Although it provides additional payment and investment options for market partici­
pants, it increases competition and weakens financial intermediaries' margins.

1. Introduction
Monetary policymakers, practitioners, and academics are growing
increasingly interested in the economic impacts of the introduction of a
central bank digital currency (CBDC). A CBDC is a digital currency issued
by a nation's central bank. As it is legal tender, a CBDC has the properties
of cash, but it may also have the properties of digital bank deposits if it
bears interest (Boar & Wehrli, 2021). As legal tender, a CBDC's value is
guaranteed by the central bank, and it can be used as a medium of ex­
change in electronic payment systems (Bordo & Levin, 2017). Discussions
of the effects of introducing a CBDC emerge following the development of
blockchain technology and cryptocurrency markets. These discussions, in
turn, have led to research on the differences in the natures and price
behaviors of traditional fiat currencies and newly emerging currencies
such as CBDCs and other cryptocurrencies (Bhaskar, Hunjra, Bansal, &
Pandey, 2022; Elsayed & Sousa, 2022; Hsu, Sheu, & Yoon, 2021; Kim,
Ryu, & Webb, 2023; Pele, Wesselhofft, Hardle, Kolossiatis, & Yatracos,
2021; Yang, Wang, & Gao, 2022).

Central to the impact of a CBDC is the assumption that central banks
can control the money supply. This assumption raises questions
regarding what money is and who controls the money supply. Laffer
(1970) argues that trade credit can be viewed as privately issued money.
Much trade credit, such as the unexpended credit lines on credit cards, is
digital. The notion that money should be broadly defined is old. Indeed,
as Miller (1984) notes, Keynes explicitly includes interest-bearing time
deposits at banks in his definition of money in his famous 1930 Treatise
on Money, and Miller (1984) argues, “One implication of including bank
time deposits is that Keynes recognized that money can pay interest,
even though he talked at times as though it did not (Keynes, 1937).” The
issuance of an interest-bearing CBDC means that individuals can effec­
tively hold savings at the central bank directly in the form of deposits
instead of holding savings in a private financial instrument, such as a
bank time deposit (Niepelt, 2020; Son & Ryu, 2022).1 As an interest rate
may be imposed on a CBDC, numerous policymakers are evaluating the
effectiveness of monetary policy in the context of the emergence of a
new interest-bearing currency (Assenmacher & Korgstrup, 2021; Auer

Our study makes the following contributions to the CBDC literature.
First, we evaluate the effectiveness of monetary policy by defining the
characteristics of cash, a CBDC, and bank deposits. We reflect the
motivation for the demand for money by considering the implications of
network externalities, banking costs, and the relative interest rates on
bank deposits and the CBDC. With respect to these features, we note the
important role that the CBDC plays as both a means of payment and a
store of value. Additionally, we include a network externality in the
model to analyze the effects of monetary policy on consumers' paymentpreferred networks. Monetary policy shocks may have greater impacts
on payment methods with lower demand owing to network external­
ities. Through this analysis, we provide implications for the appropriate
CBDC rate and the optimal interbank offered rate (IOR).
We present a framework wherein a CBDC competes with oligopo­
listic banks by combining several existing microfoundation models. We
endogenously derive the ripple effect of each impact on the economy
through private banks' positions by focusing on two monetary policy
options and controlling for the IOR and the CBDC rate. Within a rela­
tively simple framework, we derive implications for financial stability.
We investigate the extent to which the CBDC replaces deposits when the
CBDC rate changes relative to the interest rate on bank deposits. We
identify the conditions under which CBDCs coexist with private bank
deposits. Our intuitive explanations for monetary policies related to the
IOR, the CBDC rate, and reserve requirements can assist policymakers in
understanding the impacts of issuing a CBDC and setting or changing the
interest rate on a CBDC and the implications of those actions for mon­
etary policy. Therefore, this study can help evaluate the effectiveness of
monetary policy.

Some studies explore the consequences of issuing a CBDC on finan­
cial stability and the crowding-out effect. Using the monopoly banking

strates that setting an appropriate CBDC rate may not affect commercial

Uhlig (2021) discuss banks' stability as private financial market bro­

increasing financial intermediaries' credit risk. Castren, Kavonius, and
Rancan (2022) simulate the issuance of a CBDC and describe a situation
in which the deposits of households and non-financial corporations
move to the central bank. In this case, commercial banks lack the
financial resources for loans, which can sequentially impact other
financial institutions, thereby damaging financial stability. Nyffenegger
(2023) analyzes the impact of a CBDC issuance on commercial banks by
distinguishing between the CBDC's function as a saving vehicle and its
use as a payment medium. He finds that when investment opportunities
are so poor that banks do not have excess reserves, a CBDC has no effect
on profitability in the lending sector. Additionally, he empirically
demonstrates that when depositors change their preferences from
interest-bearing bank deposits to the CBDC, banks' lending volumes
decrease. Chiu, Davoodalhosseini, Jiang, and Zhu (2023) examine the
impact of CBDC issuances on bank profitability when several commer­
cial banks compete. Their findings suggest that the introduction of a
CBDC facilitates banks' competitiveness, raises deposit rates, and ex­
pands commercial banks' financial brokerage activities. Kim and Kwon
(2023) investigate the effect of a CBDC on the credit supply and financial
stability using a monetary general equilibrium model, extending the
bank-run model of Champ, Smith, and Williamson (1996). They show
that an increase in the quantity of the CBDC can increase the probability
of a bank panic by reducing the deposit supply, leading to growth in the
nominal interest rate and a decrease in commercial banks' reser­
ve–deposit ratios. However, the introduction of a CBDC can improve
financial stability if the central bank lends all deposits in the CBDC ac­
count to commercial banks, thereby increasing the supply of private

2. Related literature

analyzing the impacts of government monetary policy in depth and
accounting for the network externality, we provide a more realistic
discussion of its effects. In addition to considering the network exter­
nality, we also account for the function of a CBDC as a governmentissued digital currency. Interest-bearing CBDCs emerged as an alterna­
tive to private bank deposits. Both are digital assets. In practice, private
bank deposits are akin to a digital currency if owners use them as a
payment mechanism by instructing their banks to execute payments
from their deposits. However, because a CBDC is legal tender, the se­
curity of legal tender and the reserve requirement policy that the central
bank can implement are also relevant. The latter, which Andolfatto
(2021) analyzes, refers to a situation in which the government or central
bank implements a policy that restricts individuals' currency choices
with respect to the legal currency (we discuss this property in Section 5).
The CBDC's status as legal tender means that it is more creditworthy
than bank deposits are; thus, it is more secure. To account for this
property, we assume that a CBDC has a lower disutility cost and, thus, a
lower credit risk than bank deposits have. Therefore, banks compete
with interest-bearing CBDCs by setting their deposit rates above the
CBDC rates, and consumers face a tradeoff between security and returns.
The sensitivity of consumers to banking costs and the importance of
network externalities in decision-making is considered in other settings
in the economics literature. Borzekowski and Kiser (2008) empirically
analyze the substitutability of consumer payment instruments by
applying a characteristic-based rank-order logit model. They find that
credit card networks can hold market power. In other words, although
merchants' acceptance of credit cards as a payment method increases
their costs per unit of currency, they accept credit cards as a payment
method because the additional costs are offset by the additional revenue
owing to the network effect of consumers using credit cards and (often)
spending more. Bolt and Humphrey (2007) analyze the euro area mar­
ket, which is a single-currency economy, and argue that the network
effects of electronic payments will increasingly promote the use of
network-motivated payment instruments.
Additionally, some studies analyze the costs of using banking ser­
vices (Stavins, 2018). Borzekowski, Kiser and Ahmed (2008) use con­
sumer survey data to analyze Americans' sensitivity to debit card costs
and usage. They consider the transaction fees charged by banks as a
major variable and find that consumers are very sensitive to debit card
transaction fees. Some studies focus on cash discounting (i.e., offering a
lower price for cash payments), which can be understood as a different
measure of banking costs. Barron, Staten, and Umbeck (1992) compare
and analyze the effects before and after cash discounting in the retail
gasoline market and find a significant relationship with the use of cash.
In other words, cash discounts are incentives for consumers to use cash
in their transactions. Just as businesses can offer discounts on using
cash, the use of debit or credit cards can be discouraged by imposing fees
on their use. Bolt, Jonker, and Van Renselaar (2010) analyze changes in
consumers' payment methods when retailers can charge consumers extra
for debit card payments. They find that consumers replace their debit
cards with cash. Briglevics and Shy (2014) investigate the costeffectiveness of merchants when it is legally possible to induce con­
sumers to make less costly payments. They find that steering consumers
toward cash via simple price discounts can work.

Andolfatto (2021) and Agur et al. (2022) conduct the two most
closely related studies to our study. They discuss frameworks for the
effect of a CBDC on commercial bank profitability through an over­
lapping generations model. Andolfatto (2021) and Agur et al. (2022)
analyze consumers' payment instrument choices and the effects of
monetary policy shocks in consideration of network externalities,
respectively. Andolfatto (2021) analyzes the situation in which an
interest-bearing CBDC competes with bank deposits. By expanding the
monopoly banking industry models of Klein (1971) and Monti (1972),
he establishes a theoretical foundation for commercial banks' profitmaximizing strategies and, thus, a competitive equilibrium. He finds
that a monopolistic bank adjusts its deposit rate according to the CBDC
rate set by the government and that the monopolistic bank's profitability
depends on the IOR–CBDC spread. Several studies analyze the interest
rates and currency preferences imposed on the CBDC, but Andolfatto
(2021) discussion is more realistic in that it directly considers the private
bank's position endogenously and reflects the impact of the govern­
ment's monetary policy on bank profitability. Moreover, he considers
consumers' investment motivation toward a CBDC in a model in which
deposits compete with the CBDC in terms of returns. We extend the work
of Andolfatto (2021) by developing a model whereby an interest-bearing
CBDC competes with deposits at private banks and uses it to investigate
financial stability. We reflect on some characteristics of a CBDC that the
existing literature has neglected—namely, banking costs and network
externality effects. One limitation of Andolfatto (2021) is that the CBDC
competes with bank deposits only in terms of interest rates. He assumes
that the CBDC and bank deposits incur the same disutility (i.e., banking
costs) as other digital assets. Consequently, the consumer selects a
payment instrument by considering only the interest rates on the CBDC
and bank deposits. However, a CBDC is a legal tender issued by the
government, meaning that it is more creditworthy and, thus, less risky
than bank deposits. In other words, a CBDC carries no risk of default.
Thus, it is reasonable to assume that the disutility cost of a CBDC is less
than that of deposits. Additionally, we expand upon and discuss con­
sumers' endogenous currency decision strategies by including the
network externality effect identified by Agur et al. (2022).4
As in our study, Agur et al. (2022) focus on the design of a CBDC as a
payment instrument. They define the payment instruments which have
interest-bearing properties, and consider the tradeoffs between ano­
nymity and security, and the network externalities as their properties. In
this context, they analyze agents' preferred payment instruments under
an optimal policy according to each attribute's design parameter and
conclude that the issuance of a CBDC can reduce demand for deposits in
both perfectly competitive and Cournot banking industries.5 Several
studies discuss important economic implications of CBDC design, but
few comprehensively consider CBDCs' crucial payment and store-ofvalue properties. Specifically, the network externality, which is
considered a preference factor in payment instrument decisions, is rarely
discussed in existing CBDC-related studies despite its importance. By

5.1. Deposit and lending rates
Below, we shed light on how a change in the deposit (lending) rate
impacts the volumes of both deposits and loans and the lending (deposit)
rate. Without a reserve requirement policy, the lending and deposit rates
do not affect each other. This property is because even if a bank has
insufficient deposits to finance a loan, it can cover the loan through bank
reserves; thus, the deposit and lending rates are determined by their
market equilibria. However, if the liquidity constraint is binding, the
movements of the two interest rates are related, thereby changing the
banker's asset composition.
First, when the real lending rate rises, the demand for loans and the
equilibrium loan volume both fall. Hence, the need to seek funding
decreases, and bankers demand fewer bank reserves (or deposits).
Regardless of their funding sources, bankers must reduce their amounts
of deposits; thus, the deposit rate decreases. Symmetrically, when the
lending rate decreases, demand for loans increases, which increases the
equilibrium loan volume. Hence, to increase the deposit volume under
this constraint, the bankers increase the deposit rate.

and bankers want to increase their loan volumes as much as the con­

Thus, the crowding-out effect of changes in aggregate bank deposits

in turn, affect entrepreneurs' profit structures. We consider these points,
derive the conditions under which a network externality occurs, and
illustrate a special case in which the choice of money provides no utility.
Additionally, we discuss the impact of introducing a CBDC on bank
profitability by focusing on banks' roles in intermediating between de­

In the following section, we derive policy implications from the
above relationships under a reserve requirement constraint. First, when
the CBDC rate increases, the equilibrium deposit volume decreases,
according to eq. (40). Bankers must reduce their lending activity; thus,
the lending rate rises. Likewise, when the government reduces the CBDC
rate, the equilibrium deposit volume increases according to eq. (44), and
bankers reduce their lending volumes. Consequently, the lending rate
increases. We provide an interpretation of the change in the deposit rate
in each case. In our framework, bankers' profits depend on the spread, or
difference, between revenues from loans and the costs of deposits. As
deposit demanders, bankers want to reduce their deposit volumes when
the deposit rate is high and increase their deposit volumes when the
deposit rate is low, assuming the lending rate remains constant. As the
CBDC rate increases, the deposit supply encountered by bankers shifts to
the left. In equilibrium, the deposit rate increases, and the dollar volume
of deposits decreases. Intuitively, from a worker's point of view, a higher
CBDC rate reduces the deposit supply because the CBDC is a more
attractive payment instrument. From a banker's perspective, this situa­
tion is an exogenous supply shock in which the government increases the
CBDC rate in the deposit market. Our conclusion differs from that of
Andolfatto (2021). In his analysis, because a monopoly bank competes
with the government to make deposits more attractive than the CBDC,
an increase in the CBDC rate causes increases in the deposit rate, the
deposit volume, and the volume of lending activity, leading to a fall in
the lending rate.

6. Conclusion
The introduction of CBDCs has generated strong interest among
market participants, policymakers, and academics. This study proposes
a model that examines the impact of a CBDC issuance in a simple
economy with a government and central bank, workers, entrepreneurs,
and bankers (i.e., financial intermediaries). In our model, a utilitymaximizing worker selects among three payment instru­
ments—namely, a physical currency; private bank deposits, which are
commonly linked to debit cards; and a newly issued CBDC. Within this
basic framework, the government determines the CBDC rate and IOR as
monetary policies affecting the behavior of each agent. First, workers
benefit from the expansion of payment instrument options when the
CBDC is issued. However, as the types of available currency increase, the
risk of potential negative network externalities grows. Subsequently,
because the CBDC is a potential substitute for private bank deposits,
bankers may be pressured to raise interest rates to strengthen competi­
tiveness, and their profitability may deteriorate owing to a decrease in
deposits to lend to entrepreneurs. Entrepreneurs may also be affected if
the banks' profitability deteriorates. If the government sets a reserve
requirement policy, changes in the deposit and lending rates are linked.

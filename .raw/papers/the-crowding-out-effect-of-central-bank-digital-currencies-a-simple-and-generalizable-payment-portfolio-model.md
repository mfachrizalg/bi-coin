crowding-out effect of Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDC) on bank deposits. Specifically,

1. Introduction
With the emergence of cryptocurrencies and the advances of information technologies, Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDC)
have raised increasing attentions of both policymakers and researchers. While there is a growing body of literature on private digital
currencies such as Bitcoin and stablecoin (Baur and Hoang, 2021; Kyriazis and Prassa, 2019; Makarov and Schoar, 2020), the research
on CBDC is relatively scarce, the majority of which comes from international organizations and central banks (Auer et al., 2020;
Kumhof and Noone, 2018).
The CBDC is a digital form of central bank money that is different from physical cash or digital central bank reserves/settlement
accounts. Based on the accessibility, CBDC can be further categorized into wholesale CBDC and general purpose CBDC (CPMI-MC,
2018). Given that wholesale CBDC do not change the basic infrastructure of the financial system (Bindseil, 2020), we focus on general
purpose CBDC in our model.
The primary goal of general purpose CBDC is to replace physical cash to improve the safety and efficiency of the payment system
(Barotini and Holden, 2019). Note that, in practice, many policymakers seem to underestimate the potential crowding-out effect of
CBDC on deposits over the course of its substitution for cash.1 However, the argument that CBDC would simply replace physical cash
without crowding out deposits is not justified. The general purpose CBDC may cause the structural and cyclical bank disintermediation
since it provided competition for commercial bank deposits as well and the crowding-out effect of CBDC on deposits may disrupt the
credit provision of commercial banks and threaten the financial stability (see Bindseil (2020) for a detailed review).

Despite its theoretical and practical importance, existing studies on CBDC focus primarily on the consequences of CBDC for interest
rates, tax evasion, or anonymity (Fernández-Villaverde et al., 2021). In contrast, the potential impact of CBDC on bank deposits has not
been thoroughly examined.2 To fill the gap, we construct a simple and generalizable payment portfolio model to illustrate the potential
crowding-out effect of CBDC on bank deposits. In our model, economic agents choose among cash, deposits, and CBDC to maximize the
utility while satisfying their payment needs. Under the assumptions of four utility-contributing features of payment assets and the CES
utility function, we derive the optimal payment portfolio for households before and after the issuance of CBDC. The model shows that,
when households’ preferences for legal tender and digital payments dominate the preferences for anonymity and interest bearing, they
have demand for CBDC and reduce the holdings of cash and deposits at the same time. Given the occurrence of the crowding-out effect,
we further examine the extend to which CBDC impact cash and deposits.
Our paper contributes to existing literature in several aspects. First, while several recent studies intend to examine the interactions
between CBDC and bank deposits (Brunnermeier and Niepelt, 2019; Fernández-Villaverde et al., 2021; Keister and Sanches, 2019), we
differentiate them by characterizing different features of payment assets and analyzing how economic agents react to those features.
Our model can help to explain the sources and mechanisms of the crowding-out effect. Second, we extend the “money in the utility”
literature (Barnett et al., 1992; Belongia and Ireland, 2019; Chetty, 1969; Gauger and Schroeter, 1990) by exploring how payment
assets add to utility differently through their specific features and examining how CBDC interact with the existing near-money assets.
Third, our model shows great generalization and can be easily extended to capture other payment innovations such as Bitcoin and
stablecoin.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the payment portfolio model and analyzes the potential crowdingout effect of CBDC on cash and deposits. Section 3 concludes our study and illustrates the generalization of our model.
2. The payment portfolio model
2.1. The payment assets and the utility maximization process
We focus on the payment assets that provide liquidity for daily transactions. In the classic Cash in Advance (CIA) and Money in
Utility (MIU) models, the payment asset refers to cash because physical currency has traditionally been perceived as the main payment
instrument. With the development of payment systems, the primary payment instruments have changed to deposits, credit lines, and
checks, all of which are mainly provided by commercial banks. The gradual decline in cash usage and the emergence of private
cryptocurrencies have prompted central banks to explore the possibility of the issuance of CBDC as a supplement to physical cash.
Given these, we assume that households choose among cash, deposits, and CBDC to construct their payment portfolio for daily use.

Combing the above propositions, we could see that the demand for CBDC and its crowding-out effect on cash and deposits would
occur simultaneously. That is, as long as households choose to hold CBDC, they will reduce the holdings of cash and deposits at the
same time. It may be confusing at first glance, but it is in accordance with economic intuition for the following two reasons. First, given
that there are three payment assets, the rise of CBDC would necessarily lead to the fall of the other two payment assets. Second, CBDC
combines parts of the features from cash (legal tender) and deposits (digital payment), it is natural to reduce the demand for cash and
deposits with the usage of CBDC.
These two propositions have significant policy implications, given that many central banks are considering the issuance of CBDC as
a supplement to cash with the assumption that it has no material effect on deposits (e.g., Norges-Bank, 2019, 2018; Sveriges Riksbank,
2018, 2017). It is worth noting that moving from deposits to CBDC would threaten the core function of commercial banks—maturity
transformation—which transforms short-term deposits into long-term loans. When households withdraw their bank deposits and
embrace CBDC, it is akin to a bank run and would impair banks’ credit provision for the real economy. In this sense, CBDC’s issuance is
a double-edged sword: the benefit of replacing physical cash for a more efficient payment system comes at the cost of disrupting the
deposit base for commercial banks.

3. Conclusions
We provide a simple model to examine the potential crowding-out effect of CBDC on bank deposits. In our setting, economic agents
choose among cash, deposits, and CBDC, to maximize the utility while satisfying their basic payment need. We find that, when
households’ preferences for legal tender and digital payments dominate their preferences for interest bearing and anonymity (αLαD >
αRαA), they have demand for CBDC and reduce the holdings of cash and deposits at the same time. Given the crucial role of commercial
banks in channeling of funds, the issuance of CBDC may result in financial disintermediation and credit crunch.
The model can help us better understand the conclusions in prior studies. Fernández-Villaverde et al. (2021) show that, during a
panic, the issuance of CBDC would cause depositors to withdraw all deposits away from commercial banks. We can mimic this extreme
case by assuming that CBDC bears the same interest as deposits. Thus CBDC would always generate higher marginal utility than
deposits, resulting in a full crowding-out effect on deposits. Keister and Sanches (2019) model cash-like CBDC and deposit-like CBDC
and arrive at different conclusions for different types of CBDC. We can reproduce those conclusions by assuming that cash-like CBDC
share the same features with cash and deposit-like CBDC share the same features with deposits.
While our model is simple, it shows great flexibility and can be generalized to more complicated scenarios. For instance, we can
incorporate Bitcoin and stablecoin into our model by including another utility-contributing feature: decentralization. In addition, we

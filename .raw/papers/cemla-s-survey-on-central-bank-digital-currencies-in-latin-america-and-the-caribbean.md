ABSTRACT
In late 2021 and early 2022, CEMLA conducted a thorough survey involving 12 central banks to
analyze key aspects related to the potential implementation of a Central Bank Digital Currency
(CBDC). The findings revealed prevalent issues and objectives regarding digital payments in
the region, as well as promising avenues for central bank involvement. However, it highlighted
the challenges of creating a universal CBDC framework due to significant disparities among
neighboring countries. Nonetheless, the collaborative research approach promises valuable
insights for future efforts. To protect participant anonymity, each country’s responses were
anonymized with randomly assigned letters.
The survey extensively examined awareness and understanding of CBDCs across diverse
stakeholders in Latin American nations, including the general public, corporations, and financial
institutions. These insights could shape educational campaigns and communication strategies,
enabling policymakers and central banks to effectively share information about CBDCs.
Moreover, this research effort sheds light on public perceptions, expectations, and concerns
about CBDCs in Latin America. By exploring various dimensions such as awareness, interest,
adoption potential, concerns, preferences, and regulatory considerations, the survey offers a
comprehensive understanding of the CBDC landscape in the region. This holistic approach
provides valuable insights for policymakers, central banks, and stakeholders to navigate the
complexities of CBDC implementation effectively and responsibly.

1. Introduction
Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) have emerged as a focal point of global interest, igniting discussions among policymakers, academics, and financial stakeholders. Within the landscape of Latin American countries, conducting a survey on CBDCs serves
a multifaceted purpose, pivoting on the specific objectives and interests of the conducting organization or researchers.
In the ever-evolving realm of financial innovation, CBDCs have become a subject of considerable expectation and debate. Latin
America, renowned for its diverse economies and complex financial landscapes, stands at a crossroads, facing the prospect of
integrating CBDCs into its monetary systems. This survey seeks to critically examine the multifaceted implications of CBDC adoption
in Latin America, addressing the promises and perils that lie ahead.

2. Brief summary of general findings
The emergence of Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) represents a digitized form of national fiat currency, under the
governance and issuance authority of central banks. Unlike decentralized cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, CBDCs are regulated and
issued by central banking authorities. Engineered to enhance secure and efficient payment systems, CBDCs offer potential benefits
including increased financial inclusivity, decreased transaction costs, and expanded monetary policy options. They exist in wholesale
forms for interbank settlements and retail versions accessible for public transactions. Growing interest in CBDCs is driven by rising
digital payment adoption, the prominence of private cryptocurrencies, and efforts to modernize financial systems. Many central
banks globally are actively exploring or implementing CBDC initiatives, indicating significant interest in this area.
In Latin America, several countries and institutions have embarked on pilot projects and initiatives to explore the potential of
digital assets and Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs), see Fig. 1. Uruguay initiated a pilot project called e-Peso to explore the
feasibility of adopting a CBDC. Led by the Central Bank of Uruguay (BCU), the e-Peso pilot aimed to assess technical, economic, and
regulatory aspects of implementing a CBDC, focusing on testing digital currency for retail transactions, peer-to-peer transfers, and
interoperability with existing payment systems. Similarly, the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank (ECCB) conducted a pilot program
for CBDC adoption called DCash. Collaborating with Bitt Inc., the ECCB aimed to provide a secure and efficient digital payment
solution for the Eastern Caribbean region, testing DCash for retail purchases, peer-to-peer transfers, and bill payments. Meanwhile,
El Salvador made headlines in June 2021 by becoming the first country to adopt Bitcoin as legal tender. The Bitcoin Law aimed
to promote financial inclusion, facilitate international remittances, and stimulate economic growth, integrating Bitcoin into El
Salvador’s financial infrastructure, including the launch of the Chivo wallet. Despite facing praise for innovation and empowerment
and criticism for volatility and regulatory challenges, El Salvador continued its Bitcoin adoption efforts, exemplified by projects like
Bitcoin City. These initiatives reflect a growing interest in digital assets and CBDCs across Latin America, with countries exploring

3. Analysis of each question
3.1. Country generalities
3.1.1. Geography
The descriptions of these 12 Latin American countries reveal a diverse range of geographic and demographic features that
significantly influence their financial and cash management landscapes. These characteristics include varying sizes, from small
countries to one of the largest in the world, geographic challenges such as mountainous terrain, islands, and remote areas,
disparities in rural and urban populations, with percentages ranging from 24% to 61.7% living in urban areas, a broad spectrum
of municipalities, provinces, and regions affecting the distribution and management of physical cash, limited access to public and
private services in rural and remote areas, which can increase the costs of financial service provision, regional disparities that
hinder access to financial services, causing differences between regions, rugged surfaces, high mountain ranges, and volcanoes,
creating obstacles for financial services and driving up costs. The varying characteristics of these countries underline the unique
challenges and opportunities faced by each in the context of CBDCs and cash management. Understanding these factors is crucial
for policymakers and financial institutions seeking to navigate the complex financial landscapes of Latin America.
3.1.2. Political
Across these countries, several key political characteristics related to CBDC decisions are evident. All 12 countries operate
as democracies, emphasizing the importance of political representation and participation in governance. The central banks are
institutions independent of the central government, responsible for core mandates such as currency issuance, free from political
intervention. Multiple stakeholders within these democratic nations hold the potential to influence CBDC issuance decisions.
Interest in CBDC decisions is evident within these democratic settings. The countries maintain stable political systems with
strong democracies, characterized by multiparty elections and a separation of powers among the executive, legislative, and judicial
branches. The Central Bank serves as the sole issuer of the sovereign currency, establishing it as the legal tender. These democracies
are open to financial innovations, indicating a willingness to explore new financial technologies.
Positive relations between the Legislative Assembly and other state powers promote cooperation and understanding, facilitating
decision-making. Addressing matters related to CBDC issuance within the financial system is recognized as a complex undertaking.
Central Banks have not actively engaged with members of Congress or the Executive Branch regarding their interest in CBDCs.
Constitutional provisions underscore the countries as sovereign, democratic, independent republics with representative governments.
Central banks are decentralized entities, potentially granting them significant authority over CBDC decisions. These political
characteristics provide a comprehensive overview of the democratic nature, governance structures, and key considerations that
may influence the path of CBDC adoption in these Latin American countries.
3.1.3. Economic
Economic activities in these countries are primarily related to agriculture, trade, manufacturing, and services, with a focus on
commodity-based exports. A significant informal sector exists, representing a substantial portion of the workforce, ranging from
approximately 22% to 83% of the total employment (see Fig. 2). Most economic activity is concentrated in urban areas, with the
services sector being a major contributor, accounting for nearly 70% of the total economy (International Labour Organization, 2023).
Some countries possess abundant natural resources, including oceanic oil fields, diversified industrial sectors, strong private
sectors, and active participation in international commodities markets. Informal labor markets account for a considerable share of
the workforce, ranging from around 20.8% of GDP to as high as 60.1% in the labor market (World Bank, 2023).
These countries exhibit stable macroeconomic conditions, including low levels of indebtedness, stable inflation rates, and steady
economic growth over the past two decades. The adoption of electronic or digital payment methods is incipient, with a significant
reliance on cash. Limited financial inclusion and low financial education are noted.
Despite a minor increase due to the pandemic, the use of electronic or digital payment forms is not widespread, partly due to the
need for appropriate technology and internet connectivity. The acquisition of technology and connectivity services is challenging,
especially for those with limited economic resources. Some countries have experienced substantial economic growth since 2010,
with major activities including construction, local manufacturing, commerce, and hospitality sectors. Micro, small, and medium
enterprises represent a large proportion of firms and the workforce. Remittances also play a significant role in the economy, as
shown in Fig. 3.
The informality issue is not the primary focus of CBDC projects in these countries, with varied goals for their implementation. These economic characteristics offer insights into the diverse economic landscapes within these Latin American countries,
highlighting key factors that may influence the adoption and use of CBDCs and the broader financial ecosystem.
3.1.4. Technological
In these Latin American countries, there is a notable presence of high connectivity and mobile phone penetration. However, this
technological landscape is characterized by a significant urban-rural divide, with robust connectivity primarily in urban areas and
limited access in remote regions. The degree of internet connection varies, with some countries showing high numbers of connections
in major cities but lacking coverage in distant locations, which may pose challenges for the implementation of a CBDC.
Several countries have a surplus of mobile telephone lines, even one of them surpassing their total population, with a substantial
portion of the population having access to 4G mobile broadband. Fiber optics technology provides internet access to around 84%
of municipalities, contributing to reliable internet infrastructure.

3.1.5. Sovereign currency
In these 12 Latin American countries, the degree of sovereignty of the national currency is generally high, and it is not considered
a decisive factor in the potential implementation of a CBDC. While some high-value transactions, such as real estate operations, are
often settled in US dollars, there is a historical context of macroeconomic volatility leading to a process of assets substitution. This
history raises concerns about the potential risk of ‘‘digital dollarization’’ with the introduction of CBDCs.
Legal tender is strictly issued by the Central Bank in most countries, and the official currency is the legal tender. Dollarization
varies across the countries, with some adopting the US dollar as legal tender or having a fully dollarized economy. A particular case
is El Salvador, where Bitcoin circulates as legal tender alongside the official currency.
In one country, There is low currency substitution compared to other Latin American countries in some nations, indicating high
confidence in the domestic currency and the credibility of the Central Bank. However, the level of dollarization is significant in
certain countries, with a considerable portion of deposits denominated in dollars.
In summary, these countries exhibit a range of currency characteristics, from high sovereignty and limited dollarization to fully
dollarized economies. The potential impact of introducing CBDCs is influenced by the historical context of currency usage and the
degree of confidence in the domestic currency and Central Bank credibility.
3.2. What other factors do you think will benefit or affect the implementation of a CBDC in your country?
In these 12 Latin American countries, several factors will either benefit or affect the adoption of CBDCs:
• Affect
1. Drawbacks of CBDC Implementation:
Challenges include limited access to digital technology, changes in legislation, implementation costs, potential bank runs,
among others.
2. Barriers to Widespread Adoption:
Challenges include low access to banking services, informality, widespread use of cash, and the population’s limited access
to financial knowledge and products in the population. Financial education and cultural aspects also play a role in hindering
widespread CBDC adoption.
3. Competitive Threats:
Competition with the banking sector and potential lack of public adoption are identified as major threats to CBDC projects.
4. Cultural Preferences for Cash:
Cultural preferences in certain sectors favoring cash transactions may slow down CBDC adoption.
5. Infrastructure and Security Concerns:
Power outages, cyberattacks, fraud and internet access and connectivity problems, especially in remote areas, pose challenges
for a CBDC adoption. Low levels of digital financial education are additional concerns.
6. Lack of Financial Literacy/Trust in CBDC:
Poses a barrier as a significant portion of the population may lack understanding or trust in CBDC.
7. Country-Specific Considerations:
Varying levels of progress in digital payments influence the central bank’s decision on CBDC implementation. Technology
limitations, competition with banks, and the population’s technological literacy are important considerations.
8. Incentives to Avoid Formal Payment Means:
Encourages the use of informal means of payment, potentially hindering CBDC adoption.
9. Technology and Regulatory Challenges:
The CBDC may lack applications in the current payment system, but integrating DeFi tools could promote innovation.
However, regulatory challenges need careful consideration.
• Benefit
1. Benefits of CBDC Adoption:
Innovation, security, efficiency, and decreased use of cash. It can also detect illegal activities such as money laundering.
2. Potential Benefits of CBDC Implementation:
It can lower the cost of money management, reduce the need for physical travel to access financial services, and expedite
payment processes.
3. Promotes financial inclusion:
If properly designed a CDBC can support the modernization of low-value payment systems improving the efficiency of the
payment systems in terms of costs, and boosting financial inclusion, giving the population that is not banked access to efficient
payment schemes, discouraging the use of physical money and promoting competition.
4. Monetary Policy Tools and Improved Payment Services:
Adoption of CBDCs could provide new monetary policy tools, promote competition, enhance payment services, and reduce
costs for transfers and payments.

3.4. Which other authority share the responsibility of the banking and financial system?
Central banks, ministries of economy, and various superintendencies collaboratively oversee and regulate the banking and
financial systems. Responsibilities include ensuring the stability, integrity, and proper functioning of institutions such as banks,
insurance companies, and credit cooperatives. Specific entities, such as the Financial Information Unit, focus on areas like anti-money
laundering. The regulatory framework involves multiple boards and committees issuing regulations to maintain system integrity and
stability. Overall, these measures aim to promote financial stability, protect consumer rights, and prevent illicit financial activities.
3.5. Is the Central Bank autonomous?
The autonomy of the Central Bank is emphasized in various ways across nations. In some cases, it is described as an autarchic
organization, while others define it as a legal entity with administrative, budgetary, and technical independence. Specific laws
may establish and outline the autonomy of the Central Bank, with a primary focus on achieving price stability. Organic laws and
regulations in different countries affirm the full autonomy of the Central Bank, describing it as a decentralized entity of the state
or an autonomous public institution of a technical nature. The General Law of Public Administration and the Law of the Central
Bank often reinforce the autonomy of the institution, highlighting functional and administrative independence governed by its
own constitutive laws and regulations. In some cases, a broader framework, such as the Monetary and Financial Administration,
grants autonomy to institutions like the Monetary Board, Central Bank, and Superintendence of Banks, allowing them functional,
organizational, and budgetary independence for fulfilling specified functions under the Monetary and Financial Law.
a. How much autonomy does the central bank have in the design, development and implementation of a CBDC?
The autonomy of central banks varies across nations. Some central banks possess complete and exclusive legal authority for
currency issuance, while others have partial autonomy, necessitating coordination and approval from the national government.
Certain countries grant absolute autonomy to central banks for CBDC development, while others face legal barriers, requiring
congressional regulations for issuance. Collaborative efforts with national financial entities are observed in electronic payment
systems in some cases. Full autonomy in CBDC design and research is present in certain countries, contingent on legislative
amendments for implementation. Meanwhile, legislative limitations in other countries only allow research on CBDCs but not their
issuance. The autonomy of the Monetary and Financial Administration, encompassing the Monetary Board and the Central Bank,
is emphasized for fulfilling designated functions. Reforms to existing laws are often seen as prerequisites for promoting CBDC
development, with some countries expressing the need for consultations with the public and stakeholders during the eventual CBDC
launch. Overall, the extent of autonomy in CBDC initiatives reflects a diverse regulatory landscape across Latin American countries.

3.6. What is the current design of your monetary system and what are the problems with it?
In Country 1, the Central Bank issues legal tender, relying on banks and financial intermediaries for secondary money creation
and lending activities. Country 2 emphasizes an organized central banking system with no major reported problems. Country 3’s
Financial System, managed by a National Monetary Committee, includes the autonomous Central Bank responsible for monetary
policy and financial stability. In Country 4, the legal tender is represented by physical cash issued by the central bank, following
an inflation targeting scheme. Country 5’s Central Bank operates independently, overseeing monetary policy execution under the
Ministry of Economy and Public Finance. Country 6 describes a conventional monetary system with emission monopoly and financial
intermediation within legal reserve limits. Country 7 reports a dollarized monetary system, regulated and overseen by the Central
Bank. Country 8 outlines a monetary system guided by constitutional and legal frameworks, utilizing various policy instruments.
Country 9’s Central Bank manages a high-value payment system, while retail payment services are provided by other entities.
Country 10 follows an Inflation Targeting regime, with limited financial intermediation entities. Country 11 utilizes the U.S. dollar
as legal tender, with the Central Bank supplying notes and coins, facing challenges in working capital quantification and fractional
currency provision. Overall, each country exhibits distinct features and considerations in its monetary system design and operation.
3.7. Where does the central bank sit with respect to issuance of sovereign currency and the country’s government?
In Latin American countries, the Central Bank universally holds the exclusive responsibility as the sole issuer of the sovereign
currency. This authority is outlined in various legal provisions, such as monetary laws, constitutions, and organic laws governing
the Central Bank. The Central Bank’s role encompasses the issuance of coins and banknotes, ensuring legal tender status, circulation,
and withdrawal within the country’s territory. However, in some instances, legal restrictions may limit the Central Bank’s ability to
conduct inorganic issuance, particularly in the context of CBDC schemes, where backing by a specific currency, such as the United
States dollar, may be required. Overall, the Central Bank’s monopoly on currency issuance is a consistent feature across these Latin
American nations.
3.8. How does the central bank interface with banks and other regulated financial entities?
In Latin American countries, the Central Bank plays a crucial role in regulating and overseeing the financial system, including

mention having their own convertible currency, while others, like the CB team in one country, expect the Digital Real to serve as

G coin to fiat currency. In certain instances, the need for further research is acknowledged to define the native asset.
b. Asset supply management
The responses from different Latin American countries regarding the configuration of their asset supply management indicate
variations. Some mention a limited deterministic approach, which allows determining the maximum number of coins issued and
delimits the scope of use and growth. One country specifies an unlimited deterministic model. Another country notes that the supply
is managed by the central bank, and the specific approach is described as limited deterministic. Overall, there is a preference for
deterministic models with limitations on the supply of the digital currency.

The responses from different Latin American countries regarding the configuration of tokenization vary. One country mentions
the term ‘‘Tokenization’’ without specifying further details. Another country indicates that more research is required to define CBDC
tokenization in the country. There is an expectation from one country for a widespread use of tokenization, encompassing private
stable-coins and other tradable assets. Another response mentions tokenization with 3rd party add-ons. In contrast, a different
country states that there is currently no tokenization, expressing uncertainty about the added value of tokens in their context.

3.23. In terms of identity management, what do you prefer?
a. Access layer and control layer
The responses from various Latin American countries regarding identity management in the context of CBDC emphasize a
preference for permissioned private blockchains. This choice is motivated by the desire for centrally-coordinated changes, control
enforcement, and enhanced cybersecurity. The decision on public or private access to the CBDC depends on the scope of the
implemented system. The countries highlight the importance of read and write, as well as consensus management rights, being
granted by a centralized organization in a permissioned private blockchain. Some countries also mention the possibility of using
both permissioned public and private blockchains, allowing for flexibility in the design and security considerations. The prototype
in one country utilizes a private permissioned blockchain, with potential openness for public verification of blockchain information.
b. Identity layer
The responses from different Latin American countries regarding identity management in the context of CBDC reveal a preference
for a KYC/AML (Know Your Customer/Anti-Money Laundering) approach. This ensures compliance with national regulations and
facilitates the validation of participants and their data from authoritative sources. Some countries emphasize the importance of
maintaining user privacy and actions within the legal requirements of the country. Additionally, there is a mention of a hybrid
approach, combining KYC/AML for certain transactions or users with the allowance for anonymous transactions in other use
cases, particularly for low-value transactions. The identity layer is recognized as having different onboarding options with varying
limitations on amounts or transaction numbers based on the guarantees provided by each method.

closed-source language specifically for in-house developments within the Central Bank.
b. Charging and rewarding system
One country emphasizes the absence of fees, attributing it to the private nature of the solution in a permissioned network and
the presence of an identity layer. Another country suggests that more research is required for a definitive definition of the charging
and rewarding system for their CBDC.

4. Final remarks
In exploring the landscape of Central Bank Digital Currencies across 12 diverse Latin American countries, this survey has
illuminated critical insights into the region’s attitudes and advancements regarding CBDC adoption. Across the spectrum of economic
development and financial infrastructures, a clear trend emerges: growing interest and exploration of CBDCs as potential catalysts
for financial inclusion, efficiency, and sovereignty.
Evident from this comprehensive survey is the varied approach among nations, reflecting nuanced priorities, technological
readiness, and regulatory landscapes. While some countries are advancing steadily, laying foundations for CBDC development
aligned with their economic needs and technological capacities, others remain in exploration phases, considering the multifaceted
implications before committing to implementation.
Common threads emerge in the perceived benefits of CBDCs, notably in enhancing financial inclusion, fostering innovation in
payment systems, and bolstering monetary sovereignty. However, challenges loom large, encompassing technological readiness,
regulatory frameworks, interoperability concerns, and public acceptance.
As we navigate this dynamic landscape, collaboration among governments, financial institutions, and technology partners
becomes imperative. Sharing insights, best practices, and addressing common challenges collectively will pave the way for a
smoother and more inclusive integration of CBDCs into the Latin American financial ecosystem.
The road ahead for CBDC implementation in Latin America is multifaceted, requiring a delicate balance between innovation,
regulation, and collaboration. This survey serves as a starting point, shedding light on the region’s aspirations, challenges, and
opportunities in embracing the future of digital currencies.

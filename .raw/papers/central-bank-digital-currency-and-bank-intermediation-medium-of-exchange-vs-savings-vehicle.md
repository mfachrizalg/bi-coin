ABSTRACT
This paper analyzes the effects of a retail central bank digital currency (CBDC) on bank
intermediation within a general equilibrium model and provides a quantitative assessment. In
the model, banks offer both transaction and savings deposits. This allows to study how a CBDC’s
impact varies based on its usage as a medium of exchange or as a savings vehicle. When a CBDC
is used as a savings vehicle that competes with savings deposits, the level of disintermediation
almost doubles compared to a scenario where the CBDC is used solely for transactions and
competes only with transaction deposits.

1. Introduction
The concept of a retail central bank digital currency (CBDC) has gathered significant attention. Numerous countries are actively
researching the subject and are conducting pilot projects. In some instances, such as Nigeria and certain Caribbean states, a
retail CBDC has already been launched. One major concern surrounding the introduction of a retail CBDC is its potential impact
on financial stability and the disintermediation of the banking sector. A (BIS, 2021) report highlights that ‘‘the potential for the
introduction of a CBDC to affect financial stability risks arises primarily from a significant substitution away from private money’’.
Understanding how the introduction of a CBDC influences bank intermediation and other macroeconomic variables is crucial. The
literature generally agrees that a CBDC would act as a substitute for bank deposits, which are a primary and inexpensive source of
funding for banks. A crowding out of deposits could lead to diminished bank intermediation, resulting in reduced credit availability
and higher credit costs.
An area that warrants further exploration is the effect of a CBDC on banks, depending on its utilization as a medium of exchange
or as a savings vehicle. In the former scenario, the CBDC would primarily compete with short-term transaction deposits, while in
the latter scenario, it would also compete with longer-term savings deposits. This distinction could lead to varying impacts on bank
lending. Panetta (2022), a member of the ECB’s Executive Board, expresses a similar perspective, suggesting that the central bank
could employ varying remuneration on the CBDC to ensure that ‘‘it is a means of payment that is as attractive as cash’’ but ‘‘to reduce
the attractiveness of the CBDC as a store of value’’.
The novel contribution of this paper is to examine the distinct roles of a CBDC and analyze its effects on banks based on its use
as either a medium of exchange or a savings vehicle. This paper addresses the following research questions: What are the impacts

To address the research questions, I utilize a general equilibrium model that integrates the (Lagos and Wright, 2005) framework
with an overlapping generations model based on (Wallace, 1980). The resulting model environment is similar to the framework
presented in Altermatt (2019). This model accounts for agents’ needs for both payments and savings. Within this framework, agents
can choose to hold private money with banks or use public money, such as cash. The agents’ choice of assets depends on the interest
rate offered as well as their personal valuation of various non-interest rate characteristics, referred to as the asset’s quality. These
characteristics, though not explicitly modeled, include factors such as ease of transaction, additional services, or privacy. Banks fund
themselves through transaction and savings deposits and extend loans to entrepreneurs, holding market power in both the deposit
and loan markets. Additionally, they are subject to a minimum reserve requirement constraint.
The introduction of a CBDC provides agents with a new option for holding a payment or savings vehicle. If agents value this new
option, then its initial rollout leads to a surge in CBDC usage, accompanied by a simultaneous outflow from deposits and cash. This
increased competition in the deposit markets prompts banks to offer higher deposit rates. Regarding bank lending, I find that a CBDC
has no impact when banks hold excess reserves with the central bank. However, when liquidity is scarce, the introduction of a CBDC
negatively affects bank lending. This impact is mitigated by the higher deposit interest rates, which counteract the crowding-out
effect on both the intensive and extensive margins. This is particularly relevant when a CBDC is used solely for payments or savings.
If used only for payments, transaction deposits decline on the extensive margin. However, both transaction and savings deposits
increase on the intensive margin due to higher interest rates.
To quantify the effects on bank lending, I calibrate the theoretical model using data from the US economy between 1987 and
2006, a period chosen to investigate a scenario without excess reserves. First, I analyze the initial rollout of a non-interest bearing
CBDC with the same quality as cash. If the CBDC provides value only as a medium of exchange, leading to a crowding out of
transaction deposits, bank lending decreases by 0.6%. In contrast, if the CBDC provides value solely as a savings vehicle, impacting
savings deposits, the negative effect on lending increases to 1.0%. This nearly doubling of the impact highlights the importance of
distinguishing between different types of deposits. In a scenario where people use the CBDC for both payments and savings, resulting
in an outflow from both types of deposits, bank lending experiences a reduction of 1.6%.
Furthermore, I investigate the impact on bank intermediation when the CBDC is remunerated or possesses a higher quality than
cash, assuming the CBDC is used for both payments and savings. The findings indicate that increasing the quality of the CBDC to
approximately 20% closer to that of deposits reduces bank lending by about 1% compared to when its quality is equal to that of
cash. Enhancing the quality of the CBDC results in a slight decrease in total welfare. If the CBDC pays an interest rate according
to the Friedman rule, bank lending only marginally decreases by 0.07%. Welfare can increase slightly if the interest rate is not
excessively high.
These results suggest that if a central bank introduces a CBDC, it should be less concerned about the negative effects of paying
positive interest and more cautious about making the CBDC too attractive through non-interest rate characteristics, such as superior
services. Additionally, since a CBDC used solely for payments causes less disintermediation, the central bank could focus on providing
strong payment services while limiting its use as a savings vehicle. This could be achieved by setting limits to ensure that the CBDC
primarily meets payment needs.
The discourse surrounding the introduction of digital public money dates back to Tobin (1987). In recent years, interest in this
topic has surged significantly. My paper contributes to the growing body of literature exploring the implications of CBDC on bank
intermediation, and is most closely related to the works of Chiu et al. (2023) and Whited et al. (2023). Chiu et al. (2023) show that
a CBDC can increase bank lending by heightening competition in the deposit market, which leads to higher deposit interest rates.
However, if the interest rate on the CBDC becomes excessively high, banks’ interest margins shrink to zero, resulting in a decrease
in bank lending.
My paper differs from theirs in two key aspects. First, I introduce two types of deposits with endogenously determined interest
rates. Second, I present a distinct demand system where CBDC, cash, and deposits coexist despite unequal returns, with non-interest
rate characteristics also playing a role. While Whited et al. (2023) employ a similar demand system, they emphasize banks’ ability
to use wholesale funding and utilize a partial equilibrium model. In contrast, I focus on the roles of payment and savings within a
general equilibrium model.
My paper also distinguishes itself from other works in the literature. Keister and Sanches (2023) feature a perfectly competitive
banking sector and analyze bank lending depending on whether the CBDC is similar to cash, deposits, or both. A cash-like CBDC
has no impact on bank lending, while both deposit-like and universal CBDCs lead to a decrease in bank lending. Andolfatto (2021)
models a monopoly banking sector using an overlapping generations model and finds that bank lending remains unaffected by the
introduction of a CBDC if banks can borrow reserves from the central bank at the interest rate on reserves. Similarly, Brunnermeier
and Niepelt (2019) establish an equivalence result suggesting that, under specific conditions, a CBDC has no effect on bank lending
because banks can borrow from the central bank in response to deposit outflows to CBDC.
Assenmacher et al. (2021) develop a general equilibrium model with frictions that require entrepreneurs to borrow both bank
deposits and CBDC. They find that bank lending can increase if there is a high interest rate spread between CBDC and the deposit
rate, a tight collateral constraint, and high substitutability between the two assets. Agur et al. (2022) explore the impact of CBDC
on the use of cash and deposits, where demand is influenced by varying preferences over anonymity and security. They find that if
CBDC closely substitutes bank deposits, it leads to a decrease in bank lending.
Williamson (2022) develops a model with competitive banks and free entry, examining the welfare and policy implications
of different CBDC designs. Niepelt (2024) finds that optimal interest rates on CBDC and reserves differ and that a CBDC may raise

If 𝜆 > 0, the reserve requirement constraint is binding. This makes transaction deposit funding more costly for a bank because it
must hold a fraction of these deposits as reserves, whereas savings deposits can be fully invested into loans. Therefore, if the quality
of the two deposit types is similar and the reserve requirement is sufficiently high, the interest rate on savings deposits will be
higher to attract more funds through this deposit type. A higher quality of transaction deposits 𝑞𝑑𝑚 will allow a bank to attract the
same level of funding at a lower interest rate, resulting in 𝑅𝑑𝑠 > 𝑅𝑑𝑚 . Conversely, if the quality of savings deposits is significantly
higher than that of transaction deposits (𝑞𝑑𝑠 ≫ 𝑞𝑑𝑚 ), a bank can reduce the interest rate on savings deposits since it attracts funds
through the higher quality.
Whether the interest rate on deposits is higher or lower than the interest rate on reserves is ambiguous due to the counteracting
effects of two prevailing frictions. On one hand, the reserve requirement imposes a cost on the bank, leading to higher deposit
interest rates. On the other hand, banks’ market power tends to lower deposit rates. Therefore, with a high reserve requirement and
low market power, deposit rates could exceed the rate on reserves (𝑅𝑑𝜏 > 𝑅𝜁 ); conversely, with low reserve requirements and high
market power, deposit rates would be below the rate on reserves. Without market power (𝐵 → ∞), it holds that 𝑅𝜁 < 𝑅𝑑𝑚 < 𝑅𝑑𝑠 .
If 𝜆 = 0, the reserve requirement constraint is loose and banks hold voluntary reserves. In this case, the only friction is market
power. Thus, if the quality of the deposits is similar, similar deposit interest rates will result, provided that the quality and interest
rate on cash are the same for both payment and savings uses. Otherwise, the market shares of transaction and savings deposits
would adjust, influencing the outcome. As before, if the quality of one deposit type improves, the corresponding interest rate is
lowered, shifting the relative size of the rates. Since the only prevailing friction is market power, deposit interest rates are pushed
below the interest rate on reserves (𝑅𝑑𝜏 < 𝑅𝜁 ). Without market power (𝐵 → ∞), it holds that 𝑅𝜁 = 𝑅𝑑𝑚 = 𝑅𝑑𝑠 .

An increase in the CBDC’s quality or interest rate heightens competition for bank deposits, reducing their market shares and
thereby diminishing the bankers’ market power. As a result, deposit interest rates rise.
Overall, deposit funding will decrease if a CBDC is introduced or if its quality or interest rate is raised. However, this outflow
is partially offset by higher deposit interest rates. On one hand, the higher rates boost deposit demand on the intensive margin. On
the other hand, the decline on the extensive margin is mitigated as some agents switch back to deposits. Despite this, the overall
effect on the extensive margin remains a shift away from deposits, as evidenced by the decline in their market shares.
The effects on interest rates and quantities vary depending on whether the CBDC is used solely as a medium of exchange, solely
as a savings vehicle, or for both purposes, as outlined in Proposition 5. A CBDC used exclusively as a payment vehicle impacts only
transaction deposits and has no effect on savings deposits.
Regardless of the CBDC’s design, the loan quantity and interest rate remain constant if the reserve requirement constraint is
non-binding. A bank holding excess reserves indicates that it has already reached the profit-maximizing loan amount and has no
incentive to adjust it in response to a crowding out of deposits. Instead, the bank will reduce its excess reserve holdings to compensate
for the outflow of deposits.

3.2. A binding reserve requirement constraint
The results for a binding minimum reserve requirement constraint (𝜆 > 0) are summarized in Proposition 6. I examine the impact
of changes in the CBDC’s quality and interest rate based on its use as a medium of exchange (𝑞𝑒𝑚 , 𝑅𝑒𝑚 ), as a savings vehicle (𝑞𝑒𝑠 , 𝑅𝑒𝑠 )
or both (𝑞𝑒 , 𝑅𝑒 ).
Proposition 6. If the reserve requirement constraint is binding (𝜆 > 0), an increase in the quality or interest rate of a CBDC that competes
as a payment vehicle, a savings vehicle, or both will (i) increase the interest rate on both transaction and savings deposits (𝑅𝑑𝑚 , 𝑅𝑑𝑠 ), (ii)
decrease aggregate deposit funding (𝑑𝑚 + 𝑑𝑠 ), (iii) reduce the overall deposit market share (𝛼𝑑 ), and (iv) decrease loan supply 𝓁 while
increasing the loan interest rate 𝑅𝓁 .
Proof. A proof is available in Appendix A.6.

■

A binding reserve requirement constraint indicates that bankers would have an incentive to issue more loans, but they are
restricted by the constraint. As increased demand for CBDC leads to a crowding out of deposits, deposit funding declines, limiting
the bankers’ ability to provide loans to entrepreneurs.
Another key difference from the non-binding constraint case is that, even if the CBDC is used only as a medium of exchange,
bankers now respond in both the payments and savings deposit markets, leading to higher interest rates for both transaction and
savings deposits. With the constraint binding, it becomes optimal to attract funds not only through transaction deposits but also
through savings deposits.
Total deposit demand decreases on the extensive margin but increases on the intensive margin, leading to an overall negative but
moderated impact on bank lending. As 𝓁 decreases, the interest rate 𝑅𝓁 on loans rises because the marginal product of investment
increases with lower overall loan issuance.
Table 1 summarizes the results. It illustrates the impact of an increase in the quality of a CBDC 𝑞𝑒 when used as a payment
vehicle (𝑞𝑒𝑚 ), a savings vehicle (𝑞𝑒𝑠 ) or both (𝑞𝑒 ). The results are equivalent for an increase in the CBDC interest rate 𝑅𝑒 or for an
initial introduction of a CBDC, which can also be modeled by an increase of 𝑞𝑒 . The table presents the outcomes for both a loose
reserve requirement constraint (𝜆 = 0) and a binding one (𝜆 > 0).
Lastly, I briefly consider the impact of adjusting a CBDC’s quality, 𝑞𝑒 , and interest rate, 𝑅𝑒 , in opposite directions. As outlined in
Propositions 5 and 6, increasing 𝑞𝑒 reduces deposit funding, while lowering 𝑅𝑒 increases it. Therefore, for an already implemented
CBDC, a higher quality combined with a sufficiently lower interest rate could raise deposit funding by making the CBDC less
attractive, thereby triggering a shift in market share to deposits.
However, this dynamic differs for the initial introduction of a CBDC. Since the CBDC’s market share is initially zero, lowering
its interest rate cannot trigger a shift in market share from the CBDC to deposits. Furthermore, a lower CBDC rate reduces deposit
rates, which diminishes deposit demand on the intensive margin. Consequently, the initial introduction of a CBDC cannot increase
deposit funding, even with a negative nominal interest rate.
4. Calibration
To quantify the results, I calibrate the model using data from the U.S. economy between 1987 and 2006.6 This time period is
chosen to account for a binding reserve requirement. As demonstrated in Section 3, the introduction of a CBDC in an excess reserve
regime has no impact on bank lending. However, the objective of this paper is to determine the magnitude of the effects on banks
when a crowding out of deposits cannot be offset by a reduction in the reserve position. Therefore, it is essential to conduct the
calibration during a period with a binding constraint.

requirement ratio (𝜔) are aligned with their data counterparts. In my model, the interest rate on reserves acts as the policy rate.

Lastly, I examine the welfare effects. Consumer welfare increases significantly due to higher deposit interest rates. However,

entrepreneurial profits are substantial, so their utility loss outweighs the gains for consumers, assuming equal welfare weights for
all agents.
Bank profits decrease when a CBDC is used for payments (−1.2%) or for both payments and savings (−1.1%). However, bank
profits increase slightly (+0.2%) when the CBDC competes only with savings deposits. This surprising outcome holds for the
calibrated parameters but is not generalizable across the entire parameter space. All else being equal, a high concavity parameter
(𝜂) would decrease bank profits if the CBDC functions solely as a savings vehicle.
Under the calibrated parameters, bank profits increase because banks rely more on short-term funding at a lower cost, and the
loan rate rises enough to offset the higher deposit rates. However, this profit increase occurs only if the entire banking sector shifts
to the new equilibrium. If a single bank unilaterally deviates by issuing more transaction deposits but fewer savings deposits and
loans, the sector-wide interest rates would not adjust sufficiently to increase profits for that individual bank.

suggest that a central bank should be less concerned about negative effects of paying interest on CBDC but should exercise caution
in designing a CBDC that is overly attractive in terms of non-interest rate characteristics.
5. Conclusion
This study investigates the impact of introducing a central bank digital currency (CBDC) on bank intermediation within a general
equilibrium model that accounts for agents’ money needs for both payments and savings. This approach enables an analysis of how
the effects of a CBDC vary depending on whether it is used solely as a medium of exchange or also as a savings instrument.
A CBDC, which can be interest-bearing, offers people a new option for holding a payment or savings vehicle, with certain noninterest rate characteristics that they value. Upon its initial introduction, there is a surge in CBDC holdings and a corresponding
decline in deposit and cash holdings. Since banks have market power in both the deposit and loan markets, the outflow of deposits
leads to higher deposit interest rates, which help to mitigate the crowding out effect. I find that a CBDC has no impact on bank
lending if banks hold excess reserves. However, if reserves are scarce, bank lending contracts.
In a calibration, I find that the negative impact on bank lending nearly doubles when a CBDC is used as a savings instrument
and almost triples when it is employed for both payment and savings purposes, compared to being used solely as a medium of
exchange. An interest-bearing CBDC can improve welfare if its interest rate is kept moderate, with only minor effects on bank
lending. However, if a central bank designs a CBDC with highly attractive non-interest rate characteristics, the impact on bank
lending can be significantly greater.

Appendix C. Data
I use data from four distinct sources for the calibration process: (1) FRED for standard time series such as GDP, inflation, and
cash holdings data; (2) FDIC call reports for bank-level data on interest rates and total deposits; (3) FDIC Summary of Deposits for
county-level deposit data; and (4) U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) for county-level GDP data.
I provide a detailed discussion of each data source and the specific data derived from them below. To compute steady-state
values, I use data spanning the period from 1987 to 2006. For estimating the utility parameter 𝜎, I use a shorter time frame due to
data limitations, which is discussed in more detail below.
FRED
From FRED, I collect data on total GDP (𝑌 ), the federal funds rate (𝑅𝜁 ), cash holdings, and 1-year inflation expectations (see
Table C.1). Expected inflation is used to calculate real interest rates. For cash holdings, I assume that all $100 bills are used for
savings, while smaller denominations are used for payments. I use FRED’s denomination-specific series to compute 𝑐𝑚 and 𝑐𝑠 . Since
these series are only available from 1993 onward, I address this limitation by combining data from another series related to the
currency component of M1, which dates back to 1975, with information from Feige (2012, Figure 2) that shows the percentage of
currency by denomination since 1964.
Given that a significant portion of U.S. currency is held abroad, I focus my analysis on currency held within the U.S. by
incorporating estimates from Judson (2024). These estimates vary over time and are subject to a high degree of uncertainty, largely
depending on the estimation method used. Judson (2024) notes that ‘‘the range of estimates is from about 40 percent to about 75 percent
for all denominations and from about 60 percent to about 70 percent for $100s.’’ Therefore, I use an average estimate of 60 percent for
currency holdings abroad in the calibration exercise.
FDIC Call Reports
To obtain historical data on deposits and loans and to calculate the corresponding interest rates, I use FDIC call report data. This
dataset is available quarterly and includes balance sheets and income statements at the bank level. Historical quarterly data for all
banks can be downloaded in bulk from the following link: https://www.fdic.gov/foia/ris/. The specific time series used are listed
in Table C.2.
To obtain data on overall transaction deposits, total savings deposits, and aggregate loans, I aggregate the values from all banks
within each quarter. The savings deposits category includes both regular savings deposits and money market deposit accounts
(MMDAs). For loans, I focus on total loans within domestic offices. The aggregated time series for deposits and cash holdings are
shown in Fig. C.1. The steady-state values used in the calibration for transaction deposits (𝑑𝑚 ), savings deposits (𝑑𝑠 ), and loans (𝓁)
are computed by averaging the corresponding time series.
To calculate the corresponding interest rates, I use data on interest expenses for transaction and savings deposits, as well as
interest income from loans. These interest expenses and earnings are reported in a year-to-date format. Therefore, I first recalculate
quarterly expenses and earnings for all banks over the specified time period. To determine the quarterly sector-wide deposit interest
rates, I aggregate the quarterly expenses for all banks and divide by the aggregate deposits.
To calculate the loan interest rate, I first determine interest rates at the bank level by dividing the loan income by the loan
issuance. Following the approach of Chiu et al. (2023), I consider only the bottom 25% of bank-level interest rates, as I do not

Appendix D. Robustness of the calibration
To assess the robustness of the calibration, I conduct a parameter sensitivity analysis and present the model outcomes for varying
levels of the reserve requirement 𝜔.

D.1. Sensitivity of the parameters
I analyze how key parameters influence the targeted moments (listed in Table 4), as well as the untargeted moments (listed in
Table 5). This approach is akin to the methodology utilized by Elenev et al. (2021), which is based on Andrews et al. (2017). The
sensitivity analysis is shown in Fig. D.4, where I display the elasticities of the target moments in blue and the validation moments
in red, following a 1% increase in each key parameter. I report the six parameters used in the joint calibration, the utility function
parameter 𝜎, the reserve requirement 𝜔, and the quality of deposits 𝑞𝑑𝑚 and 𝑞𝑑𝑠 .
A higher share of 𝜃 𝑚 -types (𝛾) increases the fraction of payment vehicle held (𝑑𝑚 ∕𝑑𝑠 and 𝑐𝑚 ∕𝑐𝑠 ). An increase in the production
function parameters 𝐴 or 𝜂 has a significant impact on interest rate spreads. More productive firms boost returns on loans, which in
turn raises the loan interest rate (increasing the loan spread). This also leads to higher deposit interest rates (decreasing the deposit
spreads). The greater elasticity of the savings spread is due to the fact that the spread is smaller in equilibrium compared to that of
transaction deposits. The elasticity of the two interest rates is very similar.
Increased competitiveness in the banking sector affects prevailing interest rates. Greater competition in the loan market (𝐵𝓁 )
primarily lowers the loan interest rate, while elevated competition in the transaction (𝐵𝑑𝑚 ) or savings (𝐵𝑑𝑠 ) deposit market raises
the respective deposit interest rates and narrows the corresponding spreads. In contrast, the interest rate in the deposit market where
competitiveness remains unchanged decreases (the spread increases), as banks capitalize on their relatively higher market power.
A higher utility parameter 𝜎 increases asset demand, which in turn allows banks to set lower interest rates, leading to an increase
in deposit spreads. The elasticity of an increase in the reserve requirement 𝜔 on the targeted parameters is small. It has the most
significant impact on the savings deposit rate, as the need to hold more reserves prompts banks to rely more on savings deposits
for funding, thereby increasing the savings deposit interest rate (narrowing the spread). Finally, raising the quality of transaction
deposits (𝑞𝑑𝑚 ) or savings deposits (𝑞𝑑𝑠 ) boosts their demand, reducing the ratio of cash to deposits.

D.2. Different reserve requirement
There is reason for concern that the calibrated reserve requirement of 𝜔 = 0.1 might be too high, as banks could employ
accounting methods to reduce required reserve holdings. To address this, I replicate Table 6 and include a scenario where the
reserve requirement is reduced to 𝜔 = 0.025. Specifically, I recalibrate the model by adjusting the reserve requirement parameter
𝜔. I then match the data moments as outlined in Section 4 and conduct the policy experiment where the quality and interest rate
of CBDC are equal to those of cash (𝑞𝑒 = 𝑞𝑐 , 𝑅𝑒 = 𝑅𝑐 ). The results are shown in Table D.4, with the equilibrium values that differ
in the new steady state reported in brackets in the Equilibrium column.
The negative impact on bank lending is similar in magnitude for both scenarios. However, the effects on deposits differ slightly.
When people use CBDC for both payments and savings, aggregate transaction deposits decrease slightly less, while savings deposits
decrease slightly more when 𝜔 = 0.025 Overall, the effects are very similar in both cases. These small differences reinforce the low
elasticities of 𝜔 reported in Appendix D.1.

to have the same quality as cash (𝑞𝑒 = 𝑞𝑐 ) and pays the same interest rate (𝑅𝑒 = 𝑅𝑐 ).

A lower reserve requirement reduces aggregate deposits while increasing bank lending, as banks need to allocate less funding
to reserves. As agents hold fewer deposits, they increase their holdings of cash and CBDC instead. The higher loan issuance leads
to a lower loan interest rate and a narrower spread. At the same time, deposit rates decrease, resulting in a wider spread. While
consumers are worse off due to the lower deposit rates, overall welfare increases slightly due to a rise in the entrepreneurs’ profits.
Surprisingly, bank profits increase when the reserve requirement is higher. This occurs because a sector-wide shift toward holding
more reserves and issuing fewer loans drives up the loan interest rate enough to boost profits. However, if a single bank were to
make this move alone, it would not raise the equilibrium interest rate sufficiently to increase profits. This outcome is highly sensitive
to the parameters of the production function. All else being equal, a high 𝜂 would reverse this result, causing bank profits to decrease
if the reserve requirement is raised.
Appendix E. Model timeline
In Fig. E.6, I provide a more intuitive description of the model environment. Time is discrete and continues forever (… , 𝑡 −
1, 𝑡, 𝑡 + 1, …). Agents discount between time periods with the discount factor 𝛽. Within each time period, there are two subperiods: a
centralized market (CM) and a decentralized market (DM), reflecting the framework outlined in Lagos and Wright (2005). The CM
is a centralized, Walrasian market where all agents engage in trading. The DM, on the other hand, is a decentralized market where
only bilateral trades occur.
Moreover, the model incorporates generational dynamics similar to the overlapping generations model. Generation 𝑡 is born at
the beginning of time period 𝑡 in the CM and dies at the end of the CM in period 𝑡 + 1. The economy includes two distinct goods:
the CM good, denoted as 𝑥, and the DM good, represented as 𝑦. Both goods are perishable, meaning they cannot be stored and used
for consumption in subsequent subperiods or periods.
The model includes four distinct agent types, as depicted in Fig. E.6. There are consumers who are divided into early consumers
and late consumers. Both types of consumers can work and produce the good 𝑥 when young in the CM, but they wish to consume
at a later stage in life. Specifically, the early consumer wants to consume one subperiod later in the DM, while the late consumer
intends to consume in the CM in period 𝑡 + 1.
Given the perishable nature of the goods, consumers need alternative means to defer consumption to later periods. Consequently,
the early consumer works and sells good 𝑥, receiving payment in the form of a payment vehicle (cash or liquid transaction deposits).
In contrast, the late consumer acquires a savings vehicle (cash or illiquid savings deposits). The early consumer can then use the
payment vehicle to acquire goods in the DM, while the late consumer utilizes the savings vehicle to purchase goods in the CM in
period 𝑡 + 1.

Additionally, there are producers who live indefinitely. They can only work in the DM and produce good 𝑦, but they want to
consume in the CM. Since 𝑦 is not storable, producers demand a payment vehicle. They obtain this payment vehicle in the DM from
the early consumer in exchange for selling good 𝑦.
Entrepreneurs live for one period and possess an investment opportunity, but they lack an initial endowment and are unable
to work. Consequently, they rely on obtaining a loan from a bank. When a loan is granted, the bank deposits the corresponding
amount into the entrepreneur’s account. The entrepreneur then uses these funds to purchase good 𝑥 in the CM and invest it. After
one period, returns are generated. The entrepreneur sells a portion of the returns in the CM during period 𝑡 + 1, acquiring bank
deposits to repay the loan. The remaining portion of the returns is used for consumption.
Bankers also live for one period. They issue loans when young, creating deposits endogenously. Bankers pay interest on deposits
and charge interest on loans. Additionally, they must comply with a minimum reserve requirement constraint on all deposits
exchanged after one subperiod in the DM. Consequently, all liquid deposits exchanged in the DM can only be partially invested
in loans. Bankers offer two types of deposits: liquid transaction deposits and illiquid savings deposits. Early consumers, who want
to consume in the DM, hold the former, while late consumers, who want to consume in the CM when old, hold the latter. Bankers
have market power in both the deposit and loan markets.

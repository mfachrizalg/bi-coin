FOREWORD


Digitalization has been enabled consumers and businesses to transfer value
instantaneously, technology platforms to scale up rapidly in payments, and new digital
currencies to facilitate these payments. The payment system is transforming into a
capability that allows users to deliver greater value and convenience at lower cost.
Digital transformation of payments extends not only to the systems and players, but
also to the medium of exchange. On the other hand, private digital-currency-based
payment systems could magnify concerns surrounding illicit activity and consumer
risk, while potentially creating challenges for the public sector’s ability to safeguard
financial stability and use monetary policy to buffer the economy.

     The Central Bank is now focused on overcoming interruptions in the
development of payment systems, financial and technical advancements through the
use of digital currency. Several governments across the world have studied CBDCs
and have implemented CBDC pilot projects in their operations. A Central Bank
Digital Currencies (CBDC) is basically a digital banknote. Individuals may use it to pay
businesses, shops, or each other (a “retail CBDC”), while financial institutions could
use it to settle transactions in financial markets (a “wholesale CBDC”).

     Moreover, CBDC would again grant the general public direct access to public
money. Deposits and other digital currencies would be convertible into CBDC.
This would immediately restore substitutability between payment instruments and
keep their relative prices fixed. Hence, CBDC could be essential in maintaining the
uniformity of money in a digital economy. CBDC would open up a direct channel
by which monetary policy could be transmitted to the public. It may also permit
the central bank’s unit of account to remain relevant in a rapidly changing digital
economy.

     According to the issue, this webinar are expected to be a platform for sharing
knowledge about the basic concept, design, and technological innovation of Central
Bank Digital Currencies implementation as financial innovation. Moreover, participants
are expected to understand the infrastructure of CBDC to enhancing crossborder
payment and also its impact toward economy. This webinar is a collaboration from

       The five initiatives of Indonesia’s Payment System Blueprint 2025 include: (i)
developing open banking standardization, (ii) strengthening the configuration of

digital trends that have already emerged and will strengthen in the future. One of the
digitalization trends that has recently emerged is the emergence of various types of
crypto assets. Today, there are more than 14,000 types of crypto assets worldwide
along with technological advancements, such as blockchain and distributed ledger
technology (DLT), which will continue to grow over time. Nonetheless, crypto assets
still have various negative aspects that could harm consumers. For example, the
value of crypto assets are very volatile and there is no guarantee authority. Also, the
anonymous nature could be misused by irresponsible people. Against this backdrop,
many central banks are considering the issue of central bank digital currencies (CBDC)
as part of their role in providing trusted money to the public.

     CBDCs have the potential to play a role in the Central Bank’s efforts to continue
providing secure payment instruments in line with the use of digital technology in
everyday lives. However, before issuing CBDC, the central bank must proceed with
a careful, transparent, and collaborative assessment to understand and mitigate any
potential risks and impacts. This is because CBDC will gradually affect the stability
of the monetary and financial system as well as the overall economy. I would like
to highlight three key points that need to be taken into account while scrutinizing
the possibility of CBDC issuance. First, interoperability. CBDC incentives should be
conducted in collaboration among central banks and alternative authorities. We

CBP Landscape in Indonesia

First, we will look at how cross-border payments work in the context of financial
market infrastructures. Financial market infrastructure (FMI) in Indonesia facilitates
transaction processing that stem from economic activities in both the financial and
non-financial markets. The diagram in this slide shows the configuration of FMI in
Indonesia and covers the functions of trading, recording, as well as the clearing and
settlement of financial assets, including bonds and securities.

     In the area of the settlement function, there is the Bank Indonesia – Real
Time Gross Settlement (BI-RTGS) system for performing wholesale transactions
from financial institutions, including all settlement of securities transaction from
the Bank Indonesia – Scripless Securities Settlement System (BI-SSSS), which is the
infrastructure for administrating transactions and securities electronically. There is
also the National Clearing System (SKNBI). Also, below Real Time Gross Settlement
(RTGS) in the flowchart is cross-border. Bank Indonesia already has interlinkage with
the US Dollar Real Time Gross Settlement (RTGS) system (also known as US dollar
CHATS) in Hong Kong to facilitate settlements of USD to IDR and payment-versus-
payment mechanisms.

      The next slide shows cross-border payments in Indonesia, which is mostly
initiated through electronic bank transfers (front-end) and processed predominantly
by correspondent banking arrangements (back-end). The payment processing has
three types of models, including a correspondent banking model, interlinking model,
and a single platform model. There is also peer-to-peer (P2P) model, which usually
involves digital currency but has not been widely used in Indonesia. On the front-end,
cross-border payments in Indonesia are mostly initiated by electronic bank transfer
as a service provided by the banking industry. Payment cards with international
principal like Visa and Mastercard, money transfer operators (MTOs), such as Western
Union or MoneyGram are all common ways for individuals to make cross-border
payments. On the back end, the correspondent banking mechanism is dominated by
the settlement of cross-border payments in Indonesia as a single platform (by MTO
and multinational banks), and an interlinking model (i.e., APN).

 Challenges and Issues

 The common challenges associated with cross-border payments (CBP), include high
 costs, such as transaction fees, operating costs, network costs, appliance costs, FX
 costs, and liquidity costs for prefunding as well as low speeds and limited access.
 Furthermore, dealing with extended payment processing times from end-to-end might
 be a burden. This includes the time it takes to resolve disputes, reconcile, and search,
 fund and defund, daily cut-off timings, and do AML/ CFT checks. There are additional
 difficulties because of our users’ restricted access to services, payment systems, and
 other arrangements (for PSPs) as a result of technological and financial restrictions,
 legal constraints, and liquidity availability. Limited transparency also affects prices,
 speed, the processing chain, and payment status. Other issues include evolving

customer requirements, more businesses, notably FinTech, providing cross-border
payment services, and global stable coins emerging as a viable alternative to CBDC.


G20 Building Blocks to Enhance CBP

As mentioned earlier, there are four main challenges to enhancing cross-border
payments, which include high cost, low speed, limited access and transparency that
cause frictions in the payment process. To address these issues, the Committee on
Payments and Market Infrastructures (CPMI), in collaboration with other international
organizations, identified a set of building blocks to improve the present global cross-
border payment arrangements. In fact, the Committee on Payments and Market
Infrastructures (CPMI) in collaboration with other international organizations identified
a total of 19 building blocks in five new focus areas to enhance existing payment
ecosystems, short- and medium-term impact focus areas, new infrastructures and
arrangements as well as longer-term impacts. For Indonesia, we focused on building
block number 13, especially interlinking the payment system for cross-border
payments. We also focused on building block number 15 to harmonize API protocols
for data exchange. These two building blocks are among the most important and
relevant when it comes to mitigating impacts and to enhance overall cross-border
payments.


Indonesia Payment System Blueprint 2025

Question:

How is the readiness of the industry to adopt the new Real-Time Gross Settlement
(BI-RTGS) system? Do banks in Indonesia need to make new investments and
infrastructures for the new RTGS system?


Response: Dudi Dermawan Saputra

The BI-RTGS system first needs infrastructure and facilities to operate, including
the hardware, software, data communication network, dial-up facilities and other
supporting facilities. Once this happens, crucial differences will be evident, such as
faster and cheaper transactions than the prior system. We also have a helpdesk to
assist participants overcome operational difficulties as well as provide training to
banks and other participants. This includes the implementation of ISO 20022. As
of now, most foreign banks are ready to implement the next generation BI-RTGS
system along with around 50% of other domestic banks, which have adopted the
new ISO 20022 to increase transparency and traceability of financial transaction data.
Of course, banks should invest more in the new generation BI-RTGS infrastructure,
but we have more solutions which I went over in my presentation. The banks or
participants can use their own infrastructure, or they can use a settlement platform
facilitated by a switching company or another bank. If banks wish to have faster
and cheaper transactions, more investment is required, but this is at the discretion
of the banks.

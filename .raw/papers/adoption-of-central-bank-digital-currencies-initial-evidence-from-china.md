Central banks all over the world are at various stage of developing central bank digital currency
(CBDC). Few countries have launched the CBDC or widely used it in the economy. In addition to
surveying the literature, which features extremely scarce empirical studies due to data limitations, this paper provides likely the earliest and the most comprehensive empirical documentation
of the adoption of China’s CBDC (e-CNY) after the central bank launched and actively promoted it
in pilot regions using both regulatory power and economic incentives. We find that regions with
active promotions for e-CNY plausibly witness more frequent and larger e-CNY transactions, more
wallet creations, and greater merchant adoption. However, despite the strong intervention, individual users mostly stick to existing electronic payment Apps and are reluctant to switch to eCNY. Given the world eagerly learns from China’s experiment and China plans to expand the
application scope of e-CNY as a general payment tool domestically and internationally with added
smart-contract functions, we discuss primary challenges and potential paths forward for the
development of e-CNY and CBDCs in general.

1. Introduction
Central banks all over the world are actively exploring central bank digital currencies (CBDCs), but few countries have launched
CBDC as a legal means of payment. CBDC refers to the digital version of a country’s fiat currency, but in different forms and with
different roles as set out by each country (Cong et al., 2024). According to the CBDC survey results released by the Bank for International Settlements in July 2023, 93 % of the 86 surveyed central banks have engaged in some form of CBDC work and 25 % are
piloting a retail CBDC. China is a pioneer in exploring CBDC with an early-stage research starting in 2014. The People’s Bank of China
(PBOC) has released four rounds of CBDC (e-CNY) in chosen pilot cities and promoted e-CNY in various retail business scenarios and
applications. We provide evidence on the effects of China CBDC adoption in retail business, which has important implications for other
countries in considering using CBDC as an alternative means of payment - as well as for China’s continued expansion of e-CNY

The research on CBDC is still in a nascent stage. Current studies mainly focus on its theoretical design and implications that need
empirical verification. The empirical work on CBDC is very limited due to the lack of empirical data on CBDC. This paper provides the
earliest and the most comprehensive empirical documentation of the adoption of China’s CBDC, in addition to the research overview of
CBDC development and adoption around the globe. The evidence shows that individual users are sticking to existing electronic
payment Apps such as Alipay and WeChat Pay, and are reluctant to switch to e-CNY even after several rounds of promotional events in
pilot regions. The central bank of China plans to expand the application scope of its CBDC as a general payment tool in business-tobusiness, bank loan-to-business, and individuals-to-business scenarios. It also explores appealing features of the added smart-contract
functions on CBDC and experiments in various business transactions to attract more users. We discuss the challenges and potential
paths forward for China e-CNY and CBDC in general. We obtain data from the official website of the People’s Bank of China, financial
reports of commercial banks, media reports of the pilot launches in local cities and provinces, and news releases from local
governments.
The remainder of the paper is as follows. Section 2 reviews the theoretical and empirical literature on CBDC. Section 3 describes the

2. Literature and themes in CBDC studies
Digital payments have become increasingly prevalent in retail, wholesale, and business transactions around the world. For
example, digital platforms have transformed the landscape of payments in emerging market economies—examples include Alipay and
WeChat Pay in China, PayTm in India, and Pix in Brazil. Decentralized cryptocurrencies inspired by Bitcoin have created another
option for conducting transactions without using central bank money or trusted intermediaries such as commercial banks and credit
card providers. Cong et al. (2024) provide an overview of various forms of digital payments—electronic payment systems, decentralized cryptocurrencies, and central bank digital currencies. Private-sector innovations in digital payments have spurred upgrades of
government-issued currencies, leading to the development of Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs). A large number of central
banks have already issued CBDCs, initiated pilots, or are at advanced stages of technical preparation for conducting trials. The People’s
Bank of China (PBOC) is a pioneer in exploring CBDC with an early-stage research starting in 2014, and subsequently, has released four
rounds of CBDC (referred to as e-CNY) in chosen pilot cities. Other major central banks, including those of Brazil, India, and Japan, had
already commenced CBDC trials in 2023. The recent development of CBDCs globally has created interest and demand for research on
this topic. However, studies on CBDCs are still at an early stage. In the following, we first summarize current theoretical and empirical
studies on the design and implications of CBDCs.
2.1. Definitions of CBDCs
CBDC is a digital version of a country’s fiat currency. There’s no unified CBDC definition, with individual country designing its own
conceptual and technological aspects. Discussions focus on its feasibility, benefits over cash, interest-bearing, international payments
and settlements, and design considerations such as validation, transaction security, and anonymity (Allen et al., 2020; Cong et al.,
2024). For example, Kumhof and Noone (2018) define CBDC as electronic central bank money with broader accessibility than reserves,
designed for retail transactions, potentially interest-bearing, and operationally distinct from other central bank monies. As another
example, Bordo and Levin (2017) present three CBDC designs as secure value stores, each impacting the central bank’s monetary
policies differently.
International payments and settlements are particularly challenging. Some central banks are actively working on improving the
infrastructure for cross-border payments through the use of CBDCs. However, the participating central banks have to develop technologies compatible with multiple currencies. The challenges include the need for stable connectivity among all participants in the
business network, the need to accommodate differences in regulatory requirements between countries and security protocols used by
various banks, and the complications arising from differences in every country’s macro policy.
The use of physical cash has diminished significantly as digital payment technologies rise. CBDCs can limit illicit activities, offer
superior transaction efficiency over cash, improve transaction monitoring, offer variable interest rates, and enforce tax evasion
penalties (Mishra and Prasad, 2024).Government policies can affect CBDC and cash coexistence. Despite CBDC’s advantages over cash,
it’s not feasible to eradicate cash due to privacy concerns and potential exclusion of underprivileged populations from the digital
financial realm. The BIS emphasizes that CBDCs should complement, not replace, existing money forms (BIS, 2020).
2.2. Optimal designs of CBDCs
Current research on CBDC mainly focuses on its theoretical design and implications. The empirical literature on CBDC is scarce at
this point due to the lack of data on CBDC. The theoretical studies on the optimal design of CBDC mainly explore its attributes for
monetary and financial system development, such as CBDC’s circulation amount (Burlon et al., 2022), the competition between cash,
CBDC, and bank deposits, interest bearing properties (Agur et al., 2022; Li, 2023; Wilkins, 2022), budgeting usefulness, anonymity and
bundling of bank services (Auer et al., 2022; Baronchelli et al., 2022; Li, 2023; Skinner, 2023).

deposit-like CBDC, Chiu and Davoodalhosseini (2023) theoretically and quantitatively assess the effects of a CBDC on consumption,

welfare.
From regulators’ point of view, Liu et al. (2024) argue that central banks will need to develop sustainable economic models in order
for an intermediated central bank digital currency to be successful. They present a framework for analyzing different economic models
of CBDC ecosystems and analyze the trade-offs of three main CBDC ecosystem models, considering different levels of central bank
involvement in activities of the ecosystem and the usage of different policy levers. They also analyze the benefits and costs of a central
bank provision of network infrastructure and a central bank digital wallet versus delegating the service to a regulated entity.
2.3. Implications of CBDCs for the monetary policy and banking systems
The academic literature has only recently begun to grapple with the implications of CBDC for monetary policy and existing banking
systems, including state benefits (Barrdear and Kumhof, 2016), credit and financial stability (Brunnermeier and Niepelt, 2019;
Andolfatto, 2021; Fernández-Villaverde et al., 2021; Keister and Sanches, 2023), trade efficiency (Keister and Sanches, 2023), and
consumption and welfare (Chiu and Davoodalhosseini, 2023). The conclusions, as indicated by the limited and selective survey below,
depend to a great extent on the model structure and the manner in which the CBDC is introduced into the economy.
In a model calibrated to U.S. data, Barrdear and Kumhof (2016) find that infusing CBDC on the order of 30 % of GDP through
central bank purchases of government bonds could result in substantial steady state output gains of nearly 3 % due to reductions in real
interest rates, distortionary taxes, and monetary transaction costs. Bordo and Levin (2017) consider how digital cash could bolster the
effectiveness of monetary policy. They conclude that digital cash could significantly enhance the stability of the financial system. Grym
et al. (2017) believe that for the new payment instrument to be a substitute for cash, it should share many of its properties such as
anonymity, immediate finality, and transaction clearing without third parties.
Most studies find positive impact on the existing banking systems of the introduction of CBDC. Brunnermeier and Niepelt (2019)
believe that CBDC coupled with central bank pass-through funding need not imply a credit crunch nor undermine financial stability.
Andolfatto (2021) studies the implications of CBDC in an overlapping generations model with a monopolistic banking sector. They find
that the CBDC has no detrimental effect on bank lending activity and lending rates. Although the introduction of an interest-bearing
CBDC reduces the demand for cash, it does not disintermediate banks. Fernández-Villaverde et al. (2021) show how, in an economy
with CBDC, the central bank becomes a deposit monopolist even in normal times. Chiu et al. (2023) further develop a micro-founded
general equilibrium model of payments to study the impact of CBDC on intermediation of private banks. They find that if banks have
market power in the deposit market, a CBDC can enhance competition, raise the deposit rate, expand intermediation, and increase
output.
On the contrary, Keister and Sanches (2023) present some negative evidence that while a digital currency tends to improve efficiency in exchange, it may also crowd out bank deposits, raise banks’ funding costs and decrease investments. In sum, Prasad (2021)
provides a broad overview of how developments in financial technology could affect financial markets, monetary policy implementation and transmission, and financial stability. As with the implementation of monetary policy, the introduction of a CBDC, by
itself, should not in principle make much of a difference to monetary policy transmission, as it would mean simply that central bank
money is in digital rather than physical form. In practice, however, changes in financial technologies and the structures of financial
markets could have big effects on transmission. New financial technologies and the proliferation of various forms of digital currencies
could complicate the implementation and transmission of monetary policy in ways that are not year clear and could also heighten the
degree of uncertainty with which the central banks have to cope.
2.4. Launching strategy of CBDCs
Some studies document the launching strategy of CBDC from a global perspective and its user adoption. As discussed in Cong and
Mayer (2022), countries around the world have been strategically navigating the digital transformation of their monetary systems
amidst dynamic competition among national fiat currencies, cryptocurrencies, and CBDCs. Cong and Mayer (2022) model the dynamic
competition among national fiat currencies, cryptocurrencies and CBDCs. They find that countries with strong but non-dominant
currencies benefit the most from launching CBDC early on. Claessens et al. (2024) highlight the potential benefits and risks associated with CBDCs, including challenges and opportunities associated with proposed CBDC regulation in the United States and the
European Union. Minesso et al. (2022) derive a two-country DSGE model with CBDC to analyze the open-economy implications of
CBDC for the transmission of shocks, optimal monetary policy and welfare. They find that the presence of a CBDC amplifies the international spillovers of shocks and increases international linkages. From user adoption perspectives, Huynh et al. (2020) develop and

2.5. Empirical studies of CBDCs
Most prior studies draw conclusions based on theoretical models with few data and empirical evidence, as very few central banks
have put CBDC into practical use. Three recent papers use empirical data to examine the impact of CBDC. Using US quarterly banklevel data, Whited et al. (2023) estimate a dynamic banking model to quantify the impact of CBDC on the banking system, particularly
bank lending and bank deposits. They show that a one-dollar introduction of CBDC replaces bank deposits by around 80 cents on the
margin. Bank lending falls by one-fourth of the drop in deposits because banks partially replace lost deposits with wholesale funding.
Using Canadian household and consumer data, Li et al. (2024) explore to what extent a central bank digital currency competes with
bank deposits. A non-interest-bearing CBDC that does not provide complementary financial products can substantially crowd out bank
deposits only if it provides an extensive service network. Using U.S. and Eurozone listed firms data, Berg et al. (2024) analyze the effect
of announcements on the digital euro on payment firms. They find that stock prices of U.S. payment firms decrease, while stock prices
of European payment firms increase in response to positive announcements on the digital euro.
In sum, with limited empirical studies on the impact of CBDCs, we exploit the unique setting to report empirical findings regarding
the initial adoption of CBDCs in China while highlighting its limitations and future directions. Our study therefore provides useful
initial benchmarks for future research and CBDC development in China and other countries.
3. The design and salient features of e-CNY
E-CNY is a digital form of China’s fiat currency, a legal tender backed by the People’s Bank of China (PBOC). The original plan for eCNY is to substitute physical cash in retail business and be counted as part of M0. Once the PBOC gains more experience with e-CNY,
the scope and goals of its use may evolve. E-CNY follows a two-tier system with distinct layers of functionality. In the first layer, the
PBOC issues and redeems e-CNY through ten designated commercial banks with 100 % reserve. In the second layer, commercial banks
serve as the main intermediaries and distribute e-CNY to the general public by exchanging and circulating e-CNY. Other authorized
entities with a large number of users, such as existing mobile payment platforms (Alipay and WeChat Pay) and telecommunication
companies are impulsed to join the network to promote the e-CNY as one payment means in their business ecosystem. PBOC does not
charge exchange and circulation fees to tier-two operating institutions, and the institutions are not allowed to charge service fees to
individual users.
The two-tier operation system helps alleviate competition between the central bank and commercial banks, prevents disintermediation of commercial banks, and avoid disrupting the existing banking system. For example, e-CNY holders currently do not
receive any interest from the central bank or commercial banks, which prevents crowding out of bank deposits. Fig. 1 depicts the twotier operating system of e-CNY.
According to the white paper published by PBOC in July 2021, the design of e-CNY is to maintain automatic settlement and anonymity of physical cash, while achieving the goal of low cost and high efficiency of electronic payment.1 In order to function like cash
with automatic settlement, e-CNY is loosely coupled with bank accounts, which means transactions can happen between two e-CNY
wallets instead of two bank accounts. E-CNY users need to download a digital wallet to store e-CNY, similar to a physical wallet to store
cash. The wallets can have different levels of KYC requirements, allowing both individual and enterprise wallets, which can be soft
wallets or hard wallets.
China e-CNY follows the principle of “small amount anonymity and large amount traceability.” For privacy protection, individuals
and their transactions in small amount can be anonymous from third party intermediaries, different from transactions using traditional
banking system. However, e-CNY is a centralized governmance system where transactions are not anomymous from the PBOC or
authorized entities sharing information with the PBOC. PBOC has access to all transaction data and can trace identities and transactions in abnormal amount for regulatory purposes. In addition, e-CNY installs smart contract features and can automatically execute
transactions by programming agreed conditions and rules into the code.
Currently, the PBOC is actively promoting the ecosystem of e-CNY smart contract applications built on a potentially open source
platform. Under the two-tier operation system, the PBOC is responsible for building the foundational smart contract platform to
connect with designated banks. It issues technical standards and authorizes smart-contract modules that are developed by itself and the
participating entities. The designated banks ensure a safe smart contract execution environment that can connect with the PBOC’s
system, and tailor smart contracts to various needs of business enterprises. A major smart-contract module currently in use is “Secured
Pay,” an application installed in the e-CNY wallet for storing the prepaid deposit from customers. Consumers need to sign off each time
when they spend e-CNY from the wallet before the prepaid funds can be transferred through banks to the merchants, which helps

4. Timeline and e-CNY adoption
China pioneered CBDC exploration. The PBOC established a task force in 2014 to research on the design of CBDC covering topics
such as the technical design, issuance, and testing of CBDC. The interest of PBOC in digital currency might derive from the prevalence
of electronic payment system built by private companies, emergence of digital currencies such as cryptocurrencies, and incentives to
regulate the development of digital finance. PBOC established its Digital Currency Institute in 2016 to develop e-CNY prototypes. In
2017, PBOC launched the Digital Currency/Electronic Payment (DC/EP) project and completed the top-level design, standard,
debugging and testing.
In April 2020, PBOC launched the first pilot testing in Winter Olympics and in four cities: Shenzhen, Suzhou, Xiong’an, Chengdu. In
October 2020, Shanghai, Hainan, Changsha, Xi’an, Qingdao, and Dalian are another round of cities to launch CBDC. By August 2024,
the PBOC has launched CBDC for the fourth time coving a total of 17 provinces. Table 1 contains all the cities and provinces.
To promote the adoption of e-CNY in pilot launches, local governments distributed e-CNY through digital Red Envelopes. Individuals can use e-CNY coupons to purchase goods in retail stores, recharge transportation card, and others, but cannot withdraw cash
from the coupon. The coupons are distributed to participating individuals by lotteries. Appendix A lists e-CNY red envelope coupons
that each city distributed at promotional events.
Many other events were also held in launching cities to promote the usage of e-CNY. For example, Suzhou subway Line 5 officially
started operation on June 29, 2021, where all ticket vending machines support e-CNY as a means of payment. On January 7, 2022, the
Bank of Communications, in collaboration with Shanghai Employment Center distributed unemployment subsidies through e-CNY. In
March 2022, e-CNY is included in new energy vehicle promotions in Hainan province to offset down payment.
After the promotional events for e-CNY, individuals and enterprises have started to accept it as a means of payment in those cities
and provinces. We summarize the information on e-CNY usage in each region in Table 2.
According to the summary statistics disclosed by PBOC, the total amount of e-CNY transactions in deployment regions reached
6600 billion yuan as of May 31, 2024. The cumulative number of transactions reached 950 million as of June 30, 2023. Fig. 3 depicts

(i) The pilot e-CNY focuses on retail and consumer discretionary products and individual users. China has largely transformed to a
cashless economy thanks to the mobile payment technologies from the private sector. Designing e-CNY as substitutes for
physical cash is neither novel nor competitive relative to Alipay and Wechat Pay.
(ii) The initial adoption of e-CNY requires significant offline promotional efforts in the presence of dominant third-party payment
platforms, which demands significant regulatory efforts and operating budget from PBOC, government of pilot regions as well as
participating enterprises. It is challenging for PBOC to provide continuous incentives to relevant parties. Participating banks
dealing with individuals cannot generate profits from e-CNY exchanges like issuing bank cards, nor can they use customer eCNY as deposits to issue interest-bearing loans. Instead, banks bear extra operating costs of transforming current technology to
be compatible with e-CNY wallets creation and related tech infrastructures.
(iii) Incentives from individual customers to use e-CNY are weak in the presence of third-party electronic payment platforms like
WeChat or Alipay for purchases in small amount and bank cards for payment in large amount. The benefits of switching
payment means for individuals are small or zero, even though the pilot regions distribute e-CNY red envelops as incentives to
create e-CNY wallets. Compared to cash, e-CNY loses some anonymity, which is less attractive than physical cash.
(iv) The ecosystem of e-CNY is less appealing to individual consumers compared to those of prevalent electronic payment platforms
like WeChat or Alipay. Retail users are used to the amenieties from existing networks of private online payment platforms. These
platforms offer financial products with interest income, a variety of personalized products to purchase online, chatting tools,
video sharing, and many others with great appeal to existing users. Online payment companies also take advantage of individual
consumer data accumulated on the platform to generate additional market opportunities and profits, continuously building up
the ecosystem.

institutions. Since 2019, online payment platforms such as WeChat and Alipay have to put down 100 % reserves with the central
bank for every Yuan that a customer deposits into their wallets. There is little regulatory risk associated with private online
payment platforms, further weakening e-CNY’s advantages as a state-endorced product in the online payment market.
The PBOC is reviewing the impact of e-CNY initial deployment. With more experience, the scope and goals of its use will expand
from replacing cash in retail to a general payment tool domestically and internationally with added smart-contract functions for a
variety of application scenarios such as cross-border payment, business-to-business transactions, financial products purchases, or bank
loan issuance. The potential changes or transformations need to be accompanied by broader institutional, regulatory, and legal
changes. For example, the deployment of smart contract functions in prepaid cards adds another layer of regulations that need to be
compatible with current regulations on business’ use of customer deposits.4 According to PBOC semi-annual meeting held on August
1st, 2024, China plans to smoothly and gradually advance the research and deployment of e-CNY nationally and internationally, which
constitutes a major endeavor in developing domestic digital finance industry.
Often shunned by the PBOC, a public discourse of e-CNY as a tool for advancing the Chinese Yuan on a global stage is equally
relevant. The above challenges would manifest themselves again when e-CNY gains more momentum in cross-border and regional
payment applications. Since the 2008 financial crisis, many nations have sought after alternative trade currencies. The yuan’s international standing has steadily improved (35th most utilized currency worldwide in 2010 to the 5th in 2023). E-CNY therefore has a
national strategic significance. First, the Belt and Road Initiative offers an opportunity for internationalizing yuan and requires a digital
medium in place of other systems such as the SWIFT. Second, China’s growing influence in regions like the Middle East and North
Africa could stimulate the international adoption of e-CNY, which could also accelerate interoperability for other CBDCs. Project
mBridge launched in June 2023 is a good example of advancing the international adoption of e-CNY. Expanding usage categories,
building application ecosystems, and expanding functionalities through smart contracting, etc., would similarly play an important role
when e-CNY is used to promote more global adoption of Yuan.
6. Conclusion
CBDCs are rarely deployed and data for empirical studies is extremely limited. The experiences of China e-CNY deployment have
raised interesting research questions on central bank digital currency that deserve further investigation. How should governmentbacked digital currency be positioned in the market with existing online payment developed by private third-party institutions,
physical cash, and bank cards? CBDC is a public good and it is important to structure what insitutions constitute this important
financial infrastructure, what role each participating entity should play, and what benefits and costs each entity should bear. Should
CBDC be designed to have more beneficial advantages such as no transaction fees charged on merchants accepting CBDC, or accruing
deposits in CBDC with interest income? Along with the development of CBDC all over the world, cross-border transactions could
accelerate interoperability among CBDCs. The RCEP region, the world’s largest free-trade agreement area, could emerge as a market
dominated by these digital currencies. With the growing influence of digital currencies on cross-border transactions, CBDC could
further stimulate the internationalization of alternative trade currencies and the trend of de-dollarization worldwide. More rigorous

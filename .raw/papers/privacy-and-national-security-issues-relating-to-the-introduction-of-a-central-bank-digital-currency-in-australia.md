Abstract
Purpose – The purpose of this paper is to analyse privacy and national surveillance laws in Australia,
including but not limited to the federal Privacy Act 1988 and the federal Anti-Money Laundering and CounterTerrorism Financing Act 2006 (AML/CTF), to determine whether a tension exists between these two statutes
within the context of the deployment of central bank digital currency (CBDC) in Australia.
Design/methodology/approach – The paper adopts doctrinal and normative approaches for analysis of the
relevant legislations. Furthermore, the paper adopts a functionalist theoretical perspective to interrogate the
interrelationship between regulation and society.
Findings – The paper suggests that the relevant legislations contain undefined terms, such as “reasonable
grounds”, which may give the appearance of a balanced approach to the interactions between privacy and national
surveillance laws within the context of deployment of CBDCs. The paper argues that lack of definition of these
terms, however, renders the terms ineffective, leading to a potential regulatory overreach. The paper recommends
administrators need internal policies and procedures that would give clear guidance on the possible meanings of
those legislative terms to achieve the balance desired between privacy and national security requirements in
Australia when deploying CBDCs. It is suggested that internal policies are to base suspicion on factual basis for
decision-makers to have reasonable grounds for contravening privacy legislations. This may be achieved through
requiring decision-makers to justify their decisions and consider alternative options before infringing on privacy,
thus enhancing accountability. Furthermore, it is suggested that the transparency and traceability provided by
distributed ledger technologies will compel decision-makers to assess the benefits of privacy violations against
their costs, promoting a balanced approach to surveillance and personal data disclosure.
Originality – The originality of the paper lies in its seminal analysis of the interaction of privacy an antimoney laundering laws in the context of the introduction of a central bank digital currency.

Introduction
As nations around the world contemplate the issuance and use of a central bank digital
currency (CBDC) using distributed ledger technologies (DLTs), the Australian law reform
discourse can provide useful guidance to law and policy makers. While Australia has not
as yet determined to introduce such a CBDC, (Australian Treasury and RBA, 2024), its
detailed and comprehensive analysis of risk and reward is instructive.

The Australian examinations of CBDCs has identified a variety of significant and diverse
legal concerns. Significant among such concerns is the relationship between privacy and
national security. It is significant to examine these concerns because privacy is a human right
in democratic States such as Australia, and national security is guaranteed by democratic
States for their citizens. The primary objective of this article is to analyse the impact of
surveillance powers and anti-money laundering and counterterrorism financing (AML/CTF)
laws on privacy in the context of CBDCs in Australia. To address this central question, it is
of importance to stress the significance of how technical design features in DLTs such as
transaction traceability and transparency may aid in combating criminal activities and at the
same time the relevant national security and privacy laws, lacking clear definitions of terms
like “reasonable grounds” may allow intelligence agencies to overreach, unintentionally
eroding privacy rights. The article therefore, advocates for decision-makers to develop
internal policies promoting accountability and transparency in state actions – as a solution.
This approach would help protect citizens’ privacy while still supporting national security
goals. It is argued in this paper that due to the use of DLT platforms which are widely
known for their transparency and transaction traceability capabilities (Bashir, 2020), the
human right of privacy may be threatened when it comes to CBDCs context.

Since DLTs may be the proposed option for the issuance and usage of CBDCs, it is now
important to consider the state of CBDCs on these platforms. Hangen identifies four main
areas for consideration: (1) the network model, (2) how the price of the digital currency is
determined, (3) the extent to which user information is stored on the blockchain, and (4) how
changes to the blockchain are managed (Hangen, 2022). First, the architectural network
model in a CBDC is reported to be typically controlled by the central bank because it is
private and permissioned network (Laboure et al, 2021). This implies that the entire CBDC
platform may be functionally under the control of the central manager, even if the
development were outsourced to a third party, and that only authorised entities can access
and validate transactions (Hangen, 2022). The central manager would necessarily maintain
an application programming interface allowing CBDCs to be issued to commercial banks or
directly to users (Hangen, 2022). Second, the price of the digital currency is to be determined
in the same way as fiat currency: through carefully devised monetary policy from the central
bank (Laboure et al, 2021). In other words, a CBDC would be issued as a “liability” of
the Reserve Bank of Australia that is widely available to the general public (RBA
website on CBDCs). A CBDC could be directly issued to other banks or private parties
without mechanisms such as deposit insurance or backing by an underlying asset pool
(Hangen, 2022). Third, use of a CBDC would necessarily require users to reveal personal
information, for example, the same information traditionally used to open a bank account:
name, contact details, verification of identification (Hangen, 2022). A CBDC would also
generate data about users’ financial transactions and history, not unlike the financial data
that is generated today (Hangen, 2022). However, this data would include the entire web of
transactional data between users: showing how each CBDC came to be in each individual’s
wallet (Hangen, 2022). Such transaction history would theoretically be centralised with the
DLT manager, even if minimally pseudonymised by using a public-private key encryption
model. This in turn poses threats to privacy if scanning applications are used.

Transaction monitoring and suspicious matter reports. Reporting (or regulated) entities
are required to implement effective risk-based AML/CTF transaction monitoring systems to
identify high-risk transactions, obfuscation techniques or attempts to complicate customer

the risk-based approach, regulated entities must assess the level of risk that their customers
will, or are able to, engage in illicit behaviours and assess their customers’ transactions
against this framework. This requires regulated entities to have a complete understanding of
their customers’ business models, the identity and jurisdiction of whom they transact with on
a regular basis and their typical transaction size and frequency (FATF, 2025).

The potential application of surveillance powers in Australia in the context of CBDCs
To understand surveillance offences in Australia, it is further useful to examine the context in
which it was devised and implemented. Governments of developed economies have long
held the belief that organised crime poses a worldwide peril, affecting not just their
countries’ progress and stability but also society (Harvey, 2019a). This perception stems
from the considerable likelihood of corruption spreading due to the substantial illicit wealth
generated by organised crime activities (Calederoni et al., 2020). In contrast to ordinary
crime, the danger of organised crime arises from its covert and gradual erosion of institutions
and society (Calederoni et al., 2020). Consequently, the repercussions of organised crime
were characterised as causing global detriment, endangering democracy and national
security rather than just individual harm. The Australian government at the time stated:[t]he
security of Australia is the […] highest priority, and maintaining that security and the safety
and security of Australian citizens requires decisive action to target serious and organised
crime (McClelland, 2009). Such characterisation, coupled with the transparency feature of
DLTs used in the deployment of CBDCs, threatens privacy legal rights because it may trigger
an unwarranted reaction from decision-makers and not a considered response to crime.
This concern about surveillance at the cost of privacy has been challenged, with some
questioning the portrayal of organised crime as a substantial threat and questioning the
existence of a dominant ‘global international crime’ control. Despite the surge in illegal

AML/CTF criminal laws in Australia are found in two main legislations: Proceeds of Crime
Act, 2002 (Cth) (Proceeds of Crime Act), Criminal Code Act, 1995 (Cth) (Criminal Code Act).
AML/CTF regulatory regime is found in AML/CTF Act 2006 (Cth) (AML/CTF Act). Both
regulatory and criminal regimes form AML/CTF requirements in Australia. The landscape of
AML/CTF regulatory regime is controversial because certain consumers’/clients’ behaviours
are to be reported based on mere suspicion as long as person reporting has reasonable grounds
(Harvey, 2019b), which is an unorthodox practice compared with traditional regulatory
processes. This shift is accompanied by an erosion of fundamental rights and liberties, such as
privacy (Harvey, 2019b). In addition, the international involvement in law enforcement has
grown, while oversight of official agencies through judicial review has lessened (Goldbarsht
and Harris, 2022). Simultaneously, laws are being enacted without thorough parliamentary
scrutiny (Goldbarsht and Harris, 2022). In essence, AML/CTF legislation has profoundly
altered regulation and institutional operations (FATF, 2007). It is within this context that this
article makes a call for internal policies where decision-makers provide reasons and reasoning
processes in transparent way.

a moment comes for assessment of legislation. The passage of AML/CTF legislation did not
provoke substantial discussion in the media nor in Parliament, where it was passed with
bipartisan support (Goldbarsht and Harris, 2017). The Australian AML/CTF legislation
serves as a model of the efforts employed to combat perceived threats on both national and

The potential application of privacy laws to CBDCs
Privacy challenges presented by evolving CBDC technologies. The specific context
concerning CBDCs revolves around payment practices, particularly the act of making
payments using CBDCs. Involved in this process are four key actors: the payer, the payee,
entities responsible for facilitating the payment (such as central banks, commercial banks,
money service providers, and other authorised deposit-taking institutions), and law
enforcement agencies. Information associated with these transactions includes details
such as the names, phone numbers, addresses and account balances of both payers and
payees, as well as specifics on where and when the payment occurred, and which entities
were involved in processing it (Jiang, 2023). Hence, the identification of payees’ details,
such as, their names, dates of birth, and addresses are at risk of being disclosed not on
reasonable grounds, which would go against relevant national security legal provisions
discussed above.
Transmission principles are fundamental norms governing the flow of information in this
payment context, varying based on the actors and information attributes involved. A single
payment transaction may encompass multiple transmission principles across different
entities. For example, when a payer uses CBDCs to make a payment to a payee, the
transmission principle involves the exchange of data related to the monetary amount, the
payer’s account or wallet address, the payee’s account or wallet address, and other relevant
details with the payment processors. However, these processors might transmit the data to
additional parties under different principles, such as forwarding the information to law
enforcement under a national security principle (Jiang, 2023).
Privacy is a critical aspect of CBDC payments, encompassing the concealment of identity
and transaction data from various parties, such as the payee, participating entities and the
general public. Protecting privacy in CBDC payments involves preventing unauthorised
access to CBDC data, unauthorised use of identity and transaction information, reputation
damage from disclosure, breaches of confidentiality, surveillance and similar disruptions
(Davis et al., 2014).
Reputation damage from disclosure and confidentiality breaches are not subject to
compensation in the context of national security. Even when proven innocent, the alleged
suspects suffer the consequences, and these are serious damages that may continue to be noncompensable. Therefore, there needs to be an internal policies for decision-makers to follow
in Australia to combat such damages (Davis et al., 2014).

requires a fair and reasonable assessment. One could argue that identity and transaction
information in CBDC payments should be kept private from government entities, the public
and unrelated third parties. Retail payments, including CBDCs, inherently leave behind a
data trail that can potentially reveal detailed personal information such as travel patterns and
financial circumstances (Goodell et al., 2021). Similarly, CBDCs, as a new payment method,
share these characteristics. Disclosure of account, identity, and transaction data to

The potential application of the Australian privacy principles to CBDCs
Australian Privacy Principles (APPs), detailed in Schedule 1 of the Privacy Act
(Commonwealth Privacy Act, 1998), establish the fundamental legal principles guiding the
responsibilities of entities governed by the APPs in safeguarding individuals’ personal
information [USA Online Privacy Law 2017 (USA Congress)]. This approach is described
as “principle-based”, emphasising core principles over rigid directives. The objective is to
create broad principles of responsible governance that can remain relevant in the rapidly
evolving landscape of technology (Kunc, 2020 and Cole, 2014). As highlighted in the
Explanatory Memorandum, this approach is designed to offer adaptable regulation
(Commonwealth Privacy Act, 1988).
Instead of linking privacy obligations to specific technologies or activities, the APPs delineate
the “situations” in which these obligations are applicable, with a focus on the circumstances
surrounding the generation, collection, utilisation and sharing of information (Gilmour et al,
2024, Kunc, 2020 and Commonwealth Privacy Act, 1988 (Cth)). APP entities are obligated to
manage personal information transparently and employ reasonable measures to ensure
compliance with the APPs (USA Online Privacy Law 2017). Likewise, exceptions to privacy
obligations are also associated with designated “situations” (Selvadurai, 2013). These exceptions
are grounded in two key concepts: a “permitted general situation” and a “permitted health
situation”. These situations create exemptions from the general obligations imposed on APP
entities (Selvadurai, 2013). Section 16A outlines scenarios where an APP entity can gather,
employ, or disclose personal information or a government-related identifier without obtaining the
individual’s consent (Selvadurai, 2013). This is permissible when obtaining consent is
unreasonable or unfeasible, and the entity reasonably believes that such action is necessary to
mitigate or avert a serious threat to, for example, national security (Selvadurai, 2013).
Schedule 1 of the Privacy Act consists of 13 APPs, and it is useful to consider principles
related to CBDCs and privacy in some detail, given that AML/CTF requirements affect
Principles 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 12 and 13 of the APP. APP 2 sets out requirements regarding
anonymity and pseudonymity. Under clause 2.2, when dealing with APP entities, individuals
must have the option not to reveal their identity or use a pseudonym. However, this broad
requirement is subject to exceptions listed in clause 2.2. These exceptions include situations
where (a) an Australian law or a court/tribunal order mandates the identification of
individuals by the APP entity or (b) it is impractical for the APP entity to deal with
individuals who have not identified themselves or used a pseudonym (Gligorijevic, 2021 and
Witzleb, 2020). Building upon APPs 1 and 2, APPs 3 and 4 focus on ensuring privacy during
the collection of personal information. If an APP entity is an agency, it can only collect
personal information (except sensitive information) if it is reasonably necessary for its
functions or activities (cl 3.2, which also applies to organisations, requiring them to collect
personal information (except sensitive information) only if it is reasonably necessary for their

The manner of collecting personal information is governed by clauses 3.5 and 3.6. Clause
3.5 requires APP entities to collect personal information using lawful and fair means.
Clause 3.6 further specifies that APP entities must collect personal information directly
from the individual, except when it is unreasonable or impractical to do so. Exceptions
listed in clause 3.6 allow entities to collect information from someone other than the
individual under certain conditions (Lindsay, 2005 and Richardson, 2020). Notably, while
the Act may require regulated entities to collect personal information that is reasonably
necessary for their functions or activities, “reasonably necessary” is not defined and may
cause a contravention of APP 3.
Unsolicited personal information, which is personal information received without
solicitation, is separately addressed in APP 4. In such cases, the APP entity must determine
whether it may lawfully collect the information under APP 3, where APP3 relates to solicited
information. If the entity determines it could have lawfully collected the unsolicited personal
information, it must destroy or de-identify it within a reasonable time (Uppal, 2023 and
Clarke and Viva, 2021). However, exceptions are provided in clause 4.3 if it is not lawful and
reasonable to do so. If the entity determines it could not have lawfully collected the
information, it must comply with APP 5 to APP 13 for that information as if it had collected
it lawfully under APP 3 (Carne, 2011).
Obligations related to notifying individuals about the collection of personal information are
outlined in APP 5. An APP entity must take reasonable steps to notify the individual of its
identity, contact details, purposes for collecting the information, and potential consequences of
not providing the information as soon as practicable after collecting the information (Williams,
2011). Additionally, the individual must be informed about any other parties to which the entity
usually discloses personal information. If the information was collected from a third party and
the individual may not be aware of it, the entity must notify the individual about the collection
and its circumstances. If the entity is likely to disclose the information to overseas parties, it
must inform the individual about the countries where these overseas parties are located. The
scope of APP 5 was examined in the case of Tenants’ Union of Queensland Inc, Tenants’ Union
of NSW Co-op Ltd v. TICA (Tenants Union of Queensland and Tenants’ Union of NSW, n.d.)
Default Tenancy Control Pty Ltd, where the Privacy Commissioner emphasised the importance
of appropriately referencing required information in forms or locations.

disclosure of personal information. Clause 6.1 specifies that if an APP entity has personal
information collected for one purpose (the primary purpose), it cannot use or disclose the
information for another purpose (the secondary purpose) unless certain conditions are met.
Exceptions include individual consent and situations where the individual would reasonably
expect the entity to use or disclose the information for the secondary purpose related to the
primary purpose. For sensitive information, the secondary purpose must be “directly” related,
while for non-sensitive information, a “related” purpose suffices. Additional exceptions

individuals’ access to personal information held by an APP entity are articulated in APP 12.
Generally, upon an individual’s request, an APP entity must provide that individual with
access to their personal information, with exceptions outlined in clauses 12.2 and 12.3.
In recognition of the dynamic and changing nature of personal data, APP 13 places
obligations on APP entities regarding correcting and updating personal information to
ensure its accuracy and reliability. Therefore, the AML/CTF Act may prevent customers from
accessing or correcting their personal information held by reporting entities or AUSTRAC,
which may breach APP 12 or APP 13. For instance, reporting entities or AUSTRAC may
refuse to give access or make corrections to personal information if doing so would prejudice
an investigation or prosecution of money laundering or terrorism financing offences.
Within the CBDCs context, a clearly developed internal policies, when used by decisionmakers as a tool or a method, would assist in clarifying legislative terms such as “objective”,
“reasonable” and “reasonably necessary”. This, in turn, would ensure that there is no zealous
breach of fundamental rights such as privacy or cautious oversight of illicit criminal
activities. This use of transparent internal policies may be achieved through applying the
criteria of consideration of alternatives to a breach of privacy or rationally proving that the
benefit of breaching privacy in any questionable circumstances outweighs the cost. This is
mainly because one of the central features advocated for in this article is the duty to give
reasons for decisions. Hence, since transactional traceability and transparency are features of
DLTs (where DLTs are the platform suggested for the deployment of CBCDs), decisionmakers know they are required to give reasons for their decisions and are required to
consider alternative means to infringe upon a fundamental human right, such as privacy, or
substantiate that the benefit of a breach outweighs costs, would exercise reasonable and
objective steps to surveillance and disclosure of personal information.
